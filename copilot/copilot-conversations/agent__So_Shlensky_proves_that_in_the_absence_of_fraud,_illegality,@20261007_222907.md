**ai**: Let me read both files to answer this precisely.Your framing is right, but the answer requires a careful distinction.

**None of the three Shlensky triggers were found in Van Gorkom.** The court was explicit about this: "fulfillment of the fiduciary function requires more than the mere absence of bad faith or fraud." The directors had no self-dealing, committed no fraud, and broke no law. Van Gorkom himself owned shares and stood to gain from a sale, but the court did not treat that as a conflict — there was no transaction on both sides.

**So why did the court intervene?** Because Shlensky and Van Gorkom operate in different doctrinal lanes:

- **Shlensky** is about **substance** — *what* the board decided. The BJR protects substance near-absolutely. A court will not second-guess whether refusing lights was smart. The three triggers (fraud, illegality, conflict) are the only exits from that protection on the substance side.

- **Van Gorkom** is about **process** — *how* the board decided. The BJR only attaches to an **informed** decision in the first place. If the decision was not informed, the presumption never arises and the court never has to invoke one of the three Shlensky triggers to intervene.

The duty of care breach is the mechanism by which a decision is found *uninformed*, and therefore the mechanism by which the BJR presumption fails before it can protect anything. This is the precise distinction the Van Gorkom brief captures: "The business judgment rule protects substance, not process."

**Practical upshot:** in Shlensky, the plaintiff attacked substance (lights are profitable, refusal was dumb), and the court said that question is not ours to answer. In Van Gorkom, the plaintiff attacked process (the board never knew what it was selling or whether $55 was fair), and the court said the presumption of an informed judgment was rebutted — so there was nothing left for the BJR to protect.

The three triggers are a ceiling on substantive review, not a floor for process review. Gross negligence in the deliberative process is its own path to liability, entirely separate from fraud, illegality, or conflict.

**ai**: Yes, in effect. The class-layer posture in [[Shlensky v. Wrigley (1968)|Shlensky v. Wrigley]] describes the suit as a derivative action "for negligence and mismanagement." The industry-practice argument was a negligence theory. Nineteen of twenty clubs played night games, the Cubs were losing money, and the plaintiff said refusing lights was therefore negligent.

The court rejected it in one move: "mere failure to 'follow the crowd'" is not dereliction of duty, and directors cannot be required to surrender their judgment to other companies' boards. The court's issue framing treats this as a separate question from the fraud, illegality and conflict analysis.

The plaintiff pleaded negligence about the **substance** of the decision, which was that refusing lights was a bad business choice. The plaintiff never pleaded negligence about **process**, meaning that the board failed to inform itself before deciding. Nothing in the facts alleges that the directors did not study attendance, costs or financing.

That gap is why the two cases do not conflict. Van Gorkom's attack was that the board never informed itself. Shlensky's attack was that the board decided badly, and the court called that question "beyond our jurisdiction and ability."

A plaintiff who had alleged the Cubs board never looked at any attendance or financing data might have had a Van Gorkom-style theory. The brief does not say whether Shlensky could have pleaded that. The facts do say the plaintiff alleged financing was readily available and that lights would pay for themselves, so someone had looked at the numbers.
