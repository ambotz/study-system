**user**: does ultra vires mean beyond the powers
[Timestamp: 2026/10/10 16:38:16]

**ai**: Yes. *Ultra vires* is Latin for "beyond the powers." It describes an act taken outside the legal authority of the person or body that took it.

Two uses matter for your classes:

- **Corporate law (BusOrg):** A corporation's act is ultra vires if it exceeds the powers granted in its charter or by statute. Modern statutes, like 8 Del. C. § 124, have mostly limited the doctrine. A third party can rarely use it to void a corporate act, and challenges are generally confined to a stockholder suit, an action by the state, or an action by the corporation itself.
- **Administrative and constitutional law (Con Law):** An agency or officer acts ultra vires when it goes beyond the authority Congress or the Constitution gave it. That is probably the sense in the Slaughter note you attached, where the question is whether a removal or an agency action exceeded legal authority.

**user**: what is the antithesis of the unitary executive theory
[Timestamp: 2026/10/10 18:42:35]

**ai**: The usual opposite is the **functionalist, or pluralist, view of executive power**. It holds that Congress can structure the executive branch, including by insulating some officers from presidential control. Other labels are the "departmentalist" or "checks-and-balances" account, and the "Madisonian" or "administrative-state" view.

**Unitary executive theory:**
- Article II's Vesting Clause gives all executive power to the President, and the Take Care Clause makes the President answerable for how it is used.
- Anyone who exercises executive power must therefore be removable at will.
- This is the position *Trump v. Slaughter* adopts, as the note on that case explains: "only one President."

**The opposing view:**
- Article II does not say that removal power is implied. Congress creates offices under Article I and the Necessary and Proper Clause, so it may set the terms of those offices, including tenure protection.
- The Take Care Clause "speaks of duty, not power," as Kagan put it in *Seila Law*. At-will removal is one way to carry out that duty, not the only way.
- Some functions, such as monetary policy, adjudication and impartial enforcement, work better at a distance from partisan politics.
- Accountability is one value among several. Continuity, expertise and impartiality count too.

In your materials, this is Sotomayor's *Slaughter* dissent (the "Holding and reasoning" section). The same position runs through *Humphrey's Executor* and *Morrison v. Olson*, which asked whether a removal limit "impedes" the President instead of asking whether the officer is "purely executive."

There is also a **strong-unitary versus weak-unitary** distinction. Strong unitarians want at-will control over all executive power, including prosecution and adjudication. Weaker versions accept some limits, such as civil-service protections or Fed-style exceptions. The *Slaughter* majority takes the strong view but reserves the Fed, non-Article III judges and bodies with no executive power.

I haven't opened the *Cook* note you attached. Its treatment of the Fed exception may add a historical, non-functional counterweight to the unitary view.

**user**: so if I Was arguing against the unitary executive theory, is there a name of the opposite argument I could use?
[Timestamp: 2026/10/10 18:43:46]

**ai**: There's no single name like "anti-unitary executive theory," but you can use these labels, from most to least common:

- **Functionalism.** Ask whether a removal limit actually impairs the President's ability to perform constitutional duties. This is the *Morrison* and *Humphrey's* approach, and the label your professor will most likely expect.
- **The "independent agencies" or "Humphrey's" model.** Congress may create expert, bipartisan bodies insulated from at-will removal.
- **Congressional-power or Necessary-and-Proper view.** Congress creates offices and defines their terms, so tenure protection is part of that power. This is the core of Sotomayor's dissent in *Slaughter*.
- **Checks-and-balances or "separated powers, shared powers" view.** The Constitution does not wall the branches off from each other, and Congress can check the President through how it designs offices. This draws on *Youngstown*, especially Jackson's concurrence.
- **Departmentalist** and **"liquidation by practice."** Longstanding practice, such as 140 years of independent agencies, has settled the meaning of the Constitution. The note ties this to Baude's work on liquidation.
- **Pluralist executive** or **"the executive branch is not unitary."** Some scholars use this label. Examples are Lawrence Lessig and Cass Sunstein ("The Myth of the Unitary Executive"), Peter Strauss, and Jed Shugerman on the historical side. Check these attributions before you cite them. I'm going from memory, not from anything in your vault.

The strongest framing is usually one of two. You can argue that the text leaves removal to Congress, as in the Necessary and Proper point. Or you can argue that the Decision of 1789 did not settle the question and that later practice did. I'd rely on one of those instead of a label, because courts decide on arguments, not on names.

**user**: so was the alleged breach of fiduciary duty in caremark the breach of the duty of care?
[Timestamp: 2026/10/11 12:01:45]

**ai**: It was framed as a duty-of-care claim, but *Caremark* recast it as a **good-faith** claim, which Delaware later placed in the duty of loyalty.

**The original claim.** The plaintiffs alleged the directors breached their fiduciary duty by failing to monitor employee misconduct. That is an inattention theory, which is traditionally a care claim. Allen's opinion sits in the care tradition. The note says he draws from Learned Hand's *Barnes v. Andrews* "the core element of any corporate law duty of care inquiry: whether there was good faith effort to be informed and exercise judgment."

**How Allen reframed it.** The note says Allen separates two contexts of inattention liability:

- **A board decision that proves ill-advised.** The business judgment rule governs, and the court does not review the content of the decision. This is the duty-of-care branch, covered in [[Duty of care - informed decision]].
- **Unconsidered inaction.** This is the oversight claim, and *Caremark* is about this branch. The standard is "a sustained or systematic failure of the board to exercise oversight — such as an utter failure to attempt to assure a reasonable information and reporting system exists."

The test turns on **good faith**, not on diligence or negligence. Liability needs a lack of good faith, so the standard is not ordinary care.

**Where it ended up.** The Context section of the note says *Stone v. Ritter* later "locates oversight in the **duty of loyalty**." That has a practical consequence. Oversight liability falls outside § 102(b)(7) exculpation, which covers care but not loyalty or bad faith. This makes oversight one of the few routes to director damages that a charter exculpation clause cannot close.

**Short answer for a cold call.** *Caremark* pleaded and reasoned as a care-style inattention claim. It set a good-faith standard, and *Stone v. Ritter* made that a loyalty claim.

The note's coverage of *Stone* is brief. If you want its exact holding, I'd check the doctrine module [[Oversight liability - Caremark and good faith]], which I haven't opened.

**user**: Was there something special in the Iran Nuclear Agreement Review Act that allowed the plan to go into effect with only one third of the two Houses of congress? **ai**: Your guess is close, but the one-third figure doesn't come from a provision in the bill. It comes from how the veto and the filibuster work.

**What the note says.** INARA required the President to submit the agreement to Congress for approval or disapproval, "subject to veto." That meant "the Plan could go into effect as an executive agreement with the support of one third of one of the two Houses of Congress." A disapproval motion was filibustered and died.

**Why the number is one third.** The note doesn't spell this out, so this part is my own reasoning:

- **The statute only gave Congress a chance to disapprove.** INARA didn't require a vote of approval, so the agreement took effect unless Congress blocked it. The statute itself was passed by ordinary majorities.
- **A disapproval resolution is an ordinary bill, so the President can veto it.** Obama would have vetoed it. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each House, so one third of either House could sustain the veto.
- **The filibuster raised the bar in the Senate.** A disapproval resolution needed 60 votes to get past a filibuster. Opponents could therefore block it with 41 senators, which is a little over one third.

The "one third of one of the two Houses" wording is the casebook's shorthand for that blocking power. No provision in the bill says one third is enough. The structure is the veto override threshold from Article I, § 7, plus Senate cloture, and INARA left both in place.

**Why this matters in the note.** The note uses INARA to contrast two ways of binding the United States. A treaty needs two-thirds of the Senate to approve, and an agreement that only needs one third to survive a disapproval vote is far easier to adopt. That sets up the Cotton letter's point that the next President "could revoke such an agreement with the stroke of a pen." The casebook says "that turned out to be true," after Trump withdrew.

I'm relying on my own knowledge of INARA's mechanics, and I haven't checked the statute text. The veto and filibuster points are standard, but confirm them against the statute before you rely on them for an exam.

**user**: Can you help me understand the note "the debate here makes the initiation question look less ambiguous than the practice suggests" and why that makes the practice argument hard? What is the throughline there?
[Timestamp: 2026/10/11 22:45:44]

**ai**: The sentence sits in the "Practice and liquidation" bullet of [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]. The throughline is that **liquidation can resolve ambiguity but cannot overrule clear text or clear original meaning**. The practice argument therefore depends on how ambiguous the text is, and the Convention debate makes it less ambiguous than the practice assumes.

**The steps**

1. **Liquidation's job is to settle ambiguity.** Madison's Federalist No. 37 idea, as the note puts it, is that deliberate, repeated, accepted practice can fix the meaning of an unclear provision. The note says explicitly that it "cannot override clear text."
2. **The debate suggests the initiation question was not ambiguous.** The note gives these points:
   - The motion changed "make" to "declare," "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks."
   - Sherman: "The executive shd be able to repel and not to commence war."
   - Every speaker who objected to executive war-making prevailed.
   - The only executive power named is repelling attacks.
   - The Yoo reading has to treat "commence" and "clogging" as beside the point.

   On that record, the Convention gave Congress the decision to initiate war and left the President defense against sudden attacks.
3. **Practice points the other way.** The note says there have been only five formal declarations, yet presidents have used force many times without one. The casebook asks whether practice "liquidate[s]" the meaning or whether "a great deal of the nation's practice... has been unconstitutional."
4. **So the practice argument is hard.** If the text were really ambiguous, long-standing presidential practice could fill the gap. If the text is clear, as the debate suggests, then there is nothing to liquidate. Practice then looks like a series of departures from the Constitution, not evidence of what it means.

**Why this is the key move.** Anyone defending presidential war-making by practice must first show the original meaning was open. The Convention record, as the note reads it, cuts against that.

**Limits of the note's argument.** The note is taking a side. It calls the majority reading "the better reading of the debate." Sherman's worry that "declare" narrowed Congress too much and King's gloss that "make" meant "conduct" give the Yoo reading something to work with. A defender of practice would press those points to show real ambiguity, and then liquidation could apply. The note's "more ambiguous than the practice suggests" is a judgment about how those points weigh.

The same method appears in [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)]], where both sides claim liquidation. The dissent argues that deliberate practice "put at rest" the question. The majority answers that it has "never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory." Baude has written on liquidation, so be ready to state its conditions: genuine indeterminacy, deliberate practice, and settlement.

**ai**: Your instinct is right. Baude is an advocate for liquidation, not a neutral describer. The details below are mostly from the vault, plus what I remember of his work.

**The conditions**

The vault's [[Modes of constitutional argument]] module gives Baude's three requirements. The third is the one you're missing:

1. **An indeterminate text.** Liquidation resolves ambiguity and cannot override clear meaning.
2. **A deliberate course of practice.** It must be a considered course of practice, not mere drift or an unexamined habit.
3. **Settlement.** The public must accept the outcome, so the question is actually closed.

The same module records Madison's own, more demanding conditions from the [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]:

- repetition
- duration
- variety of form
- concurrence of all three branches
- concurrence of the states
- acquiescence of the people at large

It also says the party asserting liquidation carries the burden of showing them.

**Baude's position**

The vault doesn't include a Baude reading, and your professor gloss is empty, so this part is from my memory of his article "Constitutional Liquidation" (Stanford Law Review, 2019). Check it before relying on it.

- **He defends liquidation as a legitimate interpretive method.** He argues it is historically grounded in Madison and the founding generation and fits an originalist approach. It is not a back door for living constitutionalism.
- **He treats it as a way of fixing meaning.** Practice does not amend the Constitution. It settles what an ambiguous provision meant all along.
- **He builds in limits.** That is why the conditions above matter. Without them, any long-standing practice could claim to be settled.
- **Settlement can be conditional.** Liquidation is a claim about what was settled at a given time. The vault's treatment of the Bank War ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]) shows a settlement coming undone.

**Why this matters for the notes.** The questions in the *Slaughter* note ask whether the conditions were met, so they track his framework. Both sides there argue about indeterminacy, deliberation and settlement. The war-powers note turns on the first condition, which is why it says the initiation question "looks too clear for liquidation to operate."

On exams, expect the professor to press the conditions, since liquidation is his own scholarly subject. State the three conditions, and for each one say how the facts meet it or fail it.

**user**: If Congress chose to override the President's veto and the WPR became law, how is is still a concurrent resolution and how is that a correct objection by the president?
[Timestamp: 2026/10/12 15:29:11]

**ai**: The WPR itself isn't a concurrent resolution. It is a joint resolution that became law. The concurrent resolution is a mechanism **inside** it, in § 5(c), and that provision is the one the note says Nixon was right about.

**Two different instruments**

- **The WPR as a whole.** The note says Nixon returned House **Joint** Resolution 542, and Congress repassed it over his veto in November 1973. A joint resolution is presented to the President, so it can become law. The veto override satisfied Article I, § 7 for the WPR as a whole.
- **The § 5(c) mechanism.** Under § 5(c), forces "shall be removed by the President if the Congress so directs by **concurrent resolution**." A concurrent resolution passes both Houses but is **not presented to the President**. The WPR as enacted lets Congress take a future action that way.

**Why that was a correct objection**

The override cured the statute's passage. It did not cure what § 5(c) authorizes Congress to do later. Under the note's reading:

- A concurrent resolution "does not normally have the force of law, since it denies the President his constitutional role in approving legislation."
- Giving that kind of resolution binding force skips presentment, so it fails under [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
- [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]], ten years later, confirmed the point, and the note concludes: "§ 5(c) is unconstitutional after Chadha; the question is severability."

In short, Congress cannot enact a valid statute that authorizes it to bypass presentment in the future. A veto override cannot make that part valid, because the defect is in the mechanism, not in how the bill was passed.

**The caveat the note makes**

Nixon's message is mixed. The note says he was **right** on the concurrent resolution, but his broader constitutional objection to the 60-day clock "begs the question." The note notes that the casebook's 2020 Iran episode shows the consequence. A WPR resolution was presented to the President and vetoed, which is the route that works after *Chadha*.

I'm relying on the note's account, not on the statute's text. The structure is clear from the note, but check § 5(c) in the casebook if you need the exact wording.

**user**: is the opposing argument that this is a delagation of power to President? How does one respond to the concurrent objection challenge?
[Timestamp: 2026/10/12 15:31:25]

**ai**: Partly, and the answer to your second question is more important. The opposing argument is a **conditional-delegation** one, but it is the argument *Chadha* rejected, and it runs into a problem inside the WPR itself.

**The delegation framing**

Here is how the argument works in defense of § 5(c):

- Declaring war and controlling the use of force are Congress's powers.
- By enacting the WPR, Congress allows the President to use force for a period, subject to a condition. That condition is that Congress can end it by concurrent resolution.
- On this view, Congress is not making new law. It is withdrawing authority it granted.

This is the legislative-veto defense that was made in *Chadha*. The note itself shows a weak point. § 8(c)(2) says the WPR does not grant authority, and § 8(c) disclaims any intent "to alter the constitutional authority of the Congress or of the President." Congress cannot easily say it delegated 60 days of war power while disclaiming that it authorized anything. The note says the WPR "has been treated as a de facto license," which is the delegation reading, but the statute disavows it.

**Responses to the concurrent-resolution objection** (the first is from the note, the rest from my own knowledge of *Chadha*)

1. **Concede and sever.** The note says § 5(c) is unconstitutional after *Chadha* and the open question is severability. I recall the WPR having a separability clause, but check the statute. If so, the rest of the WPR stays, including the § 5(b) clock.
2. **Argue the resolution has no legal effect.** If the President never had power to continue a war without authorization, a resolution withdrawing support changes nothing about his legal rights. *Chadha* defines "legislative" by effect, meaning whether an action alters legal rights, duties and relations. A resolution that directs forces home plausibly does.
3. **Argue Congress's war powers are special.** The weakness is that nothing in *Chadha* exempts them. Any binding congressional direction still has to go through presentment.
4. **Use the route that works.** Congress can pass a **joint** resolution directing removal of forces. It is presented to the President and he can veto it, so Congress would need two-thirds in each House to override. The note's 2020 Iran example shows this. It is also the weakness Nixon's message identified: acting by statute is costly for Congress.
5. **Treat concurrent resolutions as political tools.** The note's Kosovo example shows the House defeating a § 5(c) resolution. A concurrent resolution can still be a signal even if it can't bind.

**Linking it back to Nixon.** The reason Nixon's concurrent-resolution objection was correct, and his objection to the 60-day clock was not, is that the clock operates by statute and goes through presentment. § 5(c) does not. That is why the note says the WPR was not wholly vulnerable.

I haven't checked the statute for the separability clause or for any post-*Chadha* expedited-procedure provisions, so confirm those before you cite them.
