## What it is

How a funder's return is taxed, and — more consequentially for this course — the proposal to
tax it **punitively** through a standalone excise regime rather than to regulate the
agreements themselves.

## How it works

**Today**, in outline: a funder's return is ordinary income or capital gain depending on
structure and holding period, taxed at the investor level for pass-through funds, with
tax-exempt limited partners (pension funds, endowments) generally exempt and foreign
investors relying on treaty protection. That is the baseline the proposal attacks.

**The proposal** — [[Tackling Predatory Litigation Funding Act - S 1821]], carried unchanged
into the Senate's 2025 reconciliation draft as new § 5000E-1:

| Element | Provision |
|---|---|
| Rate | **40.8%** — top individual rate plus the 3.8% net investment income tax |
| Base | "Qualified litigation proceeds": realised gains, net income or other profit derived from any litigation financing agreement |
| Payer | "Covered parties" — non-attorney third parties receiving funds under such an agreement, taxed at the **entity level**, pass-throughs included |
| Character | A separate **excise** tax, not income tax — therefore **no treaty protection** |
| Losses | Other investment losses **cannot offset** it |
| Exempt investors | **No exemption** for tax-exempt or foreign investors |
| Withholding | Named parties and involved law firms withhold **20.4%** |
| Carve-out | Straight loans below **7%**, or **2× the 30-year Treasury yield** |
| Scope of "civil action" | "[A]ny civil action, administrative proceeding, claim, or cause of action" — **no geographic limit** |

## Why it exists

As drafted, not to raise revenue. A 40.8% entity-level levy on gross profit with no loss
offset does not fit a fund whose economics depend on a few large winners covering many
zeros — see [[Pricing and returns in legal finance]]. The rate is set to remove the return.

The design reveals the theory. **Lending below roughly 10% is untouched; taking a contingent
share of someone else's litigation proceeds is taxed at 40.8%.** Strip away the tax
vocabulary and the objection is to sharing in the fruits of another's lawsuit — which is
[[Champerty and maintenance]], reached through the Internal Revenue Code.

The procedural point is the important one. Disclosure bills have stalled for a decade because
they need committee consensus. A **tax provision can move through reconciliation** on a
simple majority. Instrument choice here is about what can pass, not about what fits the
problem.

## Variants

- **Excise tax on proceeds** — the S. 1821 / § 5000E-1 approach.
- **Ordinary income characterisation** without a separate excise — the status quo.
- **Withholding at source** — 20.4% here, imposed on the claimant and its law firm, which
  makes the litigants unpaid collection agents.
- **State-level taxation** — not currently attempted.

## What can go wrong

- **The definition overshoots.** "[A] direct or collateralized interest in the proceeds"
  contingent on litigation outcomes reaches routine commercial arrangements — some insurance,
  receivables financing secured on disputed claims, and contingent-fee-adjacent structures.
  Sidley flags this as the practical problem.
- **No grandfathering**, so existing agreements are repriced retroactively in economic terms.
- **Double taxation** for corporate pass-throughs, and unresolved **basis recovery**.
- **Institutional investors take the hit.** Pension funds and endowments are significant
  limited partners and get no exemption, so the incidence falls on capital providers rather
  than on the managers the rhetoric targets.
- **Extraterritorial reach.** No requirement that the action be in a US court, under US law,
  or between US parties — which sweeps in international arbitration funding with no US
  connection.
- **Bill, not law.** The House rejected the Tillis language before the Senate draft picked it
  up, and survival into final legislation was uncertain. **Date every statement about it.**
