## What it is

The financing of **plaintiff law firms** rather than claims, in litigation where value comes
from the number of claimants rather than the strength of any one claim. It is the largest
segment of the US market — **38% of invested dollars** by Swiss Re's 2021 figures — and it
is governed by neither the commercial regime nor the consumer one.

## How it works

The chain runs firm-side, and the claimant is never party to it:

1. **A financier extends a credit line to a plaintiff firm.** Parikh's example: Centerbridge's
   "$100 million line of credit" to Mikal Watts's firm in the PG&E bankruptcy.
2. **The firm spends it on claim acquisition** — television and social media advertising,
   lead generators, aggregators working by "direct calling," "infomercials," "online
   videos." Camp Lejeune alone drew "more than $145 million" of advertising in a year.
3. **Volume creates settlement pressure.** Defendants "feel more 'pressure' to settle when
   up against a lawyer with a large 'volume of cases'."
4. **Claim count supports more borrowing**, and the cycle repeats. A broker quoted by Parikh
   describes it as: borrow, buy ads, wait for real lawyers to establish liability, use the
   clients to borrow more, "lather, rinse, and repeat."

Alongside the firm-level facility, financiers also advance **non-recourse money to
individual claimants** for living and medical expenses under Capital-Provision Agreements,
which in some cases carry settlement vetoes and restrictions on changing counsel.

## Why it exists

Mass tort litigation has a capital structure problem no other litigation has. Liability is
established once, for everyone, but each claimant must be **found, signed and worked up**,
years before any recovery. That is a large, front-loaded, purely marketing expense against a
contingent payoff — exactly the profile that requires outside capital, and exactly the
profile a bank will not lend against.

It also explains why the money attaches to the **firm**. No individual mass tort claim is
worth underwriting; the book is.

## Variants

- **Firm credit facilities** — the core structure.
- **Case acquisition financing** — advances specifically against marketing spend and
  signed-claimant counts.
- **Medical funding and lien financing** — advancing treatment costs, repaid from recovery.
- **Claim aggregators and lead generators** — intermediaries who assemble claimants and sell
  them on.
- **Crossholder positions** — Parikh's hypothetical financier holding debt at several levels
  of a defendant's capital structure, or a short position in the defendant's stock, while
  funding claims against it.

## What can go wrong

- **The screening incentive breaks.** A funder exposed to one claim must screen it; a lender
  repaid out of a book need not, and is rewarded for volume. This is why
  [[Antill and Grenadier - Financing the Litigation Arms Race]] and
  [[Avraham and Sebok - Empirical Investigation of Third Party Consumer Litigant Funding]]
  can find rigorous screening in their markets while
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] finds the opposite in this one.
- **Weak claims dilute real ones.** "[E]very dollar received by a false victim is one less
  dollar received by a meritorious one."
- **Duration harms claimants and suits lenders.** A financier can wait a decade; an injured
  claimant usually cannot.
- **Defendants exit the settlement system**, moving "from state court to federal court, from
  MDL to bankruptcy" to escape inflated claim counts.
- **Leverage plus a partner run.** A firm carrying acquisition debt is the fragile structure
  in [[The law firm as a capital structure]], with the run incentive amplified.
- **Nobody is the claimant's fiduciary as to the financing**, because the claimant is not a
  party to it.
