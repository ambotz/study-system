## What it is

Capital advanced against a legal claim that the recipient repays **only out of a successful
recovery**. If the case loses, the funder gets nothing and has no claim against the
claimant, the law firm, or any other asset. Burford's formulation: "Legal finance is not a
loan; it is generally non-recourse, meaning that Burford assumes downside risk of loss and
the client need not repay any investment or advance unless and until matters resolve
successfully."

## How it works

- The funder advances money at the front, either as it is spent (fees and expenses
  financing) or in a lump at signing (monetization).
- Repayment is conditioned on recovery, so the obligation is **contingent**, not absolute.
- Because there is no repayment floor, the funder prices for total loss on a share of its
  book. Pricing therefore looks like equity, not debt — see
  [[Pricing and returns in legal finance]].
- Recovery is distributed through a **waterfall** that sets who is paid in what order.
  Where a case settles for less than expected, the waterfall — not the headline percentage
  — determines what the claimant actually nets.

## Why it exists

Three reasons, and they are different for each party:

- **For the claimant**, it converts a risk it cannot bear into one someone else holds. A
  company with a meritorious claim and no litigation budget either does not sue or settles
  cheap.
- **For the funder**, non-recourse is what makes the return legal and the pricing
  defensible. A recourse loan at a 3x multiple is a usurious loan; a non-recourse
  investment at a 3x multiple is priced for the cases that return zero.
- **Doctrinally**, it is the structural argument against characterising these deals as
  **loans** at all, which is what keeps them outside usury statutes. That argument is the
  whole fight in the consumer market — see
  [[Consumer funding and the price of money]] — and it is why the
  [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] and
  [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] line matters in Class 4.

## Variants

- **Fees and expenses financing** — drawn down as costs are incurred.
- **Monetization** — cash up front against a pending claim or award, with residual upside
  retained.
- **Claim purchase** — the funder buys the claim outright, where assignment is permitted.
- **Partial recourse and hybrid deals** — some law firm facilities take recourse to the
  firm's other assets or fee streams, which makes them look much more like lending.
- **Consumer advances** — non-recourse in form, small, and priced as a percentage of the
  amount funded applied at intervals, which compounds.

## What can go wrong

- **Recharacterisation as a loan.** If a court finds the repayment obligation is not truly
  contingent, usury law attaches.
- **Waterfall inversion.** In a disappointing settlement the funder's preferred return can
  consume most or all of the proceeds, leaving the claimant with little. This is the
  mechanism behind Swiss Re's estimate that funded claimants receive about 43% of costs and
  compensation against 55% unfunded.
- **Capital-plus-time pricing on a long case.** Where the return is a multiple that steps
  up with time, a case that runs years costs the claimant far more than the headline
  suggests, and the funder has no incentive to shorten it.
- **Cross-collateralisation.** In a portfolio, a recovery on one matter services the
  funder's exposure across the book — so "non-recourse" as to a single case is not
  non-recourse as to the claimant's other claims. See
  [[Portfolio finance and cross-collateralisation]].
