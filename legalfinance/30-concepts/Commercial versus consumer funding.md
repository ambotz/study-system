## What it is

Two products that share a name and almost nothing else. GAO draws the line at the outset of
its report, and the line explains most of the disagreement on this syllabus: arguments made
about one are routinely answered with evidence about the other.

## How it works

| | **Commercial** | **Consumer** |
|---|---|---|
| Who receives it | Corporate claimants and law firms | Individual plaintiffs, mostly personal injury |
| What it pays for | Legal fees and expenses, or cash against a pending claim | The plaintiff's **living expenses** while the claim is pending |
| Typical size | $2.3m single-case, $4.5m portfolio (GAO); Burford floor of $3m against ~$30m expected damages | **$1,000 to $10,000** (GAO) |
| Pricing | Multiple, percentage of recovery, or hybrid, behind a waterfall | Percentage of the amount funded, applied at intervals — 15% to 18% per six months |
| Counterparty sophistication | General counsel with outside advisers | An injured individual, usually unrepresented as to the funding deal |
| Existing regulation | Essentially none | State statutes: Arkansas 17% interest cap, Tennessee 10% annual fee cap, registration and contract disclosure in Maine and Nebraska |

Market share, by Swiss Re's 2021 figures: **mass torts 38%, commercial litigation 37%,
personal injury 25%**. So roughly 63% of invested dollars sit outside the commercial
product that the industry's public advocacy describes.

## Why it exists

The split is not a market accident; it follows from **who bears what risk**. A corporate
claimant can price its own deal, hire counsel to negotiate the waterfall, and walk away.
An individual waiting on a settlement cannot, and is borrowing against a claim to pay rent.
Consumer regulation therefore tracks the consumer-credit model — rate caps, registration,
mandatory disclosure of the total repayable — while commercial funding is left to contract.

Steinitz's recommendations follow exactly this line: minimum returns for "consumer and
aggregate litigation," expressly not for sophisticated commercial parties.

## Variants

- **Mass tort and aggregate litigation** sits awkwardly between the two. The claimant is an
  individual, but the financing is at the **law firm** level against a book of cases, so
  neither regime fits cleanly. Class 3 is built on that gap, which
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] is about.
- **Law firm lending** against a contingency book — commercial in form, consumer in
  ultimate exposure.
- **Pre-settlement advances** versus **medical lien funding**, which are distinct consumer
  products.

## What can go wrong

- **Arguing across the line.** The access-to-justice case is usually made with the
  individual plaintiff in mind and defended with commercial data, and the usury critique is
  made with consumer rates and answered with commercial deal terms. Watch for it in every
  reading.
- **Regulating across the line.** A disclosure rule drafted for commercial portfolio deals
  does little for a $3,000 advance; a rate cap written for consumer advances would make
  commercial funding impossible.
- **The aggregate gap.** Mass tort financing is the largest single slice of the market and
  is governed by neither regime.
