## What it is

How a funder gets paid, and what the reported numbers actually measure. Two separate
questions that are constantly conflated: the **structure** of the return in a given deal,
and the **realised returns** the industry reports.

## How it works

**The four structures** (Burford's taxonomy):

| Structure | Form | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Variable** | A percentage of the recovery, "comparable to contingency fees" | Where duration and quantum are both uncertain |
| **Fixed** | Capital back plus a multiple, or a fixed sum | Where the funder wants a floor irrespective of quantum |
| **Hybrid** | A multiple, then a percentage above a threshold | The common commercial shape |
| **Return waterfall** | The order in which parties are paid out of proceeds | Sits on top of any of the above |

- **The waterfall is where the money is decided**, not the headline percentage. A deal
  described as "20% of recovery" behind a funder's preferred return of 3x capital is a
  different deal from the same 20% behind 1x.
- Multiples usually **step up with time**, so a case that runs four years costs the claimant
  materially more than the same case settled in eighteen months.
- Burford's stated principle: "pricing for legal finance is proportional to risk. This may
  reflect the stage of the litigation, the type of matter, the likely duration or another
  factor."
- **Consumer pricing is different in kind.** GAO found rates "starting at 15 percent of the
  amount funded" and, at a second funder, "18 percent of the amount funded (applied every 6
  months)" — which compounds to roughly 39% a year and is nowhere expressed as an APR.

**The reported returns, and what each measure means:**

| Source | Figure | Measure | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Swiss Re (2021) | Personal injury IRR 32.7% / 24.6% / 35.3% (2019/2020/2021F); commercial 29.5% / 26.5% / 29.9%; mass tort 21.2% / 25.5% / 26.0% | **IRR**, time-weighted | Sourced to Morning Investments, an industry data provider; not audited fund returns |
| Swiss Re benchmark | 13% PE, 13% VC, 10% S&P 500 over 15 years | Long-run comparators | Chosen by the party arguing the returns are excessive |
| GAO (2022) | One funder 93% ROIC since inception; another 91% on two funds since 2017 | **Return on invested capital**, not time-weighted | Self-reported by the funders; says nothing about holding period |

**ROIC and IRR are not comparable.** A 93% ROIC over an average four-year hold is roughly
an 18% IRR; over two years it is about 39%. Any argument that turns on how profitable
funding is has to say which measure it is using and over what period, and most do not.

## Why it exists

Pricing is high because the product is **non-recourse** and the loss rate is real — a
funder must recover its total exposure on winners plus enough to cover the cases that
return zero, plus the cost of capital it raised from its own investors, plus the diligence
cost on deals it declined. Whether the realised returns exceed what that risk justifies is
the empirical question nobody can answer, because the loss rates are not public. See
[[Non-recourse capital]].

## Variants

- **Preferred return plus carry**, imported from private equity, in fund-level structures.
- **Time-stepped multiples** — 2x to 18 months, 3x thereafter, and so on.
- **Capped returns**, sometimes negotiated where a claimant has leverage.
- **Portfolio pricing**, materially below the sum of single-case pricing because outcome
  risk is diversified. See [[Portfolio finance and cross-collateralisation]].
- **Percentage-of-amount-funded, applied at intervals** — the consumer shape, and the one
  usury statutes were written for.

## What can go wrong

- **The claimant nets little or nothing** on a disappointing settlement, because the
  waterfall pays the funder's preferred return first. This is the mechanism underlying
  Swiss Re's estimate that funded claimants receive about **43%** of costs and compensation
  against **55%** unfunded.
- **Duration works against the claimant and for the funder** wherever the multiple steps
  up, which is the incentive problem behind the case-prolongation allegation.
- **The APR is not disclosed** in consumer deals, so a plaintiff cannot compare the advance
  to a credit card or a payday loan. Swiss Re recommends APR disclosure for exactly this
  reason; Class 3 is built on the comparison.
- **Comparing the wrong numbers.** See the ROIC/IRR point above.
