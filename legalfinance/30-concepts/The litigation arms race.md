## What it is

The escalating spending both sides do in litigation to improve their position, most of which
creates no value because the underlying dispute is a **transfer** rather than a productive
activity. Antill and Grenadier's term for the dynamic, and the frame that makes litigation
finance an economics question rather than an ethics one.

## How it works

- Each party's spending raises its expected share of a fixed pot. Neither side's spending
  grows the pot.
- Because the return to spending is relative, spending is **strategically complementary**:
  the more one side spends, the more the other must spend to hold position. The equilibrium
  is high spending on both sides and no change in the merits.
- All of that spend is **deadweight loss** in welfare terms, however legitimate each
  individual dollar is.
- Litigation finance enters as a **constraint-relaxer** on the plaintiff's side. Two effects
  run in opposite directions:

| Channel | Direction | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Escalation** | Costs up | A funded plaintiff spends more and holds out longer, so total expenditure and deadweight loss rise |
| **Deterrence** | Costs down | Defensive spending only pays if it can move the outcome. Against a plaintiff with committed capital the marginal return to escalation falls, so the defendant escalates less |

- The net sign is **ambiguous** and depends on parameters. That is the paper's actual
  finding, and the reason both lobbies quote it.

## Why it exists

The concept exists to separate two things the policy debate constantly merges: **more
spending** and **worse outcomes**. An arms race is bad because it burns resources, not
because either side is behaving improperly. So a finding that funding increases litigation
spending is not by itself an indictment — the question is whether it also corrects the
asymmetry that let the better-capitalised party win on resources rather than merits.

It also supplies the cleanest answer to the frivolous-claims charge. A non-recourse funder
is a residual claimant paid only on success, so its incentive is to **screen harder** than
the plaintiff would alone. Funding a weak case is a bad investment, not a cheap lottery
ticket.

## Variants

- **Defensive investment** — the defendant's side of the race, and the thing the deterrence
  channel predicts will fall.
- **Substantive coercion by attrition** — the pre-funding baseline, where a defendant wins
  by outspending rather than by being right.
- **Settlement as the off-ramp** — both parties prefer to stop spending, which is why the
  race is resolved by bargaining rather than by trial in almost every case.

## What can go wrong

- **Treating higher spending as proof of harm.** It is half the model, and the other half
  points the other way.
- **Assuming the screening result generalises.** It holds where the funder is exposed to the
  **individual claim**. Where the lending is at firm level against a book, no single claim
  must justify itself and the screening incentive weakens — which is the gap
  [[Mass tort financing and claim aggregation]] describes.
- **Reading an ambiguous result as support.** The paper does not say funding is good or bad,
  and citing it for either is a misuse.
