## What it is

Insurance written against litigation outcomes rather than against the underlying loss. It
competes with litigation finance at some points and makes it cheaper at others.

## How it works

| Product | Who buys | Insures against | Economics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Judgment preservation (JPI)** | A plaintiff who has **already won** | The judgment being reduced or reversed on appeal | Sets a floor; insurer pays if the award falls below it. Appian: **$500m** floor on a **$2bn** judgment for a **$57.3m** premium — about **9.8%** |
| **Adverse judgment (AJI)** | A **defendant** | A large adverse judgment above a retention | Premiums typically **5–12%**, up to 20%+ on high-risk cases; limits **$5m to $1.5bn** per risk |
| **After-the-event (ATE)** | Usually a claimant | Own costs and adverse costs if the claim is lost | Standard in cost-shifting jurisdictions |
| **Portfolio or wrap cover** | Funders and firms | Aggregate losses across a book | Lets a funder lower its own cost of capital |

**The key interaction with funding**: an insured claim is better collateral. JPI "allows
claimants to seek financing at attractive conditions with the insured claim working as
collateral," and companies borrow against the policy itself. So insurance does not only
compete with funders — it lets them lend more cheaply against the same claim.

## Why it exists

Because **risk becomes priceable at different stages**, and insurers are better at the priced
end. An unresolved claim is a binary bet on facts, law and a jury; a judgment on appeal is a
narrower question with an actuarial history. That is why a JPI premium near 10% sits an order
of magnitude below what a funder charges for comparable downside protection, and why Aon's
Stephen Kyriacou could say "[f]unders have started to kind of cede that ground to us."

For the claimant the product converts a contingent, appealable asset into something close to a
balance-sheet item — which is why Appian's policy moved its market capitalisation rather than
just its litigation position.

## Variants

- **Wrapped funding** — a funder's position insured, lowering its required return.
- **Contingent legal risk cover** in M&A, where a pending case is the obstacle to a deal.
- **Capital relief structures**, letting a corporate recognise value earlier.
- **Insurer-backed funding**, where the carrier supplies the capital directly.

## What can go wrong

- **Appellate risk may be genuinely unpriceable.** Appian's $2bn judgment was **later
  overturned** — the insured event happened. Either the policy was mispriced, the market has
  repriced since, or appellate outcomes resist underwriting. Worth checking what happened to
  JPI capacity and pricing after 2024.
- **Adverse selection.** The claimant knows more about the case than the carrier and buys
  cover when worried.
- **Moral hazard on settlement.** A claimant with a guaranteed floor has a weaker reason to
  accept a reasonable offer — the same distortion alleged against funding.
- **Disclosure exposure.** A policy is a document about the merits of a pending case, and the
  privilege analysis is no better settled than for funding agreements. Class 8.
- **Stacking.** Insurance plus funding plus a contingency fee can leave a thin residual, which
  is the Class 1 argument about the claimant's share arriving by a new route.
