## What it is

The three things a commercial funder actually sells, distinguished by **when the client
gets the money and what the client keeps**. Burford's taxonomy — fees and expenses
financing, monetization, claim purchase — is the vocabulary this course uses.

## How it works

| Shape | What the funder pays | When | What the client keeps | Client's problem it solves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fees and expenses** | Legal costs as incurred, up to a commitment | Drawn down over the case | The recovery net of the funder's return | No litigation budget; cannot carry the spend |
| **Monetization** | Cash against a pending claim, judgment, award or fee entitlement | At signing, or on the client's schedule | Residual upside on resolution | Needs liquidity now; wants a guaranteed floor |
| **Claim purchase** | Purchase price for the claim itself | At closing | Nothing further | Wants the asset off the books entirely |

- Any of the three can be written over **one matter** or over a **portfolio**. Average
  commercial deal sizes reported to GAO were about **$2.3m single-case** and **$4.5m
  portfolio**; a 2021 market report put them at $3.5m and $8.5m.
- Monetization is the shape most people miss, and it is the reason "litigation funding" is
  a poor name for the commercial product. Nothing is being funded — an existing asset is
  being sold forward. Burford's worked example: a Fortune 100 company "accelerated $75
  million of a pending claim, thus guaranteeing minimum return ahead of case resolution
  with no downside risk."

## Why it exists

The three shapes track three different corporate problems. Fees and expenses financing
answers a **cash flow** problem. Monetization answers a **timing and accounting** problem —
a claim is an asset that cannot be recognised until it resolves, so a general counsel who
monetizes converts a contingent asset into cash in the current period. Claim purchase
answers a **risk appetite** problem for a company that wants the litigation gone.

That is also why the corporate-finance framing is not merely rhetorical: for a company with
a real claim, the choice is between financing it and self-funding it, and self-funding is a
capital allocation decision like any other.

## Variants

- **Defence-side financing**, where the funder is paid on a cost-saving or outcome basis
  rather than a recovery. Rare, and usually seen inside a portfolio.
- **Law firm facilities**, where the borrower is the firm rather than the claimant and the
  collateral is a book of contingency fees. Class 7 takes this up.
- **Consumer advances**, which are none of the above — they fund the plaintiff's **living
  expenses** while a personal injury claim is pending, not the litigation. See
  [[Commercial versus consumer funding]].

## What can go wrong

- **Calling a monetization a loan.** It is a sale of a portion of a contingent asset. Get
  this wrong in a term sheet and the tax, accounting and usury analysis all change.
- **Claim purchase runs into assignability.** Many jurisdictions restrict outright
  assignment of causes of action, especially in tort, which is why the commercial market
  mostly avoids it.
- **Mismatch between shape and client need.** A client who wants certainty and is sold a
  fees-and-expenses facility still carries outcome risk; a client who wants maximum upside
  and monetizes has capped itself.
