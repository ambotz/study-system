## What it is

A single facility secured against **several matters at once**, where a recovery on any one
of them services the funder's return across the whole book. Burford: matters combined "in a
cross-collateralized portfolio (which can include both plaintiff and defense matters)."

## How it works

- The funder commits a facility amount against a defined pool of cases, usually a law
  firm's contingency book or a corporate claimant's litigation docket.
- **Cross-collateralisation** is the operative term: the funder's return is not tied to the
  outcome of any single matter. Losses on some cases are absorbed by recoveries on others,
  inside the deal rather than across the funder's balance sheet.
- Because the pool diversifies outcome risk, the funder can price a portfolio **below** the
  sum of what it would charge for each case individually. That price difference is the
  entire commercial logic.
- Matters can usually be added and removed during the term against agreed criteria, which
  makes the pool composition a negotiated point rather than a fixed schedule.

## Why it exists

- **For the funder**, single-case funding is a binary bet with an expensive diligence cost
  per dollar deployed. A portfolio spreads that cost and converts a set of binary outcomes
  into something closer to a distribution — which is what makes the asset class fundable by
  institutional investors, and what makes securitisation conceivable at all (Class 6).
- **For a law firm**, it converts an unpredictable contingency book into working capital
  without the firm having to pick which case to bet on.
- **For a corporate claimant**, it buys budget predictability across a litigation docket
  rather than access to one case.

Deal sizes reflect this: GAO's three commercial funders averaged about **$4.5m per
portfolio** against **$2.3m per single case**.

## Variants

- **Law firm portfolio facilities** — the collateral is a fee book. Class 7.
- **Corporate portfolios** — a company's own claims, sometimes including defence matters.
- **Monetization inside a portfolio** — some matters financed for costs, others accelerated
  for cash.
- **Syndicated portfolios**, where several funders participate in one facility under a
  participation agreement. Class 6.
- **Secondary sales** of portfolio interests. Also Class 6.

## What can go wrong

- **Weak claims ride along.** The argument in
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] is that a portfolio's diversification
  is precisely what allows non-meritorious matters to be carried, because no single case
  has to justify itself. Steinitz makes the same point from the defendant's side: cases
  that would never be funded alone get funded "when bundled in portfolios."
- **"Non-recourse" stops meaning what it meant.** A claimant whose several matters are
  cross-collateralised has, in substance, pledged the others. Losing one case is no longer
  costless to it.
- **Settlement pressure moves across cases.** A funder short of its return on the pool has
  an interest in outcomes in matters it did not originally care about — which is where
  [[Funder control over the case]] gets sharper than it is in a single-case deal.
- **Adverse selection on the add list.** If the client chooses which matters enter the
  pool, it will not choose at random.
