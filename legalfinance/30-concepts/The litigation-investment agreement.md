## What it is

The contract itself, considered as a risk-allocation problem rather than as a legality
problem. Sebok and Wendel's framing: four risks are built into every funding agreement, and
the document exists to allocate them.

This is the file to have open during the **October 13 term sheet exercise** and the
**October 27 drafting exercise**.

## How it works

**The four risks, and the clause families that answer them:**

| Risk | What it is | What answers it |
|---|---|---|
| **Information asymmetry** | The claimholder and counsel know the case; after closing, everything the funder learns comes from the party whose interests may have shifted | Reporting covenants, access to counsel, budget approval, material-development notices |
| **Shirking** | Once funded, the claimholder's marginal incentive to press the case falls, because the funder's return comes off the top. Effort is unobservable | Cooperation and diligent-prosecution covenants, counsel-retention conditions, milestone triggers |
| **Control** | The claimholder decides strategy and settlement; the funder bears the downside. The decision-maker does not hold all the consequences | Consultation rights, consent rights (and their limits), approval of counsel changes, dispute mechanics |
| **Forgone opportunities** | The funder's capital is committed and illiquid for years; a cheap settlement or abandonment destroys value it cannot recover | Minimum-return floors, waterfall priority, anti-abandonment covenants, buy-out rights |

**On top of the risk-allocation layer sit the commercial terms** that the rest of the course
supplies:

- **Product shape** — fees and expenses, monetization, or claim purchase.
  [[Legal finance product shapes]].
- **Recourse** — non-recourse, and how genuinely so. [[Non-recourse capital]], and the
  recharacterisation risk in [[Usury and recharacterisation]].
- **Pricing** — multiple, percentage, hybrid, and the **waterfall**, which is where the money
  is actually decided. [[Pricing and returns in legal finance]].
- **Single matter or portfolio**, and whether cross-collateralised.
  [[Portfolio finance and cross-collateralisation]].
- **Who the counterparty is** — claimant or law firm, which changes the professional
  responsibility analysis entirely. [[Fee sharing and Rule 5.4]].
- **Governing law and forum**, which are substantive here rather than boilerplate, because
  champerty, usury and enforceability all turn on them.
  [[Champerty and maintenance]].
- **Confidentiality, privilege and work product**, and what happens on disclosure. Class 8.

## Why it exists

Because the alternative frameworks do not fit. Sebok and Wendel reject the insurance analogy
— the funder–claimholder relationship is "sufficiently different from that of an insurer and
an insured such that tort law should not be followed" — on the ground that the parties are
sophisticated, the terms negotiated, and the **dependence runs toward the funder** after
closing rather than toward the client.

What follows is that these are **relational contracts**, requiring "renegotiation of terms in
order [to] preserve the contract as an ongoing relationship" over a multi-year case, rather
than a fixed set of obligations enforced by damages at breach.

The practical consequence, and the reason this concept matters for the graded exercises: if
contract is the governing frame, **every protection has to be in the document**. There is no
background duty to fall back on.

## Variants

- **Capital Provision Agreement (CPA)** — Burford's name for its commercial instrument; the
  Sysco agreement was one.
- **Sale and Repurchase Agreement** — the consumer form in
  [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]], drafted as a purchase to avoid usury.
- **Primary and secondary contracts** — the two-layer structure in
  [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]], where the client's deal was non-recourse and the
  **lawyer** was the credit support.
- **Participation agreement** — the syndication instrument, where several funders share one
  facility. Class 6.
- **Attorney acknowledgment** — a required term in consumer deals in New York, without which
  the contract is void. [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]].

## What can go wrong

- **The consent right.** The single most contested clause in the course. Sysco's amended CPA
  said it "shall not accept a settlement offer without [Burford's] prior written consent,
  which shall not be unreasonably withheld" — and an LCIA tribunal enforced it with an
  injunction, while Minnesota called such a stipulation about as "against public policy" as
  one can conceive, and New York now bans it outright for consumers.
- **"Unreasonably withheld" cannot resolve a conflict of objectives.** Sysco wanted to settle
  to preserve supplier relationships; Burford wanted to maximise the recovery. Reasonableness
  has no content when the parties are optimising different variables.
- **Amendments change the deal's character.** The Sysco CPA began with Sysco retaining "full
  control of the assertion and resolution of the claims" and acquired the consent right by
  later amendment.
- **Diversifying into a loan.** Spreading the obligation across many matters weakens the
  contingency the non-recourse structure depends on.
- **Nobody is the claimant's fiduciary** where the money goes to the firm rather than to the
  claimant — the structural gap in
  [[Mass tort financing and claim aggregation]].
