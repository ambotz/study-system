## What it is

Three ways a funder moves an asset after originating it. Together they are the **liquidity
layer** of this market, and how developed they are is the best single measure of whether
litigation finance is an asset class or a collection of private bets.

## How it works

| | What moves | To whom | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Syndication** | A share of a **new** facility, at origination | Other funders, under a **participation agreement** | Deal too large for one balance sheet; diversification |
| **Secondary** | An **existing** interest — a case, a portfolio, or an LP fund interest | A secondary buyer | Seller wants liquidity, or a manager wants a realisation to show |
| **Securitisation** | Cash flows from a **pool**, repackaged as notes | Capital-markets investors | Cheaper funding, ratings, access to investors who cannot hold the raw asset |

**Syndication** is the only one the syllabus supplies a document for, and it is a handout —
the model participation agreement distributed in Class 5. Expect the usual participation
questions: true sale or sub-participation, who controls waivers and enforcement, voting on
settlement, and what the lead owes the participants.

**Secondaries.** The defining problem is that a buyer is usually being sold the **tail**.
Early-resolving cases leave first, so what remains is long-duration and more volatile —
"enhanced volatility (more losses) as compared to a whole portfolio." Hence "[i]nvesting in
secondaries requires a discount to market value." And a secondary **is not a realisation**: it
is one buyer's point-in-time estimate, not an outcome.

**Securitisation** is the least developed, and the obstacles are structural rather than legal:
binary outcomes resist the law of large numbers at realistic pool sizes; duration is long and
unscheduled while noteholders want amortisation; the collateral is confidential and
privileged, so diligence by an arranger, a rating agency and investors widens the disclosure
circle enormously; and in a champerty jurisdiction every noteholder is another stranger
sharing in proceeds.

## Why it exists

Primary capital is finite and patient capital is expensive. A funder that can syndicate writes
larger deals than its balance sheet allows; one that can sell secondaries returns capital to
LPs before cases resolve; one that can securitise replaces equity with cheaper debt.

The deeper reason is **duration**. Cases run to thirty months at the multiple-maximising point
and far longer in the tail, against fund lives that do not stretch. Every instrument here
answers the mismatch between how long litigation takes and how long investors will wait.

## Variants

- **True sale versus sub-participation** in syndications — determines whether the participant
  has a direct claim.
- **LP-led versus manager-led secondaries** — the motive differs, and the motive is the first
  diligence question.
- **Single-asset versus portfolio secondaries.**
- **Insurance-wrapped pools**, where [[Litigation risk insurance]] supplies the credit
  enhancement a rating would otherwise require.

## What can go wrong

- **Adverse selection by arithmetic.** Winners resolve first, so any seasoned pool is worse
  than the portfolio it came from. No bad faith required. This poisons secondaries and any
  securitisation of a seasoned book alike.
- **Marks are not prices.** A manager's valuation "is simply a point-in-time estimate of value
  by the new, prospective owner that makes a series of assumptions."
- **Manufactured track records.** A manager raising a follow-on fund has a reason to create
  arm's-length transactions that resemble exits, and nobody outside can audit it because the
  underlying is confidential.
- **Collection risk survives the win.** "Just because you win a case, doesn't mean you get to
  collect the spoils."
- **No valuation infrastructure.** Pricing is "perhaps more art than science," and of expert
  valuers "few exist in the world."
- **Privilege leakage.** Every additional holder is another party to whom case information
  flows.
