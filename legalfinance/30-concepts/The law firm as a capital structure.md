## What it is

A US law firm is a partnership whose owners can withdraw at will, whose capital cannot be
locked in, and whose only assets are people and client relationships. That combination — not
the quality of its lawyers or the state of its balance sheet — is what determines how it
raises money and how it fails.

## How it works

- **Partners own it and take distributions, not salary.** Compensation moves dollar for
  dollar with firm performance, so every departure mechanically reduces what remains for
  those who stay.
- **Capital cannot be locked in.** ABA Model Rule 5.6 bars restrictions on a departing
  lawyer's right to practise, so non-competes and meaningful capital retention are
  unavailable. Departing partners must be repaid.
- **No outside equity.** Model Rule 5.4 bars nonlawyer ownership and fee sharing in every US
  jurisdiction but Arizona and, in limited form, Utah. So the only permanent capital is
  partner capital, which is the capital that can walk.
- **No hard assets.** Nothing to pledge, nothing to liquidate, and — Morley's point —
  that makes liquidation *less* attractive to creditors, not more.
- **Three time-accruing liabilities punish staying**: fraudulent transfer liability for
  compensation taken before insolvency, preferential transfer liability for capital repaid,
  and unfinished-business liability on matters completed after departure.

The consequence is the **partner run**: departures lower the payoff to staying, and the
liabilities punish leaving late, so the rational move is to leave early and everyone
reasoning that way makes it true. "No large law firm has ever managed to reorganize its
debts in bankruptcy and survive."

## Why it exists

Not economics — **regulation**. Model Rules 5.4 and 5.6 were written to protect a lawyer's
independent professional judgment and a client's choice of counsel. Their combined effect is
to forbid the two things that make a business durable: outside permanent capital, and
restraints on the owners walking out. Morley notes Rule 5.6 "is unique to the United
States," and firms in the UK and Australia can restrict partner departure.

This is why the whole of Class 7 exists. Alternative business structures, MSOs and outside
investment are all attempts to route around rules the profession wrote for itself, and
litigation finance is the workaround that does not require changing them at all.

## Variants

- **Investor-owned firms** — Slater & Gordon in Australia survived insolvency because
  salaried lawyers have no distribution to watch shrink.
- **Accounting firms** — structurally similar but "not quite as vulnerable," because they
  can restrict partner departure.
- **Arizona ABS entities** — nonlawyer ownership permitted since 2020.
- **MSO structures** — a nonlawyer-owned management company contracting with a lawyer-owned
  firm. Class 7.
- **Portfolio finance** — outside capital without ownership, which is the point.

## What can go wrong

- **The collateral can resign.** A funder lending against a firm's contingency book is
  secured on work performed by partners who may leave, and whose incentive to leave rises
  precisely when the firm weakens. This is the credit risk that sets law firm facility
  pricing, and no Class 1 reading mentions it.
- **Debt plus a run is fatal.** Leverage raises the payoff to leaving early, so borrowing
  makes the run mechanism faster — the dynamic [[Mass tort financing and claim aggregation]]
  describes in mass tort firms.
- **Unfinished-business claims** follow departing partners, which the funder may inherit as
  a competing claim on the same fees.
