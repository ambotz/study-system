## What it is

**Rule 5.4(a)**: "A lawyer or law firm shall not share legal fees with a nonlawyer." Together
with Rule 5.4(d)'s bar on nonlawyer ownership, it is the rule that keeps outside capital out
of law firms — and the rule every structure in Classes 4, 5 and 7 is drafted around.

Its stated purpose is narrow: to "remove incentives for nonlawyers to interfere with
professional judgment."

## How it works

The operative line is **who owes the money and out of what**, per
[[NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2018-5]]:

| Arrangement | Rule 5.4(a) | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Client** borrows, repays from **recovery** | Permitted | The lawyer is not a party; the fee is unaffected |
| **Lawyer** borrows, repays contingent on **fees in specified matters** | **Prohibited** | Repayment varies with legal fees — a share by another name, whatever the instrument is called |
| **Lawyer** borrows on **recourse** at a fixed rate | Permitted | The obligation is absolute, not tied to any fee |

- **Form does not save it.** Styling the arrangement a non-recourse loan rather than a fee
  split describes the same economics.
- **The rule is prophylactic.** It forbids the structure because of the incentive it creates,
  not on proof that anyone interfered.
- **There is no funder carve-out**, and the NYC Bar declined to propose one: "That is a matter
  to be decided by the state judiciary."
- **The scope limit matters and is often overstated.** The prohibition attaches where
  repayment is contingent on fees "in one or more **specific matters**." A facility secured on
  a firm's **general revenues** is a different case that the opinion does not decide.

Read alongside **Model Rule 1.8(e)** — a lawyer shall not provide financial assistance to a
client in connection with pending or contemplated litigation, subject to the court-costs and
indigent-client exceptions. Together, 5.4 and 1.8(e) explain the market's basic shape: the
lawyer may not fund the client, and the funder may not share the lawyer's fee, so the capital
goes to the **client** and the lawyer stays out of the transaction.

## Why it exists

Independent professional judgment. A nonlawyer with a stake in a specific matter's fee has a
reason to influence how that matter is run, and the profession's answer is to forbid the
stake rather than to police the influence.

Whether that is what the rule actually achieves is the live question. A client-side funding
deal gives a nonlawyer exactly the same economic interest in the outcome of exactly the same
lawsuit; the difference is that the lawyer is not in privity. So the rule may be drawing a
line about **privity** rather than about **incentives** — which is the strongest criticism of
it and the thread that runs to Class 7.

## Variants

- **Client-side funding** — outside the rule entirely, which is why the commercial market is
  shaped the way it is.
- **Recourse lending to firms** at a fixed rate — permitted, and the post-2018 New York
  workaround.
- **Revenue-based facilities** secured on a firm's general receipts rather than identified
  matters — the space the opinion leaves open.
- **Alternative business structures** — Arizona abolished Rule 5.4 in 2020; Utah runs a
  sandbox; the UK has permitted ABS since the Legal Services Act 2007.
- **MSO structures** — a nonlawyer-owned management company charging the firm for services
  rather than taking a share of legal fees. Class 7, with Texas Ethics Opinion 706 on whether
  the fee arrangement survives the rule.

## What can go wrong

- **Drafting to the letter and not the logic.** Every workaround gives a nonlawyer economics
  that move with the firm's fee income; the question each time is whether the link is close
  enough to a "share."
- **Ignoring it in a funding structure that touches the lawyer.** Fast Trak's secondary
  contracts took a contingent interest in an attorney's fees across five to ten unrelated
  cases — squarely what 2018-5 forbids — and the case was litigated on **usury** without
  anyone reaching Rule 5.4.
- **Assuming a bar opinion binds.** It is persuasive, jurisdiction-specific, and not a court
  ruling. It still shapes how New York deals are papered.
- **Forgetting Rule 5.6.** The fee-sharing bar limits capital coming in; the bar on restraints
  on departing lawyers means the capital already inside can walk. See
  [[The law firm as a capital structure]].
