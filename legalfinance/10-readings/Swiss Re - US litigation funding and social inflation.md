## Claim

- Third-party litigation funding is a material driver of **social inflation** — "the
  social factors that drive increases in legal claims above and beyond economic trends."
- Funding does not solve an access-to-justice problem; it worsens one. Claimants in funded
  cases receive a **smaller** share of the proceeds than claimants in unfunded ones.
- The result is "an opaque, bottom-up wealth transfer from consumers to sophisticated
  investors and law firms," because the liability costs end up in insurance premiums.
- The answer is regulation: mandatory disclosure, transparent pricing, usury caps on
  consumer funding, consolidated ethics rules, and public alternatives — legal aid and
  legal expense insurance.

## Argument

- **Establish the growth.** Global TPLF investment hit USD 17bn in 2021, up 16% year on
  year, with the US at 52% of it and a projected USD 31bn by 2028 at an 8.7% CAGR.
- **Establish the returns.** Funder IRRs run far above conventional asset classes, which
  makes the capital inflow self-reinforcing and means the money must come from somewhere.
- **Establish the verdict trend.** Large awards are getting larger and more frequent
  across general liability, vehicle negligence and trucking.
- **Connect the two by mechanism, not by proof.** Four channels are asserted: funded cases
  run longer; funding pays for attorney advertising, expanded discovery and mock trials;
  longer cases with compounding finance cost more; and the funder's economics reward
  pushing for a larger award rather than an early resolution.
- **Reframe the claimant's position.** If more of the pot goes to lawyers and funders, the
  access-to-justice defence collapses on its own terms.
- **Then prescribe.** Disclosure to expose the arrangements, usury caps to price-protect
  consumers, ethics consolidation to discipline lawyers, and legal aid plus legal expense
  insurance as the cheaper route to the same access goal.

## Evidence

- **The headline claimant figures.** Without funding, plaintiffs receive **55%** of costs
  and compensation; with funding, about **43%** — "TPLF reduces plaintiff compensation by
  more than a fifth (21%)" (p. 16). Executive summary version: "up to 57% of legal costs
  and compensation go to lawyers, funders and others, compared with an average of 45% in
  typical tort liability cases" (p. 2). **These are estimates, and the report says so.**
  Check the underlying construction before quoting the 21% as a finding.
- **Returns.** Average IRR by segment — personal injury 32.7% (2019), 24.6% (2020), 35.3%
  (2021F); commercial 29.5% / 26.5% / 29.9%; mass tort 21.2% / 25.5% / 26.0%. Benchmarked
  against 15-year returns of 13% private equity, 13% venture capital, 10% S&P 500 (p. 17).
  Sourced to Morning Investments, an industry data provider — not audited fund returns.
- **Verdicts.** Awards over USD 5m rose from 29% to 37% of large general liability awards
  (2010–2019); vehicle negligence 22% to 29%; median general liability award USD 8.2m to
  USD 10.3m; trucking verdicts from USD 2.3m to USD 22.3m (2010–2018), roughly a tenfold
  increase.
- **Duration.** Funded personal injury cases averaged 37 months (2019) and 43 months
  (2020), against 28.6 months for typical civil cases (p. 18).
- **Insurer impact.** 2020 combined ratios — general liability 105.7%, medical malpractice
  117.5%, commercial auto 104.1%. Umbrella rates up 22.6% in 2020, general liability up
  7.3%.
- **The causal gap is acknowledged.** What the report actually offers is temporal
  correlation ("As TPLF has grown, so has the number of large motor and general liability
  verdicts," p. 13), a geographic correlation between plaintiff-friendly counties and low
  income, high unemployment and high inequality (p. 15), and a portfolio correlation
  between award size and duration (p. 18). It concedes that funding interacts with
  "psychology-based strategies of the trial bar," changing juror attitudes, and attorney
  advertising (pp. 14–15).
- **The duration finding has an obvious alternative reading.** Funders select cases they
  expect to be large and hard-fought. Longer duration may be **selection**, not effect —
  the report does not control for it.

## Who is speaking

Swiss Re is one of the world's largest reinsurers. Every dollar of a nuclear verdict
against a primary insurer reaches Swiss Re's book, and the combined ratios on page after
page are its own industry's. This is a report by the party that pays.

That is not a reason to discount the figures, which are more specific than anything else
on the Class 1 list. It is a reason to notice what the report does not cost out: the
defence-side spend, the value of claims never brought, and whether a 105.7% combined ratio
reflects claim inflation, a soft pricing cycle, or reserve releases from earlier years. The
policy menu also runs in a consistent direction — every recommendation either raises the
funder's cost of doing business or substitutes a public or insurance-market product for
private capital. Legal expense insurance, recommended at pages 25–26, is a product the
insurance industry sells.

## Seminar hooks

- The 43%/55% comparison is the most quoted number in the funding debate and it is an
  **estimate built by the reinsurer with the most to gain from it**. The productive
  question is not whether it is true but what a defensible version would require: matched
  cases, funded and unfunded, with the same injuries in the same jurisdictions. Nobody has
  that, which is exactly what GAO found.
- The duration finding is the strongest causal claim in the report and the most vulnerable.
  Funded cases last longer — but funders select for cases that are large, contested and
  slow. Separating selection from treatment is the whole empirical problem in this field,
  and this report does not attempt it.
- Two of the four recommendations expand markets that insurers serve. Legal expense
  insurance is sold by insurers; legal aid shifts the cost to taxpayers. Ask what a
  funder's report would recommend if it had the same evidentiary base — and then notice
  that Burford's own guide is on the same week's reading list.
