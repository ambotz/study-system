## Claim

- The same structure as the Holland & Knight piece, told from the **buy side**: what the
  investor actually purchases, how partners are paid, and how the investor gets out.
- **What is bought:** the firm's **non-legal assets and services**, moved into a separate
  entity. Legal work stays in the law firm.
- **How the MSO is paid:** fees "most often structured as 'per lawyer' fees or 'usage' fees,"
  ranging from flat fees to rates varying by practice area and seniority. Permitted services
  include "accounting, billing, marketing support, intake and information technology."
- **How partners are paid:** "cash at closing and purchase-price payments" plus **rollover
  equity in the MSO entity** in exchange for the non-legal assets. Post-closing compensation
  often **decreases** initially, with the expectation that "lawyers will receive increased
  compensation over time."
- **Exit:** typically **three to five years**, with paths defined in the organisational
  documents and mechanisms to keep partners "properly incentivised" after the investor leaves.
- **Constraints flagged:** "'fee-splitting' or 'fee-sharing' by law firms with non-lawyer
  owned entities is prohibited," and management services agreements must not be so
  "burdensome" that they operate as "quasi-non-competes or restrictions on the practice of
  law" — which would run into Rule 5.6.

## Who is speaking

A law firm writing for **investors** considering buying into law firm MSOs. The register is
private equity, not professional responsibility: assets, rollover, incentives, exit.

Neither this piece nor Holland & Knight's gives a valuation multiple, which is conspicuous in
an investor-facing overview and probably reflects how few completed transactions exist.

## Seminar hooks

- **Put the two client alerts side by side — the same structure, two audiences.** Holland &
  Knight leads with ethics and calls it a "strategic partnership"; Hunton leads with rollover
  equity and a three-to-five-year exit. The second is the one that tells you what the deal
  actually is, and it is a **private equity rollup** of the non-legal half of a law firm.
- **"Partners take a pay cut at closing" is the fact to have.** Compensation "often decreases"
  post-closing, in exchange for cash and rollover equity that pays off at exit. That converts
  a partnership's current income into a capital gain contingent on a sale in three to five
  years — and it gives partners a powerful reason to stay until then, which is the exact
  problem [[Morley - Why Law Firms Collapse]] says partner ownership cannot solve. **An MSO
  may be the first structure that actually locks lawyers in.**
- **The quasi-non-compete warning is the tell.** Hunton warns that an MSA must not be so
  burdensome as to restrict the practice of law — because that is Rule 5.6, the other half of
  Morley's fragility. If the investor's return depends on retention, and retention cannot be
  contracted for, the deal has an unhedgeable hole in it. Ask how an investor underwrites a
  business whose assets can resign.
