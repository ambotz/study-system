## Claim

- Repeal the prohibition on nonlawyer investment. Let firms incorporate, take nonlawyer
  partners, and access the equity capital markets: "it makes little sense to prevent lawyers
  from using the financial tools that virtually every other business has available to it."
- **Rule 5.4 is redundant on its own terms.** Every harm it is meant to prevent is already
  addressed by confidentiality rules, conflicts doctrine, supervisory duties under Rule 5.3,
  and fiduciary obligations. A categorical ban adds nothing but cost.
- Independent judgment can be protected by **contract, fiduciary duty, incentive design and
  share-class structure** rather than by prohibition.
- The benefits "are far outweighed" the other way round — the concerns "are far outweighed by
  the substantial benefits."

## Argument

- **The capital problem, stated four ways:**
  - **No external equity.** Partnerships run on partner contributions and retained earnings;
    "Model Rule 5.4 prohibits selling limited partner interests to nonlawyers."
  - **Human capital is unfunded.** Firms must pay for associate training and firm-specific
    development, and "increasingly cannot pass these costs to clients under fixed-fee
    arrangements."
  - **Contingency work is a working-capital business.** "Plaintiffs' firms taking contingency
    cases require substantial capital for years before recovery." That sentence, written in
    1998, is the entire demand case for litigation finance before the industry existed.
  - **Agency costs** exist under partner ownership too, so the choice is between two imperfect
    structures rather than between purity and corruption.
- **The safeguards they propose**, which is the constructive half and the part later reform
  proposals reuse:
  - **Contractual protection** — written undertakings of "no interference with the lawyer's
    independence of professional judgment or with the client-lawyer relationship," echoing the
    Kutak Commission.
  - **Fiduciary duties** in the corporate statute, enforceable in both corporate and
    professional conduct forums.
  - **Incentive alignment** — "[t]ying profitability…to vehicles such as incentive stock
    options would give additional impetus to lawyers to remain faithful to both clients and
    investors."
  - **Voting structure** — a "separate, voting dominant class of insider stock for senior
    practicing lawyers," so economic ownership and control can be split.
  - **Existing rules still bind.** "Lawyers will continue to be the persons actually providing
    legal services…Practicing in a law firm with nonlawyer investors will no more release
    attorneys from their ethical obligations than practicing as in-house counsel."
- **The objections, answered in order:**
  - ***Fear of Sears*** — corporate domination of the profession. Answered by conflicts rules,
    which limit firm size in practice: Westinghouse "disqualified Kirkland & Ellis upon
    discovery of a conflict between clients represented by the Chicago office and the
    Washington, D.C. office."
  - **Independence** — the market protects it. "Stockholders would be acting to their own
    detriment by interfering with professional independence…thereby diminishing quality of
    legal services offered." The medical analogy: doctors keep professional obligations under
    administrator cost pressure.
  - **Confidentiality** — "[l]aw firms have not been plagued by nonlawyers breaking client
    confidences," despite paralegals and consultants; Rule 5.3 and confidentiality agreements
    handle it.
  - **Unauthorised practice** — nonlawyers already work inside firms. "If these employees…can
    coexist within the bounds of ethics rules, nonlawyer investors and partners can as well."
  - **"It would make law a business"** — it already is. "Over the last ten years, the legal
    'profession' has undergone changes that arguably warrant renaming it the legal services
    'business'."

## Evidence

- **Normative and doctrinal, with comparative gestures.** No data on firm capital structures,
  no study of jurisdictions permitting outside ownership — because in 1998 there were none to
  study. The argument is entirely *ex ante*.
- **That is now its greatest weakness and its greatest strength.** Twenty-eight years later
  the natural experiments exist — the UK since the Legal Services Act 2007, Australia with
  listed firms, **Arizona since 2020**, Utah's sandbox — and **nobody on this syllabus has
  evaluated any of them.** Reading a 1998 prediction against 2026 evidence that has not been
  gathered is a very clean paper structure.
- **The market-discipline argument is the weakest link.** "Stockholders would be acting to
  their own detriment by interfering" assumes shareholders internalise quality effects they
  may not see and may not hold long enough to bear. The same argument was made about every
  professional services rollup.
- **The conflicts-limit-size argument has not aged well.** Kirkland & Ellis had roughly 700
  lawyers when this was written; it now has thousands and conflicts rules did not stop that.
  If conflicts were the natural ceiling on firm size, the ceiling has moved a great deal.
- **They did not anticipate the actual outcome.** Rule 5.4 was never repealed. Capital reached
  firms anyway — through litigation finance, then through **MSO structures**. The rule held
  and the economics arrived regardless, which is a third possibility the article does not
  consider.

## Who is speaking

Edward Adams and John Matheson, both then at Minnesota, writing in the *California Law
Review*. Corporate and securities academics rather than legal ethicists — which shapes the
whole piece: the framing is capital structure and agency cost, and professional
responsibility appears as a constraint to be satisfied rather than as a value to be weighed.

Neither has a stake in the market. Written in 1998, before litigation finance existed in the
US as an industry, so nothing here is a response to funders or written with them in mind.
That independence is worth something: the case for outside capital was made on its own terms
a decade before anyone was selling it.

## Seminar hooks

- **One sentence from 1998 is the demand case for the entire industry**: "Plaintiffs' firms
  taking contingency cases require substantial capital for years before recovery." Written
  before US litigation finance existed. The market that grew up answered a problem the
  academy had already diagnosed — and answered it **without** the rule change they asked for,
  which is the interesting part.
- **Their safeguards are being built right now, in the wrong entity.** Contractual
  non-interference undertakings, separated control rights, existing ethics rules binding the
  lawyers — that is precisely the **MSO** structure in
  [[Holland and Knight - Why law firms should pay attention to MSO partnerships]]. Private
  ordering delivered the 1998 proposal's protections while leaving the 1998 proposal's
  prohibition intact. Ask whether that is a vindication or an evasion.
- **Twenty-eight years of evidence exists and nobody has looked.** The UK since 2007,
  Australia's listed firms, Arizona since 2020, Utah's sandbox. Adams and Matheson made
  falsifiable predictions about independence, confidentiality and quality. **No reading on
  this syllabus tests any of them.** That is the cleanest unclaimed paper topic in the course,
  and Class 7 is where to say so.
