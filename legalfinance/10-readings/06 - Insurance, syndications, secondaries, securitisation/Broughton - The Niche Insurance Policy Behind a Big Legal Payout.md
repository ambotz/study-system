## Claim

- **Appian** won a **$2 billion** trade-secrets judgment against **Pegasystems** and then
  bought **judgment preservation insurance** — a policy protecting the award from being
  reduced or cancelled on appeal.
- Coverage: a floor of at least **$500 million** of the $2bn. Premium: **$57.3 million**,
  about **9.8%** of the limit including taxes and fees.
- The commercial point is not the litigation. Insuring the floor converted a contingent,
  appealable judgment into something close to a balance-sheet asset, and **moved Appian's
  market capitalisation**.
- It also worked: the Virginia Court of Appeals later overturned the judgment, which is
  exactly the risk the policy was bought against.

## Seminar hooks

- **Run the economics.** $57.3m of premium for a $500m floor is **9.8%** — and that is
  roughly a tenth of what a funder charges for comparable downside protection, because the
  insurer is pricing appellate reversal risk on an adjudicated judgment rather than
  underwriting an unresolved case. That is the whole argument for why insurance is eating
  parts of this market, in one comparison.
- **The product only exists after a win.** Judgment preservation insurance attaches to a
  judgment, not a claim. So it does not compete with funding at origination — it competes at
  the back end, where the risk is narrower and cheaper. Ask what is left for funders once
  insurers take every stage where the risk can be actuarially priced.
- **The policy paid off, and that is the uncomfortable part.** The judgment was later
  overturned on appeal. An insurer wrote a $500m floor at 9.8% on a $2bn judgment that did
  not survive — which is either a mispricing, a reason premiums have since risen, or evidence
  that appellate outcomes are genuinely hard to underwrite. Worth asking whether the market
  repriced after this.
