## Claim

- A secondary is "any transaction where one party is acquiring the interests from the
  original investor (the 'primary' investor)" — a single case, a portfolio, or an LP fund
  interest.
- **What a secondary buyer is usually being offered is the tail**, not a cross-section: "when
  an investor is provided with a secondary opportunity, they are quite likely looking at
  investing in the 'tail' of the portfolio because the early part of the portfolio has
  already been resolved."
- The tail is the worst part of the distribution — long-duration, complex, procedurally stuck
  cases with "enhanced volatility (more losses) as compared to a whole portfolio."
- Therefore **secondaries require a discount**: "Investing in secondaries requires a discount
  to market value to offset the implied volatility associated with the tail."
- And the sharpest point in the piece: **a secondary is not a realisation.** "But is a
  secondary a 'realization'? The short answer is NO!"

## Argument

- **Why secondaries exist here, and the two motivations differ in a way that matters.**
  - **Manager-side.** A manager raising a larger fund needs realisations to demonstrate
    stewardship. Without enough resolved cases, selling secondary interests manufactures
    "validation through arm's-length transactions." That is a marketing motive dressed as a
    liquidity event.
  - **Investor-side.** Liquidity pressure, frustration with duration, management change, or a
    strategy shift. But these sellers "currently face a difficult task in finding a secondary
    investor to acquire their interests," because managers have no economic incentive to help
    an LP exit.
- **The duration distribution is the analytical core.** Early-resolving cases produce
  attractive **IRRs** but low **multiples** — they return capital fast but do not make much.
  Cases around **thirty months** produce the strongest multiples. What remains after both are
  gone is the tail: cases outside the normal time distribution because of complexity,
  procedural delay, or arbitration.
- **So the seller's remaining pool is adversely selected by construction.** No bad faith is
  required. Whatever resolves early resolves early, and the buyer gets what is left.
- **Diligence must be case-level and expert.** Each significant position needs extensive
  review, particularly of merits. Do **not** take comfort from the original manager's
  mark-to-market: valuation "is simply a point-in-time estimate of value by the new,
  prospective owner that makes a series of assumptions."
- **Collection risk is separate from merits risk.** "Just because you win a case, doesn't
  mean you get to collect the spoils." A case is not resolved until cash reaches investors —
  not a lawyer's trust account.
- **Diversify.** "Make sure your secondaries are diversified or part of a larger diversified
  pool of assets."

## Evidence

- **Practitioner analysis, not research.** No transaction data, no discount ranges, no
  realised secondary returns. The tail argument is structural reasoning about duration
  distributions, and it is convincing on its own terms without being measured.
- **No figures at all**, which is itself informative: a market with no public pricing has no
  benchmark for what a secondary discount should be.
- **"Perhaps more art than science."** Truant notes that expert valuers of these assets
  barely exist — "few exist in the world." Compare private equity secondaries, where pricing
  as a percentage of NAV is published quarterly.
- **The manager-motivation point is an allegation about incentives**, stated without
  examples. It is plausible and important, and it is unsupported.
- **Written in 2022**, at the top of the capital cycle. Westfleet then recorded commitments
  falling 16% in 2024 to a market "nearly 30% smaller than levels reached in 2022," with
  funders "ration[ing] their limited capital." A liquidity squeeze is exactly when secondary
  supply rises and prices fall, so the 2022 framing probably understates how buyer-favourable
  this market has since become.

## Who is speaking

Ed Truant runs Slingshot Capital and writes the *Investor Insights* column carried by Legal
Funding Journal. He is an investor and adviser in this asset class — a **buyer's-side**
voice, and the whole piece reads that way: be sceptical of the manager's marks, understand
the seller's motives, demand a discount, diversify.

That orientation is what makes it useful. Almost every other industry voice on this syllabus
is selling capital to claimants; Truant is talking to the people who supply capital to the
funders. It is the only reading that takes the **limited partner's** view, and it is candid
about manager incentives in a way a manager's own material never is.

## Seminar hooks

- **This piece silently settles a Class 1 dispute.** Swiss Re quotes funder **IRRs** of
  24.6–35.3%; GAO reports funders self-reporting **93% ROIC since inception**. Truant
  explains why those cannot be compared: early resolutions produce high IRR and low multiple,
  long ones the reverse. **A funder can choose which number flatters it by choosing which
  cases it talks about.** Nobody in Class 1 had the vocabulary to say that.
- **Adverse selection here needs no villain.** The early winners resolve first, so the
  residual pool is worse than the original portfolio by arithmetic alone. That is a general
  point about any amortising alternative asset, and it is the single most useful idea to take
  into Class 6 — it applies equally to the syndication participations and to any
  securitisation built on a seasoned pool.
- **"A secondary is not a realisation."** Managers raising a follow-on fund have a reason to
  create arm's-length transactions that look like exits. Ask what a track record actually
  measures in an asset class where the manager can manufacture its own comparable — and note
  that nobody outside the fund can audit it, because the underlying is confidential.
