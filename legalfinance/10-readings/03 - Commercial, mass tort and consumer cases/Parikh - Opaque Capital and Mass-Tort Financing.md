## Claim

- A new kind of financier has entered mass torts — **"aggressive hedge funds and
  private-equity firms that have established a litigation-finance group"** — and it does not
  behave like the passive investor Burford describes. It "dictate[s] outcomes."
- The money does not fund cases. It funds **claim acquisition**: credit lines to plaintiff
  firms, spent on advertising and lead generation, which convert into claim volume, which
  converts into settlement pressure, which supports more borrowing.
- The result is what Parikh calls the **alchemist's inversion** — weak and in some instances
  fabricated claims marshalled deliberately, because volume rather than merit drives
  defendants to settle.
- The victims are the real claimants: "every dollar received by a false victim is one less
  dollar received by a meritorious one."
- Reform requires disclosure, regulation of claims marshalling, filing fees, better claim
  verification, and **fiduciary duties on financiers**.

## Argument

- **The structure is firm-level lending, not case funding.** Centerbridge extended a "$100
  million line of credit" to Mikal Watts's firm in the PG&E bankruptcy. That is a credit
  facility against a book, and it is the [[Portfolio finance and cross-collateralisation]]
  structure applied where the claimants are individuals who never see the deal.
- **The proceeds buy claims.** In Camp Lejeune, "more than $145 million had been spent…by
  year's end" on television and social media advertising, with projections to double. Lead
  generators and aggregators work by "direct calling," "television advertising,"
  "infomercials," and "online videos."
- **A broker states the model plainly**: "(i) borrow as much money as possible; (ii) buy as
  many television ads…as possible; (iii) wait on real lawyers…to establish liability…then
  use those…clients to borrow even more money…and (vii) lather, rinse, and repeat." Quoting
  a market participant describing a debt-financed flywheel is the most damaging evidence in
  the piece.
- **Volume is the product.** Defendants "feel more 'pressure' to settle when up against a
  lawyer with a large 'volume of cases'," so the financial incentive attaches to count
  rather than to merit.
- **The pelvic mesh example is the sharpest allegation**: financiers called mesh recipients,
  told them removal was medically urgent when often it was not, funded the surgeries, and
  connected them to attorneys — manufacturing claims that "few needed."
- **Individual-level control provisions.** Capital-Provision Agreements with individual
  plaintiffs allow financiers to veto settlements and restrict changes of attorney, as in
  *Boling v. Prospect Funding Holdings*. And **Sysco** is his corporate example: a Fortune
  100 company "rendered helpless" when Burford invoked a settlement veto.
- **The crossholder's luxury.** A financier's affiliate takes "a significant short position
  on BigCo stock" while its litigation arm funds suits against BigCo and "strategically
  releases information" to move the short. A loss on the case is offset elsewhere, so the
  financier's interest is not the claimant's even in direction.
- **The forum consequence.** Defendants facing inflated volumes "balk at settlement,"
  pushing cases "from state court to federal court, from MDL to bankruptcy, trying to find a
  venue they believe will see through the fog."

## Evidence

- **Anecdotal and documentary, not systematic.** Named transactions, named litigations, a
  quoted broker, two reported cases. There is no dataset, no base rate, and no measure of
  how much of mass tort financing looks like this.
- **The pelvic mesh allegation is the most serious and the least substantiated** in the
  essay's own terms. It is presented as reported conduct rather than as a finding of fact;
  check the underlying sources before repeating it as established.
- **The advertising figures are real and verifiable** — Camp Lejeune ad spend was widely
  reported — but advertising spend does not by itself establish that the claims generated
  are meritless.
- **The crossholder scenario is explicitly hypothetical.** It is a described mechanism, not
  a documented instance, and should be cited as such.
- **The strongest structural point survives all of this**: mass tort financing operates at
  the **firm** level against a book of claims, so no individual claim has to justify itself,
  and the claimant is not party to the financing. That is a description of the architecture,
  not an allegation, and it is why [[Commercial versus consumer funding]] leaves a gap here.
- Directly contradicted on the screening question by
  [[Avraham and Sebok - Empirical Investigation of Third Party Consumer Litigant Funding]]
  (48% approval rate, applications a median 308 days post-injury) and by
  [[Antill and Grenadier - Financing the Litigation Arms Race]] (a non-recourse funder
  screens harder, not looser). Note that both of those study **consumer and single-case**
  funding, and Parikh is describing **firm-level lending**, where the screening incentive
  genuinely is weaker. The three may all be right about different products.

## Who is speaking

Samir Parikh is a bankruptcy and mass torts academic who writes on aggregate litigation and
the use of bankruptcy to resolve it. He is not aligned with the defence bar or the funders,
and his concern throughout is the **claimant** — the argument is that opaque capital hurts
real victims, not that defendants are unfairly burdened.

That framing is what makes the essay useful in this course: it is an attack on funding from
the plaintiff's side, which neither Swiss Re nor Burford can make. Published in the *Yale
Law Journal Forum* — a short-form online companion, edited but not the main volume, so
treat it as a serious essay rather than a fully worked empirical study.

## Seminar hooks

- Parikh and Burford describe two different industries and both are accurate. Burford
  underwrites single commercial claims at a $3m floor with a passive posture; the capital
  Parikh describes lends to plaintiff firms against a book and takes veto rights over
  individual settlements. **The word "funder" is doing too much work on this syllabus**, and
  saying so is the highest-value contribution available this week.
- The screening evidence splits by product, not by ideology. Avraham and Sebok show funders
  rejecting 52% of consumer applications; Antill and Grenadier show why a non-recourse
  funder must screen. Both study products where the funder is exposed to the **individual
  claim**. Parikh describes lending against a **firm**, where the lender is repaid from the
  book and no single claim needs to be good. That distinction reconciles all three papers
  and nobody in the readings draws it.
- His reform list converges almost exactly on Steinitz's — disclosure, fiduciary duties —
  from the opposite starting point. Two authors with different concerns arriving at the same
  instrument is the strongest signal on the syllabus that fiduciary duties are the live
  reform, and it is not the one Congress is debating.
