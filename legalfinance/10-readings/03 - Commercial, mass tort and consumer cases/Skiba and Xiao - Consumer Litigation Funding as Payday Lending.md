## Claim

- The payday-lending analogy is the central move in the case for regulating consumer
  litigation funding, and it **half works**.
- **Where it holds**: the prices are comparable in magnitude (payday 260–520% APR, consumer
  funding 200%+ APR), and the borrowers look alike — cash-constrained people spending the
  money on ordinary living costs.
- **Where it breaks**: consumer funding is **non-recourse**, so "the financier cannot obtain
  repayment outside of the case." That removes the debt trap, which is the principal
  justification for payday-lending bans. Funding "cannot cause borrowers to fall into a debt
  cycle directly."
- The real harm is therefore **different from payday lending's harm**, and needs a different
  remedy: not a ban, not a rate cap, but **disclosure of a plain repayment amount and date**
  plus attorney oversight.

## Argument

- **Run the comparison dimension by dimension** rather than by slogan.
  - **Price** — comparable order of magnitude, both extreme by consumer credit standards.
  - **Use of funds** — payday borrowers spend 68% on living expenses; consumer funding
    recipients 51%. Table 1 breaks funding use down to regular expenses 38–44%,
    mortgage or rent 7–12%, food 1–2%, unexpected expenses 2%, gifts and entertainment 1%.
    This is subsistence borrowing in both markets.
  - **Rollover and debt cycle** — payday's defining pathology: 80% renewed within fourteen
    days. Consumer funding has no analogue, because the obligation terminates when the case
    does and is extinguished entirely if the plaintiff loses.
  - **Recourse** — the decisive difference, and it is structural rather than contractual.
- **Identify the harm that survives.** If the debt trap is gone, what is left is a
  **valuation problem**: "funding's tie to litigation complicates the repayment
  calculus…A plaintiff must accurately predict the size and date of his settlement." A
  payday borrower knows the amount and the date; a funded plaintiff can know neither.
- **The behavioural layer makes it worse.** Litigation obscures the cash-flow consequence
  through "salience, differential mental accounting, and lack of the pain of payment." The
  money is repaid out of a settlement the plaintiff has never held, so it does not feel like
  a payment at all.
- **Reject the two standard remedies.**
  - **Bans** — premature, because "the legal finance industry has not reached a competitive
    equilibrium, and little empirical evidence currently exists as to the consumer welfare
    effects of funding."
  - **Characteristic restrictions** (caps on rates, fees, or terms) — they "hurt low-income
    borrowers and are easily evaded."
- **Propose instead**: financiers "should disclose a reasonable approximate repayment amount
  and date" rather than an APR schedule, because consumers lack "financial or legal
  expertise to evaluate potential lawsuit outcomes"; **attorney acknowledgment provisions**
  requiring written confirmation that costs were disclosed; and states should "partner with
  financiers to gather data on customer characteristics and outcomes."

## Evidence

- **Usage data from a national financier, N = 37,799, covering 2014–2015.** Smaller and
  shorter than the Avraham and Sebok dataset, and focused on **what the money was spent on**
  rather than on pricing and outcomes. The two studies are complements, not rivals.
- **The APR comparison is the weakest link and the authors know it.** Applying an annualised
  rate to a non-recourse advance of unknown duration imports a precision the instrument does
  not have — the same objection the industry makes against APR disclosure, arrived at
  independently.
- The behavioural claims (salience, mental accounting, pain of payment) are **imported from
  the general behavioural finance literature**, not measured in this population.
- **The case against rate caps is asserted rather than demonstrated.** That caps hurt
  low-income borrowers and are easily evaded is the standard consumer-credit finding; no
  evidence specific to litigation funding is offered, and the states that have capped rates
  had not yet produced evaluable experience in 2017.
- **The paper predates almost everything.** Written in 2017, it does not see Avraham and
  Sebok's realised-return findings (2019), the 2025 state wave, or
  [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]] — which enacted **both** of its
  recommendations *and* the rate cap it argued against. Read it as the analytical frame, not
  as a current statement of the market or the law.

## Who is speaking

Paige Marta Skiba is a Vanderbilt law-and-economics scholar and one of the most cited
empirical researchers on **payday lending** specifically; Jean Xiao was her co-author at
Vanderbilt. Neither works for the industry.

Skiba's background is the point. The payday analogy is being tested by the person best
placed to know whether it holds, and she concludes it does not hold in the place that
matters most. When the leading payday-lending empiricist says consumer litigation funding
is not payday lending, that is worth more than the same conclusion from a funder — and it is
also worth noticing that her prior work makes her sceptical of bans generally.

## Seminar hooks

- **New York enacted their remedies and their rejected remedy at the same time.** Chapter
  645 requires the plain disclosure they wanted (funded amount, itemised charges, maximum
  owed, payment schedule, all in 12-point bold), the attorney acknowledgment they wanted —
  and a **25% cap on gross proceeds**, which is exactly the characteristic restriction they
  said would hurt low-income borrowers and be easily evaded. Whether that prediction holds
  is now a testable question, and it is a very good final-paper subject.
- The disclosure they propose is **not an APR**. "[A] reasonable approximate repayment
  amount and date" is a different object, and the reason is the strongest analytical point
  in the paper: a plaintiff cannot use a rate without knowing the term, and nobody knows the
  term. Swiss Re recommends APR disclosure "similar to credit cards and payday loans" —
  exactly the comparison this paper dismantles.
- The non-recourse point cuts further than the authors take it. If the debt trap is the
  justification for payday rate caps, and consumer funding has no debt trap, then the
  argument for capping consumer funding has to be rebuilt on some other ground —
  unconscionability, or the valuation problem, or the funder's discretion over haircuts that
  Avraham and Sebok document. Nobody on the syllabus rebuilds it.
