## Claim

- Litigation funding is a **utility** — "neither inherently 'good' nor 'bad'," which "can
  be well used or abused depending on the context" (p. 2, p. 8).
- The regulatory target is therefore **calibration**, not prohibition or permission:
  "neither over nor under regulation."
- The phenomenon "is too variable to be addressed by a **one-size-fits-all disclosure
  rule**" (p. 9). This is the load-bearing recommendation and it puts her against both
  camps in this week's reading.
- What is needed instead is a **discretionary balancing test** applied case by case, plus
  three structural protections: fiduciary duties on funders, minimum returns for consumer
  and aggregate claimants, and non-arbitrability of funding disputes.

## Argument

- Start from the fact that the harms and benefits **run in every direction at once**, and
  are not distributed the way either lobby says.
  - **Plaintiffs** gain access, better counsel, corporate finance for businesses, venture
    capital for firms, and impact litigation across the ideological spectrum — and face
    predatory rates, interference in the attorney–client relationship, loss of settlement
    autonomy, and funder–client conflicts.
  - **Defendants** face non-meritorious cases funded inside portfolios, protracted
    litigation, due process problems where the funder is undisclosed, and settlements
    blocked by someone who is not at the table.
  - **Courts and the public** have an interest because "how, why, and to whose benefit
    judicial resources, as taxpayer funded resources, are used is a matter of public
    concern" (p. 7).
- Because the same instrument produces all of this depending on **who is funding what and
  why**, a uniform rule will be wrong in most applications. The balancing test takes six
  factors: type of plaintiff and funding motivation, type of funder and its incentives,
  nature of the case, forum and precedential effect, financing structure, and procedural
  posture.
- The three structural recommendations:
  - **Fiduciary duties** on funders "to act in the best interests of the funded client"
    (p. 10), analogised to Arizona's application of professional conduct rules to
    nonlawyers holding economic interests in law firms.
  - **Minimum returns** so that "plaintiffs in consumer and aggregate litigation receive
    minimum returns to help combat predatory financing" (p. 10) — expressly not extended
    to sophisticated commercial parties, who can price their own deals.
  - **Non-arbitrability** of funding disputes, so the public gets transparency into how
    funding affects civil justice rather than having the record disappear into private
    arbitration.
- On **national security and foreign sovereign funding**, which is the framing the hearing
  was convened around: real, but "hypothetical and future-looking" (p. 3). These "outlier
  scenarios" "should not drive regulation," though judges should be able to reach them when
  they appear.
- Throughout: "there is almost no empirical data about litigation funding" (p. 4), which is
  itself an argument for judicial discretion over legislative bright lines.

## Evidence

- This is **testimony, not research** — a distillation of roughly fifteen years of her own
  scholarship for a congressional audience, with the argumentative shape that implies. It
  carries no new data and does not claim to.
- The empirical claim it does make is a **negative** one, and it is the same finding GAO
  reached independently a year earlier: the data does not exist. Two sources with opposite
  institutional positions agreeing on that is the most reliable fact available in Class 1.
- The **Arizona** analogy is the only concrete regulatory precedent she offers, and it is
  about nonlawyer **ownership** of firms rather than case funding. Class 7 takes up whether
  that maps cleanly.
- The balancing test is not tested against any worked example. Its weakness is the one
  every multi-factor standard has, and a defence-side witness would say so: six factors and
  no weighting is an invitation to inconsistency, and inconsistency is expensive for the
  party that has to price litigation risk.

## Who is speaking

Maya Steinitz is Professor of Law and R. Butler Gordon Scholar in International Law at
Boston University, a former Latham & Watkins litigator, an elected member of the American
Law Institute, and the author of a Cambridge University Press book on litigation finance
and law firm ownership. She is the closest thing this field has to a neutral expert, and
she is the most-cited academic on both sides of the disclosure fight.

Note the venue. She was invited by a committee whose hearing was titled **"Unsuitable
Litigation"** and whose evident purpose was to build a record for restriction — and she
declined to give it. Telling a committee that its headline concern is an outlier that
"should not drive regulation" is the detail that establishes her independence, and it is
worth carrying into any use of her work. She reappears in Class 8 with the full disclosure
proposal in [[Steinitz - Follow the Money]].

## Where it goes

- [[Commercial versus consumer funding]] — the split drives every recommendation she makes.
  Minimum returns for consumers, nothing for sophisticated commercial parties.
- [[Disclosure of funding agreements]] — **against** blanket disclosure, and therefore the
  best counter to both [[Swiss Re - US litigation funding and social inflation]] and the
  defence-bar position in Class 8. Her fuller proposal arrives there.
- [[Funder control over the case]] — **for** the proposition that control is a real
  problem, directly contradicting [[Burford - Legal Finance 101]]. Her answer is fiduciary
  duties rather than prohibition.
- [[Consumer funding and the price of money]] — **for** intervention, via minimum returns
  rather than rate caps.
- [[Access to justice]] — **mixed**, and the only genuinely mixed voice in Class 1.

## Seminar hooks

- She is the only person on this week's list who refuses the frame of the question put to
  her. The committee wanted foreign sovereign wealth funding American lawsuits; she called
  it hypothetical and redirected to predatory consumer pricing and attorney–client
  interference. Ask what a witness gains and loses by doing that.
- Her position and Swiss Re's **both** rest on the absence of data, and they draw opposite
  conclusions from it. Swiss Re treats the gap as a reason to mandate disclosure so the
  data can be gathered; Steinitz treats it as a reason to leave discretion with judges
  until it is. That is a genuine methodological disagreement about who should act under
  uncertainty, and it will recur every week.
- The fiduciary-duty proposal is the most consequential idea in Class 1 and gets the least
  attention. It would make funders something other than investors, and it collides head-on
  with Burford's "we are a passive outside investor" framing. If a funder owes the client
  a duty to act in the client's best interests, the entire non-recourse pricing model has
  to be reconsidered — the funder's interest is in the multiple, and the client's is in
  the net recovery.
