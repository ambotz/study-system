## Claim

- GAO was asked to describe the market and could not, because **the data does not exist**.
  It could not quantify market size, the number of active funders, typical deal terms, or
  investor returns from any public source.
- What it could establish: the market splits cleanly into **commercial** and **consumer**
  funding, which are different products serving different people at different prices; the
  commercial side is growing; there is **no nationwide disclosure requirement** in federal
  litigation; and the industry "is not specifically regulated under U.S. federal law."
- It lays out **six policy options** for closing the data gap without recommending any of
  them.

## Argument

- The report is deliberately not an argument. Its structure is: describe what funders said,
  describe what experts said, describe what state law does, and lay out options with
  trade-offs.
- That posture is itself the finding. When the only federal body to look at this market
  reports that it cannot size it, every confident figure quoted elsewhere in Class 1
  becomes a claim rather than a fact.
- The commercial/consumer distinction is drawn at the outset and does the organising work:
  commercial arrangements run to millions of dollars between funders and corporate
  litigants; consumer funding is typically under $10,000 advanced to an individual
  plaintiff for **living expenses**, not for litigation costs.

## Evidence

- **Sample.** Twelve US funders — seven commercial, five consumer — selected by
  **judgmental sampling** "to include a mix in the type of TPLF provided" (p. 3). Only
  **four of the twelve** provided transaction data for 2017–2021. A roundtable of twelve
  experts. GAO states plainly that the sample is nongeneralizable.
- **Commercial deal size.** Three commercial funders deployed on average about **$2.3m per
  single-case agreement** and **$4.5m per portfolio agreement** over five years (p. 10). A
  2021 market report put the averages higher — **$3.5m single-case, $8.5m portfolio**
  (p. 10). One trade association said members invest in litigation with damages of $10m or
  more, with a **$2m minimum** per transaction (p. 9).
- **Growth.** Among the three commercial funders reporting, funding provided through
  single-case and portfolio arrangements **more than doubled** over 2017–2021 (p. 11).
- **Consumer deal size.** Average advances between **$1,000 and $10,000** (p. 13).
- **Consumer pricing.** One funder's rates "starting at 15 percent of the amount funded";
  another at "18 percent of the amount funded (applied every 6 months)" (p. 20). The second
  compounds — that is roughly 39% a year, and it is not expressed as an APR anywhere in the
  contract, which is precisely the disclosure problem.
- **Commercial returns, self-reported.** One funder reported **93% return on invested
  capital** on concluded assets since inception; another **91% ROIC** on completed
  investments in two funds since 2017 (p. 22). Note that ROIC since inception is not an
  IRR and is not time-weighted — it says nothing about how long the capital was deployed.
  Compare the IRR table in [[Swiss Re - US litigation funding and social inflation]], which
  is a different measure from a different source.
- **Return structures.** "Returns based on a multiple of what a funder invested, a
  pre-negotiated percentage of the recovery, or a percentage rate of return" (p. 5 n.13).
- **State consumer regulation.** Arkansas caps interest at **17%**; Tennessee caps annual
  fees at **10%** of the original advance (p. 25). Maine and Nebraska require registration
  and contract disclosure of the total repayable (p. 26).
- **Federal disclosure.** "There is no nationwide requirement to disclose litigation
  funding agreements to courts or opposing parties in U.S. federal litigation" (p. 26).
  Two local rules exist: **N.D. Cal.** since November 2018, requiring disclosure of the
  identity of any funder in class, collective or representative actions; and **D.N.J.**
  since June 2021, requiring a statement identifying the funder, stating whether the
  funder's approval is needed for litigation or settlement decisions, and describing the
  nature of the financial interest (p. 28). The New Jersey rule returns in Class 8 as
  [[New Jersey Local Civil Rule 7.1.1]].
- **Six policy options for the data gap** (pp. 16–18): collection by arbitration
  institutions; by state regulators through licensure; by federal regulators via the CFPB
  or SEC; by the court system through disclosure rules or surveys; voluntarily by funders;
  or by lawyers and firms through bar reporting. All "involve trade-offs" and "may require
  legal action or other steps to implement."

## Who is speaking

GAO is Congress's audit arm, writing at the request of members, and its institutional
incentive runs toward describing rather than concluding. It has no position in this market
and nothing to sell.

The limitation is structural rather than motivated: GAO could only report what funders
chose to tell it. Four of twelve supplied transaction data, and the returns cited are
**self-reported by the funders who wanted them reported**. A report that says "we cannot
size this market" is credible precisely because it had every reason to produce a number and
did not.

## Seminar hooks

- Put GAO next to Swiss Re and the disagreement is not about funding, it is about
  **epistemics**. Swiss Re publishes a 21% reduction in claimant compensation; GAO, with
  subpoena-adjacent standing and a year of work, reports it cannot determine deal terms at
  all. Whichever side you argue, the first move is to say which evidentiary standard you
  are applying.
- The consumer numbers are the ones to have at hand, because they end every abstract
  argument. A **$3,000 advance at 18% applied every six months** is roughly 39% a year,
  compounding, for someone waiting on a personal injury settlement. Bedi and Marra's
  pre-claim framework has nothing to say about that transaction, and Burford's guide does
  not describe it either, because neither is in that business.
- Four of twelve funders provided data. Ask what a mandatory-disclosure regime would
  actually produce that voluntary cooperation did not — and notice that of GAO's six
  options, only two run through courts. The disclosure fight in Class 8 assumes the courts
  are the right collection point, and GAO does not.
