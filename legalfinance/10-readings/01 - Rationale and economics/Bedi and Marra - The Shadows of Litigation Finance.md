## Claim

- Litigation finance is badly regulated because of two prior failures: an **awareness
  problem** (policymakers do not understand the mechanics, or how the product differs from
  financing that already exists) and an **analytical problem** (there is no framework for
  assessing its welfare effects).
- Every existing assessment looks only at what funding does **after a claim accrues**.
  That is half the picture. Funding also casts a **pre-claim shadow** — it changes how
  parties behave while contracting and performing, before any dispute exists.
- Once both shadows are counted, the welfare accounting comes out **net positive**, and
  policymakers should "encourage rather than obstruct litigation finance."

## Argument

- Litigation finance is an **enforcement mechanism**: it raises the probability that a
  liquidity-constrained or risk-constrained party can actually vindicate a legal right.
- Framed that way, it differs from contingency fees, pro bono representation, and ordinary
  debt and equity financing **in degree, not in kind**. All of them transfer enforcement
  risk from a rights-holder to someone with more capital or more appetite. This move does
  most of the work in the article, because it denies the premise that funding is a novel
  thing requiring novel regulation.
- **Post-claim effects** are the familiar ones: claim selection, litigation strategy,
  settlement behaviour, the price of legal services, and where risk sits. These are what
  the existing literature argues about.
- **Pre-claim effects** are the contribution, and there are three:
  - **Fewer inefficient breaches.** A party contemplating breach can no longer price in
    the counterparty's inability to sue, so the threat of enforcement does the work the
    law intends it to do. Efficient breach survives, because a breach that is genuinely
    value-creating still pays after damages.
  - **More contracting.** Parties who would have declined a relationship because
    enforcement was uncertain now enter it.
  - **Better contract design and pricing.** The enforcement risk premium falls, so terms
    can be written on the merits rather than around the risk of an unenforceable promise.
- The policy inference: a regulator who restricts funding on post-claim grounds is
  suppressing the pre-claim benefits without ever having counted them.

## Evidence

- The article is **theoretical, not empirical**. Its method is law-and-economics
  modelling of party behaviour, not data on what funded cases actually do.
- That matters for how far it can be pushed. The pre-claim shadow is a **predicted**
  behavioural response. No study is offered showing that breach rates fell, that
  contracting volume rose, or that enforcement risk premia narrowed where funding became
  available.
- The contrast with [[GAO - Third-Party Litigation Financing market characteristics]] is
  the point: GAO went looking for the data and reported that it does not exist. An
  argument that depends on unobserved behavioural effects is unfalsifiable in the current
  evidentiary environment — which cuts both ways, since the opposing claims in
  [[Swiss Re - US litigation funding and social inflation]] are equally unverified.
- The "difference in degree, not in kind" move is an **argumentative** claim, not a
  finding. It can be contested on the ground that a contingency-fee lawyer owes fiduciary
  duties and is subject to bar discipline, and a third-party funder is neither.

## Who is speaking

Suneal Bedi is Assistant Professor of Business Ethics and Law at Indiana University and
affiliated faculty at the Maurer School of Law's Center for Law and Society. **William C.
Marra was, at the time of writing, Investment Manager at Validity Finance** — a commercial
litigation funder. The affiliation appears in the first-page author footnote, not in the
abstract or the argument.

That does not make the article wrong, and the pre-claim framework stands or falls on its
own logic. It does mean the piece is not disinterested scholarship in a student-edited law
review; it is a well-built argument for a conclusion that is worth money to one author's
employer. Marra appears again on this syllabus in Class 9, writing on litigation funding
for a trade publication — check his affiliation at that date, because it is a useful
marker of how the industry's people move.

## Where it goes

- [[Non-recourse capital]] — supplies the "enforcement mechanism" framing for why the
  non-recourse structure exists at all.
- [[Commercial versus consumer funding]] — the article's argument is built on **commercial**
  parties who contract, breach and price risk. It has very little purchase on a personal
  injury plaintiff borrowing $3,000 against a claim, and it does not claim to.
- [[Access to justice]] — **for**. The strongest theoretical statement of the pro-funding
  case on the syllabus.
- [[Litigation funding and liability costs]] — **against** the Swiss Re position, though
  obliquely: if funding deters inefficient breach, rising enforcement is the system
  working, not a cost imposed on it.

## Seminar hooks

- The framework's reach is narrower than its conclusion. Every pre-claim effect assumes a
  party who **contracts in advance** — which is the commercial world and not the tort
  world. Roughly 63% of the market by Swiss Re's figures is mass tort and personal injury,
  where there is no contract to design, no breach to deter, and no risk premium to price.
  Ask what the pre-claim shadow is for a plaintiff hit by a truck.
- Nobody on this syllabus contests the pre-claim effects, because nobody has measured
  them. That is the real disagreement in Class 1 — not what funding does, but **what
  counts as evidence**. Swiss Re argues from correlations, Bedi and Marra from a model,
  and GAO reports that neither has the data.
- The "difference in degree, not in kind" analogy to contingency fees is doing enormous
  work and has an obvious answer: a contingency-fee lawyer is a fiduciary who can be
  disbarred. Whether that distinction is a difference in kind is essentially the question
  Class 4 is about.
