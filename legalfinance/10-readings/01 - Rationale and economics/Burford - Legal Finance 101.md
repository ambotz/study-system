## Claim

- Legal finance is **corporate finance applied to a legal asset**. A pending claim, award
  or fee entitlement is an asset like any other, and a company that leaves it unfinanced is
  carrying risk and forgoing liquidity for no reason.
- The capital is **non-recourse**: "Legal finance is not a loan; it is generally
  non-recourse, meaning that Burford assumes downside risk of loss and the client need not
  repay any investment or advance unless and until matters resolve successfully" (p. 4).
- The funder is **passive**. "Burford is a passive outside investor…we have no rights to
  manage the litigation in which we invest," and no "rights to control settlement of the
  litigation, which remains wholly in the litigant's purview" (p. 5).
- Champerty, work product and disclosure are all handled — treated as settled questions
  rather than live ones.

## Argument

- **Define the product by what it finances, not who receives it.** Three shapes: fees and
  expenses financing, **monetization**, and claim purchase (pp. 2–3). Monetization is the
  one that separates this from ordinary funding — it pays the client "100% of advance paid
  at deal close or on client's preferred schedule, with additional significant remaining
  upside when case resolves successfully" (pp. 3–4).
- **Sell the portfolio.** Matters can be combined "in a cross-collateralized portfolio
  (which can include both plaintiff and defense matters)" (p. 3), and portfolios are said
  to be "58% of Portfolios represent three out of five of Burford's new commitments." The
  portfolio pitch is budget predictability across a litigation book, not access to a single
  case.
- **Price by risk, in four shapes.** Variable (percentage of recovery, "comparable to
  contingency fees"), fixed (capital back plus a multiple), hybrid, and a **return
  waterfall** setting the order in which parties are paid. "[P]ricing for legal finance is
  proportional to risk. This may reflect the stage of the litigation, the type of matter,
  the likely duration or another factor" (p. 4).
- **Neutralise the three standard objections in a single page** (p. 5):
  - **Control** — the leasing analogy: "Just as a leasing company does not tell you how to
    drive your car, legal finance companies don't drive the litigation."
  - **Work product and privilege** — materials shared in diligence "are protected under the
    work product doctrine in the US," and the deal documents themselves are protected
    because their terms "reflect the information provided in work product protected
    documents, such as lawyers' mental impressions, theories and strategies," with
    confidentiality agreements executed on top.
  - **Champerty** — the doctrines "either do not exist or do not interfere with legal
    finance as practiced by Burford in the jurisdictions in which we provide financing."
- **State the eligibility bar honestly**, which is the most useful thing in the document:
  at least **$3m in financing** against expected compensatory damages of around **$30m**
  (p. 18).

## Evidence

- This is **marketing collateral**, and the evidentiary standard is what the seller chooses
  to assert. Claims about control, privilege and champerty are stated, not argued or
  sourced.
- **The control claim is the one to test.** "Passive outside investor" describes the
  contractual allocation Burford says it takes, not a legal constraint on what a funding
  agreement can contain. Steinitz testifies that funder interference with settlement
  autonomy is a documented problem; Sebok and Wendel in Class 5 ask what duty a funder owes
  when the deal breaks down; and the Sysco–Burford fight in Class 4 is a live dispute over
  exactly who controls a settlement. The answer is not that Burford is lying — it is that
  "we do not take control rights" and "funders cannot take control rights" are different
  propositions and the document runs them together.
- **The champerty sentence is carefully drawn.** It is limited to "as practiced by Burford"
  and "in the jurisdictions in which we provide financing." Both qualifiers matter; Class 4
  is about the jurisdictions where the answer is different.
- **The privilege claim is a statement of the better view, not settled law.** Work product
  protection for funder diligence materials is contested and jurisdiction-dependent, and
  the further claim that the funding agreement itself is protected is weaker than the claim
  about diligence memoranda.
- **The disclosure claim is descriptive and currently true** — "The vast majority of courts
  do not require disclosure of legal finance arrangements in commercial matters" (p. 5) —
  and Class 8 is about whether it stays true.
- **Nothing on accounting or cost of capital**, despite the corporate-finance framing. For
  a document aimed at general counsel, the absence of any treatment of how a monetization
  is booked is conspicuous.

## Who is speaking

Burford Capital is the largest publicly traded litigation funder in the world, listed on
the NYSE and the LSE. This document exists to originate deals, and its audience is a
general counsel or a firm's management committee, not a regulator.

Read it as the industry's **best case stated in its own vocabulary**, which is exactly its
value: every term you will need for the term sheet exercise is here, and Kelley has put it
on the same reading list as a reinsurer's attack and a government audit for a reason. The
tell in any marketing document is what it declines to discuss. Here that is pricing levels
(structures are described; numbers are not), the consumer market (absent, because Burford
is not in it), and any acknowledgment that control, privilege or champerty are contested.

Note also what the syllabus link actually reaches. The assigned URL is a landing page that
gates the guide; the substantive document is the PDF recorded above.

## Seminar hooks

- The eligibility threshold is the answer to the access-to-justice argument, and it comes
  from Burford's own document: **$3m minimum financing, ~$30m expected damages**. Whatever
  this product does, it does not reach the individual plaintiff — and the individual
  plaintiff is who both the access-to-justice defence and the usury critique are about.
  Ask whether commercial funding should be defended on access grounds at all, or on the
  same grounds as any other corporate finance.
- The leasing analogy breaks in one specific place. A leasing company is indifferent to how
  you drive because it is paid either way; a non-recourse funder is paid **only if you
  win**, and paid more if you win bigger. That is not passivity, it is a contingent
  economic interest aligned with outcome, and it is why Steinitz's fiduciary-duty proposal
  bites. Have the two documents side by side.
- Cross-collateralized portfolios "can include both plaintiff and defense matters." Notice
  what that does to every argument in this week's reading: the claim-by-claim merits
  analysis stops being the unit of decision, the funder's exposure is to a book rather than
  a case, and [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] in Class 3 argues that is
  where weak claims get carried.

  litigation or arbitration…or to accelerate or 'monetize' the value of pending claims,
  judgments, awards or fees, in exchange for a portion of the ultimate recovery."

  guaranteeing minimum return ahead of case resolution with no downside risk."
- p. 4: "pricing for legal finance is proportional to risk."
- p. 5: "Use of legal finance does not alter control of decision-making or attorney-client

  drive your car, legal finance companies don't drive the litigation."
