## Claim

- The bill attacks litigation funding through the **tax code** rather than through
  disclosure, licensing or champerty — a different regulatory instrument from anything else
  on this syllabus.
- It imposes a **punitive excise tax** on profits from litigation funding agreements, at a
  rate set to eliminate the return rather than to raise revenue.
- Its reach is deliberately wide: no geographic limit, no protection for tax-exempt or
  foreign investors, and no offsetting of losses.
- **Version note:** introduced **May 20, 2025**. Its language was carried unchanged into the
  Senate's 2025 reconciliation draft as new **§ 5000E-1**, which the House had earlier
  rejected. Record which print you are describing; the reconciliation version is the one
  Sidley analyses.

## Argument

The mechanics, as carried into the Senate reconciliation draft:

| Element | Provision |
|---|---|
| **Rate** | **40.8%** — the top individual rate plus the 3.8% net investment income tax |
| **Base** | "Qualified litigation proceeds": "realized gains, net income, or other profit received by a covered party during a tax year which is derived from, or pursuant to, any litigation financing agreement" |
| **Who pays** | "Covered parties" — third parties to a civil action receiving funds under a litigation financing agreement who are **not attorneys**. Applies at the **entity level**, including to pass-throughs |
| **Character** | A **separate excise tax**, not income tax — so **no treaty protection** |
| **Losses** | Losses from other investments **cannot offset** the liability |
| **Tax-exempt and foreign investors** | **No exemption**. Pension funds, endowments and sovereign investors face the full 40.8% |
| **Withholding** | Named parties and involved law firms must withhold **20.4%** — half the excise rate — on payments to covered parties |
| **Definition of the agreement** | A written agreement where "a third party agrees to provide funds" to parties or law firms, creating "a direct or collateralized interest in the proceeds" contingent on litigation outcomes |
| **Carve-out** | Straight loans below **7%**, or **2× the 30-year Treasury yield** (roughly 10% at the time) |
| **Civil action** | "[A]ny civil action, administrative proceeding, claim, or cause of action" — **no requirement** that it be in a US court, under US law, or between US parties |

- **The design is prohibitive, not fiscal.** A 40.8% entity-level levy on gross profit, with
  no loss offset, stacks on top of ordinary income tax for taxable investors. The economics of
  a fund built on a small number of large winners covering many zeros do not survive it.
- **The tax-exempt point is the sharpest.** Pension funds and endowments are significant
  limited partners in litigation finance funds and are ordinarily indifferent to entity-level
  character. Denying them exemption removes the capital base rather than taxing the manager.
- **The loan carve-out defines the target.** Lending at a fixed rate below the threshold is
  untouched; **contingent, outcome-linked capital** is what is taxed. That is a legislative
  judgment that non-recourse participation in a lawsuit's proceeds is the thing to be
  discouraged — the same target champerty had, reached by a different route.

## Evidence

- **This is a bill, not law.** The House rejected the Tillis language before the Senate draft
  incorporated it, and its survival into final legislation was uncertain as of Sidley's June
  2025 alert. **Check the current status before any use.**
- **congress.gov blocks automated retrieval** (robots.txt), so the provisions above come from
  [[Sidley - Punitive excise tax regime for litigation financing]] analysing the
  reconciliation text that carried S. 1821's language unchanged. Pull the bill text yourself
  from the syllabus link and confirm the figures.
- **No fiscal analysis is offered** in the sources here — no JCT score, no estimate of revenue
  or of behavioural response. For a tax measure that is a conspicuous absence, and it supports
  reading the rate as prohibitive rather than revenue-raising.
- **The drafting is broad in ways that may not be intended.** "[A]ny civil action,
  administrative proceeding, claim, or cause of action" with no geographic limit reaches
  international arbitration funding with no US connection. Whether Congress can or means to
  tax that is a real question.

## Who is speaking

Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.), a member of the Judiciary Committee, and a sponsor aligned with
the tort-reform and business-defence coalition that has pressed for funding restriction —
the same coalition behind the Litigation Funding Transparency Act in Class 8 and the
Chamber ILR's Rule 26 proposal.

What makes the bill worth close attention is the **choice of instrument**. Disclosure
proposals have stalled for a decade. A tax provision can move through **reconciliation**,
which needs a simple majority and no committee consensus on the underlying policy. That is a
procedural insight about how this area might actually change, and it is more important than
the bill's current odds.

## Seminar hooks

- **Compare instruments on one axis: what does each require a court to decide?** Champerty
  requires a judgment about intermeddling. Usury requires characterising the advance as a
  loan. Unconscionability requires a fairness inquiry. Disclosure requires a procedural rule
  and generates satellite litigation. **A 40.8% excise tax requires none of that** — it
  operates on the return regardless of whether the agreement is valid, fair or disclosed.
  That is why it is the most dangerous proposal on the syllabus from the industry's side, and
  the point is worth making out loud.
- **It would fall hardest on the investors nobody is arguing about.** Pension funds and
  endowments get no exemption. The public case for restricting funding is built on hedge
  funds and opaque capital; the tax base is whoever supplies the money, and much of that is
  institutional money managing other people's retirements.
- **The carve-out tells you the theory.** Lending below roughly 10% is fine; taking a
  contingent share of proceeds is taxed at 40.8%. Strip away the tax vocabulary and that is
  a **champerty statute** — the objection is to sharing in the fruits of someone else's
  lawsuit. That is exactly what the medieval doctrine was about, per
  [[Neuberger - From Barretry Maintenance and Champerty to Litigation Funding]], and exactly
  what [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] abolished. Six hundred years later, through
  the Internal Revenue Code.
