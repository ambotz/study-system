## Claim

- Litigation funding disputes are usually argued as questions of *legality* — champerty,
  usury, ethics. The more useful question is what happens **when the deal breaks down**, and
  that is a question about **duty**.
- Four risks are built into every litigation-investment agreement: **information asymmetry,
  shirking, control, and forgone opportunities**.
- The right framework is **contract, not tort**. The funder–claimholder relationship "[is]
  sufficiently different from that of an insurer and an insured such that tort law should not
  be followed."
- These are **relational contracts**, requiring "renegotiation of terms in order [to]
  preserve the contract as an ongoing relationship" rather than a single moment of breach and
  damages.

## Argument

- **Start from the risk inventory**, which is the most transferable part of the paper and
  maps directly onto term sheet drafting:
  - **Information asymmetry** — the claimholder and its counsel know the case; the funder
    knows it through diligence and reporting. Everything the funder learns after closing
    comes from the party whose interests may have shifted.
  - **Shirking** — once funded, the claimholder's marginal incentive to press the case falls,
    because the funder's return comes off the top. Effort is unobservable.
  - **Control** — the claimholder decides strategy and settlement, but the funder bears the
    downside. Whoever holds the decision does not hold all the consequences.
  - **Forgone opportunities** — the funder's capital is committed and illiquid for years; a
    claimholder who settles cheap or abandons the claim destroys value the funder cannot
    recover elsewhere.
- **Why not tort or fiduciary duty.** The obvious analogy is insurance, where an insurer owes
  the insured duties of good faith enforceable in tort because of the disparity in
  sophistication and the insured's dependence. The authors reject the analogy: the parties to
  a commercial funding agreement are sophisticated, the relationship is negotiated, and the
  exposure runs the other way — it is the **funder** who is dependent on the claimholder's
  conduct after closing, not the reverse.
- **Why contract.** Because the parties can price and allocate these risks ex ante, and
  because a relational-contract frame lets the agreement adapt as the case develops rather
  than fixing obligations at signing.
- **The consequence for drafting** is the practical payoff: if contract is the frame, the
  protections have to be **in the document** — reporting covenants against information
  asymmetry, effort and cooperation covenants against shirking, consent rights and consultation
  mechanics against control risk, and minimum-return or waterfall protection against a cheap
  settlement. Every one of those clauses is an answer to one of the four risks.

## Evidence

- **Doctrinal and theoretical**, not empirical. No funded transactions are studied and no
  disputes are coded. The method is relational-contract theory applied to a deal structure.
- **Written in 2013**, before nearly everything else on this syllabus. The market it models is
  much smaller than today's, and the paper does not anticipate portfolio finance at scale,
  law-firm-level lending, or mass tort financing — the three structures where the control and
  shirking problems are sharpest.
- **The rejection of the insurance analogy is the contestable move.** It holds well for a
  Fortune 100 claimant with outside counsel. It holds poorly for an individual consumer
  claimant, and the paper does not really address that market. Compare
  [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]], which resolves the same question the opposite
  way for consumers — by statute, removing the control right entirely.
- **Sysco is the test the paper did not get.** [[Frankel - Sysco and Burford]] is exactly the
  deal-breakdown scenario modelled here: a settlement consent right, a claimholder wanting to
  settle for non-financial reasons, and a funder refusing. The contract frame is what the LCIA
  tribunal applied — parties may "contract that right away" — and it produced an injunction
  against the claimholder. Whether that vindicates the paper or indicts it is a real question.

## Who is speaking

Anthony Sebok (Cardozo) is the leading academic defender of the lawfulness of litigation
funding against champerty objections, and appears twice on this syllabus — here and as
co-author of the consumer empirical study in Class 2. W. Bradley Wendel (Cornell) is a legal
ethics scholar.

The pairing is deliberate and matters: a funding scholar and an ethics scholar jointly
concluding that the relationship should be governed by **contract rather than fiduciary
norms**. That is the position most favourable to the industry's preferred structure, reached
by two authors with no industry employment — which makes it the strongest version of that
case on the syllabus. Note that Sebok's broader body of work is consistently
funding-friendly; that is a position, honestly held and openly argued, rather than a
conflict.

## Seminar hooks

- **This is the drafting skeleton for the October 13 term sheet exercise.** Four risks, four
  families of clause: reporting covenants, effort and cooperation covenants, consent and
  consultation mechanics, and minimum-return or waterfall protection. Walking into the
  exercise with that mapping is worth more than any substantive point in Class 5.
- **Three sources now answer the fiduciary question three different ways, and the split is
  by claimant type.** Sebok and Wendel say contract, for sophisticated commercial parties.
  Steinitz and Parikh say fiduciary duties, for consumers and mass tort claimants. New York
  says neither — it legislates the control right out of existence for consumers. The
  disagreement is not really about doctrine; it is about **who is on the other side of the
  deal**.
- **The insurance analogy they reject is worth resurrecting for one purpose.** They say the
  funder is the dependent party, so no duty runs *to* the claimholder. But in the consumer
  and mass tort markets the dependence plainly runs the other way, and the same relational
  logic would then generate the duty they deny. Ask whether their conclusion is really about
  the *relationship* or about the *parties* — and note that if it is about the parties, it
  cannot be a general theory of the litigation-investment agreement.
