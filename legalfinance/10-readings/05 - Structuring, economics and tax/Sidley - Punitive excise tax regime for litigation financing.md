## Claim

- The Senate reconciliation draft proposed **new § 5000E-1**, a **40.8%** entity-level excise
  tax on "qualified litigation proceeds," incorporating **unchanged** the language of
  [[Tackling Predatory Litigation Funding Act - S 1821]] after the House had rejected it.
- It is an **excise** tax, separate from income tax, so there is **no treaty protection** —
  and no exemption for tax-exempt or foreign investors, and no offsetting of losses from other
  investments. Withholding of **20.4%** falls on named parties and their law firms.
- Sidley's practical objections: **no grandfathering** for existing agreements, potential
  **double taxation** for corporate pass-throughs, application to **routine commercial
  arrangements** well beyond litigation funding as commonly understood, and unresolved
  questions about **basis recovery**.
- Survival into final legislation was **uncertain** when written.

## Who is speaking

Sidley Austin, in a client alert — a law firm's tax and litigation-finance practices telling
clients and prospective clients what a pending provision would do to them. The audience is
funders, their investors, and corporates with funded claims.

That shapes what it emphasises. A client alert is technically careful (the drafting
observations about basis, pass-throughs and grandfathering are real and useful) and
directionally aligned with the people who would pay the tax. It is not neutral analysis of
whether the policy is sound, and it does not argue policy at all — which is itself
informative about the audience. `speaking_for: practitioner` rather than `funder`: Sidley has
no capital at risk, but it sells advice into this market.

## Seminar hooks

- **"No treaty protection" is the sleeper.** Structuring it as an excise tax rather than an
  income tax means foreign investors cannot rely on double-tax treaties to reduce it. That is
  a deliberate drafting choice aimed squarely at the offshore capital that funds much of the
  industry, and it connects the tax proposal to the national-security framing Steinitz
  declined to endorse in Class 1.
- **"Routine commercial arrangements"** is the objection with the widest reach. The
  definition — a written agreement providing funds in exchange for "a direct or
  collateralized interest in the proceeds" contingent on litigation outcomes — catches
  contingent-fee-adjacent structures, some insurance, and receivables financing secured on
  disputed claims. Ask where the line is between a litigation funding agreement and any
  secured lending against a contested receivable.
- **Note what a client alert does not do.** There is no assessment of whether the underlying
  concern is legitimate, no revenue estimate, and no behavioural analysis. Compared with the
  academic pieces on this syllabus it is technically sharper and analytically narrower, which
  is a good illustration of what `speaking_for` is tracking.
