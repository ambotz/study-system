## Claim

- Machine learning will let funders "more quickly analyze cases," compressing diligence and
  improving the accuracy of funding decisions.
- The economic consequence is the argument: it should "**decrease the cost of litigation
  funding, since machine learning input will decrease the risk**." Cheaper diligence and
  better selection mean a lower required return.
- Two market effects, running in opposite directions:
  - **Expansion at the bottom** — funders could profitably finance **smaller cases** that were
    previously uneconomic because review costs exceeded the opportunity, and could surface
    latent claims nobody has identified.
  - **Contraction at the top** — if AI lowers litigation costs generally, well-capitalised
    parties need funding less.
- But not everything: Chief Justice Roberts, quoted, that "[m]achines cannot fully replace key
  actors in court," particularly judges assessing credibility.

## Who is speaking

**William Marra of Certum Group** — a litigation risk and funding company. This resolves the
question flagged in Class 1: Marra co-wrote
[[Bedi and Marra - The Shadows of Litigation Finance]] while at **Validity Finance**, and by
2024 had moved to Certum. Both are industry. The Vanderbilt article's conclusion that
policymakers should "encourage rather than obstruct litigation finance" was written by someone
who has now spent his career inside two funders, and the disclosure was a first-page footnote.

That does not make either piece wrong. It does mean this is an industry practitioner arguing
that his industry is about to get cheaper and reach further — a claim with commercial value to
Certum whether or not it is true.

## Seminar hooks

- **This is the first answer anyone gives to Neuberger's gap.** He said in 2013 that funding
  "cannot do the business where the claim is small," and every product on the syllabus proves
  him right — Burford's floor is $3m against ~$30m of damages. Marra's claim is that the
  binding constraint was **diligence cost per case**, not the size of the claim, and that AI
  relaxes it. If true, the access-to-justice defence of commercial funding stops being a
  category error. That is the single most consequential claim in Class 9.
- **He argues both directions and does not reconcile them.** Cheaper litigation reduces demand
  from well-capitalised claimants; cheaper diligence increases supply to small ones. The net is
  a market that shifts *downmarket* — more small claims, fewer large corporates — which would
  transform the industry's risk profile, its regulatory exposure (consumer rules, not
  commercial), and everything in Classes 3 and 8.
- **Set him against his own industry.** Marra says AI decreases risk and therefore price.
  Perla at Burford says models "are only as good as the data fed into them" and that roughly
  **90% of commercial disputes settle confidentially**, so the public record is about 10% of the
  picture. Two funders, the same year, opposite emphases on whether the data exists to do what
  Marra promises. [[Perla - The current and future state of AI in legal finance]].
