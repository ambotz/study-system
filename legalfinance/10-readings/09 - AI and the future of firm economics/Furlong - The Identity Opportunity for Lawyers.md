## Claim

- Lawyers have defined themselves by **doing legal work** — as "intelligent, insightful,
  diligent professionals who solve legal problems." That identity is now under attack, because
  "[s]omeone has invented a machine that solves legal problems and gets legal things done
  without any lawyerly intelligence, insight, or diligence at all."
- The crisis is not about capability but about **identity**, and it is a "crisi-tunity."
- The distinction lawyers reach for — that LLMs cannot really reason or hold fiduciary trust —
  is true and may be beside the point: "LLMs use a blindingly fast process of statistically
  driven probability calculation to produce the same kind of output that hard-working lawyers
  generate." If the outputs are indistinguishable, the internal difference does not sell.
- The trajectory he names in one line: from "AI augments the lawyer" to "**the lawyer augments
  the AI**."
- The opportunity is to relocate professional identity in what does not automate — **advocacy
  and representation**, **fiduciary accompaniment** of a client over time, **strategic foresight**,
  and **access-to-justice system design**. "[V]ery soon, that opportunity will become a mandate."

## Argument

- **Start from where the identity is embedded**, not from the technology. Law school prestige
  hierarchies, the billable hour, firm compensation — the profession's whole infrastructure
  encodes the premise that a lawyer's value is the work product they personally generate.
- **That premise is what AI attacks.** Not the lawyer's intelligence, but the market's need to
  pay for it by the hour.
- **Refuse the comforting distinction.** Yes, an LLM is doing probability rather than reasoning.
  The client buying the output does not experience the difference, so the distinction cannot
  carry the profession's economics.
- **Relocate identity in the functions that require a person** — standing up in a courtroom,
  negotiating, being answerable to a client over years, seeing risk before it arrives, designing
  systems that deliver justice at scale.
- **The reframing he endorses**, from a commenter, Rachel Tenin: the shift is "precedent-driven
  to foresight-driven," with identity deriving from "foresight, judgment, and trust" rather than
  "prestige."
- **The question he actually wants asked**: "What would I like to do?" Most lawyers, he argues,
  have never seriously considered it, having taken the existing professional structure as given.

## Evidence

- **None, and it is not that kind of piece.** This is a commentator's essay: no data on AI
  adoption, no firm economics, no measured effect on billing or headcount. Its value is as a
  **frame**, and it should be cited as one.
- The central empirical premise — that LLM output is becoming indistinguishable from lawyer
  output for a meaningful share of legal work — is asserted rather than demonstrated, and it is
  exactly the premise the Thomson Reuters/Georgetown market report would test. That report is on
  this week's list and is Canvas-only.
- **The strongest argument against him is in the same week's reading.** Perla's point that
  "models are only as good as the data fed into them," with ~90% of commercial settlements
  confidential, is a specific and severe limit on what AI can do in exactly the domain — legal
  judgment under uncertainty — where Furlong assumes displacement. Siegel's reporting says the
  same from five funders: AI sources and screens; it does not underwrite.
- He does not engage the **liability** structure at all. A machine cannot be sued for
  malpractice, cannot be disbarred, and cannot hold a fiduciary duty — and the profession's
  economics rest as much on bearing that risk as on producing the work.

## Who is speaking

Jordan Furlong is a legal industry analyst and consultant who has written about the future of
the profession for two decades, publishing here on Substack. He is not an academic, not a
practitioner, and has no position in litigation finance.

He does have a professional stake in the thesis: an analyst whose subject is transformation has
a standing interest in transformation being imminent. Read the essay for its framing of the
question rather than for its confidence about the answer. `speaking_for: press` is the closest
fit — a commentator, not a market participant.

## Seminar hooks

- **This is the only reading in the course about what lawyers are for**, and it is worth saying
  so. Everything else treats the firm as a capital structure, the claim as an asset and the
  client as a counterparty. Furlong asks what is left when the work is commoditised, and the
  answer he gives — advocacy, fiduciary accompaniment, foresight — is a list of the things a
  **funder cannot buy and an MSO cannot own**. That connects Class 9 straight back to Class 7.
- **He supplies the missing premise in the MSO argument.** Class 7 never quite explains why
  firms suddenly need outside capital after two centuries without it. The answer is
  capital expenditure: AI infrastructure is a large, front-loaded, fixed cost, and a partnership
  that cannot retain capital cannot fund it. Holland & Knight lists technology and AI first
  among MSO drivers. **AI is the reason the ownership question got urgent.**
- **The tension with Perla is the sharpest thing in the week.** Furlong says the machine already
  produces output indistinguishable from a lawyer's. Perla says models cannot price a legal
  claim because 90% of the outcome data is confidential. Both can be true — drafting and
  research are data-rich, and predicting *what a dispute is worth* is data-poor. Which means AI
  may hollow out the **billable middle** while leaving both the courtroom and the underwriting
  desk intact. That is a better thesis than either author states.
