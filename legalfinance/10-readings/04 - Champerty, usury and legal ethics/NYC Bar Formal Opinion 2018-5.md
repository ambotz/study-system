## Claim

- The question: "May a lawyer enter into a financing agreement with a litigation funder, a
  nonlawyer, under which the lawyer's future payments to the funder are contingent on the
  lawyer's receipt of legal fees or on the amount of legal fees received in one or more
  specific matters?"
- **No.** Such an arrangement violates **Rule 5.4(a)** — "[a] lawyer or law firm shall not
  share legal fees with a nonlawyer" — whether or not it is styled a non-recourse loan.
- **Client-side funding is untouched.** Where the *client* borrows and repays from recovery,
  Rule 5.4 is not engaged: the lawyer is not a party and the fee is unaffected.
- A **recourse loan at a fixed rate**, repayable regardless of outcome, is permitted.
- The committee **declines to recommend a rule change**: "That is a matter to be decided by
  the state judiciary."

## Argument

- Rule 5.4(a)'s purpose is protection of **independent professional judgment** — the rule
  exists to "remove incentives for nonlawyers to interfere with professional judgment."
- A payment obligation that rises and falls with **the fee in a specific matter** gives the
  nonlawyer a stake in how that matter is run. That is the interference the rule targets,
  regardless of whether any interference actually occurs.
- The **form of the instrument does not save it.** Calling it a non-recourse loan rather than
  a fee split describes the same economics: the funder is paid out of, and in proportion to,
  legal fees.
- **The dividing line is who owes the money and out of what.**

| Arrangement | Rule 5.4(a) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Client borrows, repays from **recovery** | Permitted | Lawyer is not a party; the fee is unaffected |
| Lawyer borrows, repays from **fees in specified matters** | Prohibited | Repayment varies with legal fees — a share by another name |
| Lawyer borrows on **recourse** at a fixed rate | Permitted | Obligation is absolute, not tied to any fee |

- There is **no carve-out for litigation funders**. The committee treats them like any other
  nonlawyer.

## Evidence

- A bar ethics opinion: **persuasive, not binding.** It interprets the New York Rules of
  Professional Conduct for New York lawyers, and it is the view of a city bar committee
  rather than a court or a disciplinary authority.
- **No empirical claim is made** that funder interference occurs. The rule is
  prophylactic — it forbids the structure because of the incentive it creates, which is the
  standard design of conflict rules and also their standard criticism.
- **Its practical effect was significant and the industry disliked it.** Law firm portfolio
  finance, which is much of the commercial market, is repayment out of fees. After 2018-5,
  New York firms structure around it — recourse facilities, obligations tied to firm revenue
  rather than to identified matters, or borrowers other than the firm itself. Whether those
  workarounds satisfy the opinion's logic or merely its letter is the live question.
- **The scope limit is narrower than often reported.** The prohibition attaches where
  repayment is contingent on fees "in one or more **specific matters**." A facility secured on
  a firm's general revenues is a different case and the opinion does not decide it.

## Who is speaking

The New York City Bar's Committee on Professional Ethics — a volunteer committee of
practising lawyers issuing interpretive guidance in the jurisdiction where most US commercial
litigation finance is documented. It regulates nobody, but New York lawyers follow it and
funders draft around it.

The committee's refusal to recommend a rule change is the most telling line. It is being told
the market wants this, it concludes the existing rule forbids it, and it says the remedy lies
with the courts that make the rules. That is a committee declining to solve by interpretation
a problem it thinks belongs to policy.

## Seminar hooks

- **The line is formal and the economics are identical on both sides of it.** A client who
  borrows against recovery and a lawyer who borrows against fees are both giving a nonlawyer
  a stake in the same lawsuit's outcome. The opinion's answer is that in the first case the
  lawyer is not a party — which is a statement about **privity**, not about incentives. Ask
  whether Rule 5.4(a) is protecting independent judgment or protecting a boundary.
- **This is the unasked question in [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]].** Fast Trak's
  secondary contracts took a contingent interest in Sax's fees from five to ten unrelated
  cases — squarely the structure 2018-5 forbids. The Ninth Circuit certified a **usury**
  question and never reached professional responsibility. Two of the Class 4 readings
  converge on one transaction from opposite directions, and the syllabus splits them into
  different groups.
- **Follow the money after 2018.** If lawyers cannot pledge fees in specific matters, capital
  reaches firms some other way — recourse debt, revenue-based facilities, or the **MSO**
  structures in Class 7, where a nonlawyer-owned management company takes a fee for services
  rather than a share of legal fees. Each is an answer to this opinion. Whether they satisfy
  its reasoning or only its wording is the thread running to Texas Ethics Opinion 706.

## Citable detail

- N.Y.C. Bar Ass'n Comm. on Prof'l Ethics, Formal Op. 2018-5, *Litigation Funders' Contingent
  Interest in Legal Fees* (2018).
- The question: "May a lawyer enter into a financing agreement with a litigation funder, a
  nonlawyer, under which the lawyer's future payments to the funder are contingent on the
  lawyer's receipt of legal fees or on the amount of legal fees received in one or more
  specific matters?"

  nonlawyer."
- Purpose of the rule: to "remove incentives for nonlawyers to interfere with professional
  judgment."
- Permitted: traditional recourse loans at fixed interest, repayable regardless of case
  outcome or fee recovery.
- On reform: "That is a matter to be decided by the state judiciary."
- Read with **ABA Model Rule 5.4** (professional independence of a lawyer) and **Model Rule
  1.8(e)** (a lawyer shall not provide financial assistance to a client in connection with
  pending or contemplated litigation, subject to the court-costs and indigent-client
  exceptions) — both assigned alongside this opinion.
