## Claim

- The three medieval doctrines answered a specific problem — **barons using the courts as a
  substitute for private war** — and that problem is gone.
- Their survival long outlived their rationale, and the rule they enforced ("bring your
  claim on your own bottom, and at your own expense") had the effect of **monopolising
  justice for the wealthy**.
- Litigation funding therefore serves the **same institutional goal** the prohibitions once
  served: "the exact reverse of the prohibition is justified for the same reason" (¶48).
  Both are about an inclusive, rule-of-law society.
- The risks are real but are best handled by **self-regulation plus judicial oversight**,
  not statute — at least for now.

## Argument

- **The definitions, which are worth having exactly** (¶¶11–13): **maintenance** is
  "financial assistance to another person to institute proceedings"; **champerty** is the
  aggravated form where the maintainer takes "a share of the proceeds"; **barratry** is
  being "a common mover or stirrer up of suits."
- **The origin is feudal** (¶18). When barons could no longer wage private war they took the
  same "unscrupulousness and trickery" into the courts. The prohibitions protected the
  system from capture by the powerful — the reverse of how they are often described today.
- **The statutory arc** (¶15): the Statute of Westminster 1275 declared them unlawful;
  eighteen further Acts repeated the prohibition through 1576; the **Criminal Law Act 1967,
  ss. 13–14** abolished them as crimes and torts, with a saving provision preserving them as
  a matter of **public policy**. That saving is why the doctrine still has any life at all.
- **Why they declined.** The medieval rationale was obsolete by the eighteenth century;
  Bentham called the restrictions "barbarous precautions" from a "barbarous age" (¶26). By
  the late nineteenth century Lord Esher was reduced to defending them on the ground that
  litigation might "be increased in a way that would be mischievous" (¶29).
- **The real cost of the rule.** Requiring a litigant to sue "on his own bottom, and at his
  own expense" (¶28) means rights are enforceable only by those who can pay, which is not a
  neutral outcome.
- **Access to justice is collapsing independently.** Civil legal aid eligibility fell from
  **80% of the population in 1950 to under 30% by 2008** (¶38), and further cuts were likely
  (¶55). Funding is filling a hole the state dug.
- **He does not pretend the risks away** (¶¶49–50): funding "can promote unmeritorious
  claims, encouraging settlements through intimidation," the post-1999 CFA problems "could be
  replicated if less than scrupulous funders provide funding," and the ethical pressure on
  lawyers rises when financial interests align with outcomes.
- **On regulation** (¶45): he notes the Civil Justice Council "facilitat[ed] the development
  of the present self-regulatory regime, or code, in November 2011," and that the Association
  of Litigation Funders "seems to have done well enough so far, but we are still in early
  days." That is endorsement by observation, not by recommendation. Courts retain power over
  arrangements that "undermine the ends of justice" (¶43).
- **On the Jackson reforms** (¶¶40, 44): more CFA reform, recoverability of success fees
  curtailed, damages-based agreements introduced so lawyers may "share the fruits of
  litigation" — "a very necessary rebalancing."
- **The gap nothing fills** (¶¶55–56): CFAs, DBAs and third-party funding "cannot do the
  business where the claim is small." For those claimants "quick and rough justice" beats
  "no justice."

## Evidence

- **A lecture, not a paper.** Historical narrative, statutory dates, and judicial
  observation. No data on funder conduct, and none claimed.
- **English law throughout**, which matters more than it first appears. The 1967 Act is a
  clean legislative abolition with a public-policy saving; the American doctrine was never
  abolished nationally and survives state by state, which is why
  [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] had to abolish it judicially in 2020 and why
  [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] could still raise it in 2020. Do not import his
  conclusions as statements of US law.
- **The self-regulation endorsement is now dated.** The Association of Litigation Funders
  code was eighteen months old when he spoke, and the UK position has moved substantially
  since — most consequentially the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *PACCAR* on whether
  funding agreements are damages-based agreements, which he could not have foreseen. Treat
  ¶45 as a 2013 snapshot.
- **The legal aid figures are the strongest empirical content** and they are about the
  *demand* for funding, not its supply or conduct.

## Who is speaking

David Neuberger was **President of the UK Supreme Court** when he gave this lecture. Two
things follow, and they point in opposite directions.

The office gives it weight no academic piece on this syllabus has: this is the head of a
final appellate court saying the doctrines that restrained funding were medieval answers to
a vanished problem.

The venue cuts the other way. It is the **inaugural Harbour Litigation Funding Annual
Lecture**, given at the invitation of, and named for, a commercial litigation funder. He is
not paid by Harbour and the analysis is his own, but the audience is the industry and the
occasion is the industry's. `speaking_for` is left blank because he is not advocating for a
market position, and the venue belongs in the argument anyway.

## Seminar hooks

- **The origin story inverts the modern framing.** Champerty existed to stop the *powerful*
  from weaponising litigation against the weak. The doctrine is now invoked to stop the
  *weak* from obtaining capital to sue the powerful. Neuberger's ¶48 — "the exact reverse of
  the prohibition is justified for the same reason" — is the single most quotable line in
  Class 4, and it works in a paper as well as a seminar.
- **He concedes the whole of the other side and still comes out the same way.** Unmeritorious
  claims, settlement by intimidation, the CFA experience, ethical pressure on lawyers — all
  at ¶¶49–50. Anyone arguing the pro-funding case is stronger for conceding these first, and
  it is a better rhetorical model than anything Burford produces.
- **The gap he identifies is the one nobody has closed in thirteen years.** Funding "cannot
  do the business where the claim is small." Every product on this syllabus — Burford's $3m
  floor, portfolio facilities, mass tort firm lending — serves claims large enough to be
  worth underwriting. If access to justice is the justification, the small claim is the case
  the justification cannot reach, and "quick and rough justice" rather than capital is his
  answer to it.
