## Claim

- The Sysco–Burford dispute is the one case where a funder's **settlement consent right** was
  exercised, litigated, and resolved — and it resolved in the funder's favour.
- A Fortune 100 company with sophisticated counsel found it could not settle its own antitrust
  claims, and its own lawyers were, in its view, helping the funder stop it.
- The end state is the most striking fact on the syllabus: Sysco **assigned the claims to a
  Burford affiliate**, which refiled them — represented by Sysco's former counsel.

## Argument

The sequence, which is what matters:

- **2019.** Burford invests **over $140 million** in Sysco's antitrust claims against
  chicken, pork and beef suppliers, on a **non-recourse** basis under a Capital Provision
  Agreement collateralised by recovery proceeds. The CPA described Burford as a **"passive
  provider of external capital"** with Sysco retaining **"full control of the assertion and
  resolution of the claims."**
- **Amendment.** After Sysco assigned portions of claims to indirect-purchaser customers, the
  parties amended the CPA to increase Burford's economic stake in what remained — and added
  the clause that made the case: Sysco **"shall not accept a settlement offer without
  [Burford's] prior written consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld."**
- **2022.** Sysco negotiates settlements with suppliers. Its interest is not purely
  financial — these are its **suppliers**, and it has a continuing commercial relationship
  with them. Burford objects that the settlements undervalue the claims.
- **Arbitration.** Burford commences arbitration in the **London Court of International
  Arbitration**. The tribunal issues a **preliminary injunction** restraining Sysco from
  executing the settlements, reasoning that Burford bore direct economic risk and that
  parties may "contract that right away," and that "whatever remains of champerty does not
  appear to pose [] a barrier."
- **March 2023.** Sysco petitions to vacate in the **Northern District of Illinois**, arguing
  the tribunal's interpretation "violated public policy by allowing the litigation funder to
  take control of settlement," and calling the result Burford's "secret dream come true:
  unfettered control over the settlement of its federal antitrust litigation claims."
- **Counsel.** Sysco **terminates Boies Schiller**, believing the firm was "violating its
  fiduciary duties by assisting Burford in impeding the settlements."
- **June 28, 2023.** Settlement between funder and client. Sysco **assigns all the antitrust
  claims to Carina Ventures LLC**, an entity Burford created.
- **July 2023.** Carina files a new antitrust action in the **Southern District of Texas** —
  represented by Boies Schiller. Texas has **never recognised common-law champerty**, which
  is at least a plausible reason for the forum, since New York law governed the original CPA.

- **One case.** It is the best-documented funder-control dispute available, and it is a
  single sophisticated commercial deal with a heavily negotiated amendment. It does not
  establish market practice.

## Seminar hooks

- **Put the three sentences side by side.** Burford's own guide: it is "a passive outside
  investor" with no "rights to control settlement of the litigation, which remains wholly in
  the litigant's purview." The amended Sysco CPA: Sysco "shall not accept a settlement offer
  without [Burford's] prior written consent." Minnesota in
  [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]]: it is "difficult to conceive of any stipulation
  more against public policy." One market, three positions, and the arbitral tribunal sided
  with the contract.
- **The claim ended up owned by the funder.** Sysco did not win or lose the control fight; it
  exited, assigning the claims to a Burford entity that refiled in a state without champerty,
  using Sysco's former lawyers. That is claim **purchase** arriving as the resolution of a
  funding dispute — the third of Burford's product shapes, reached by accident. Ask what it
  means that the cleanest way out of a control dispute was for the funder to become the
  plaintiff.
- **Why did Sysco want to settle?** Not because the money was good — because the defendants
  were its **suppliers**. A funder's interest is one-dimensional (maximise this recovery); a
  commercial claimant's is not. "Unreasonably withheld" cannot resolve that, because the two
  parties are not even optimising the same variable. That is the structural argument for why
  settlement consent rights are different from other contract terms, and it is better than
  the champerty argument Sysco actually ran.
