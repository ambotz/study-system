## Rule

- **Minnesota's common-law prohibition against champerty is abolished.** An agreement to
  divide litigation proceeds between a claim owner and a stranger to the suit is no longer
  unenforceable on that ground alone.
- The doctrine's work is transferred to **other tools that were always available**:
  **unconscionability**, equitable review by the district court, the Rules of Professional
  Conduct, and the civil-procedure sanctions for frivolous claims.
- Courts must "carefully review uncounseled agreements, particularly between parties of
  unequal bargaining power."
- **Control remains the outer limit.** The court reserves its sharpest language for a funder
  who takes settlement rights: it is "difficult to conceive of any stipulation more against
  public policy" than requiring a financier's permission to settle.

## Facts

Pamela Maslowski was injured in a 2012 car accident in Woodbury and retained counsel on a
personal injury claim. Needing money for living expenses while the case was pending, she
signed a **"Sale and Repurchase Agreement"** with Prospect Funding Partners on **May 21,
2014**, selling a portion of her future settlement proceeds for a **$6,000 advance**.

The pricing is the fact that matters. Her obligation **increased 30% every six months**,
capped at **$25,245** — and she owed nothing if there was no settlement. **Her attorney
reviewed and signed the agreement.**

In June 2015 Prospect told her she owed **$14,108** if she settled before September. Her
attorney declared the agreement unenforceable. She settled in July 2015 and refused to pay.

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Hudson, J.), reversing the court of appeals and remanding:

- **Champerty is abolished in Minnesota.** The agreement was champertous under existing
  doctrine — Prospect was a stranger to the suit supporting the claim in exchange for
  recovery rights — and would have been unenforceable. That doctrine no longer stands.
- **"[T]he common law must also evolve."** Four reasons given:
  - **The abuses are now policed elsewhere.** "[R]ules of professional responsibility and
    civil procedure prevent both attorneys and parties from profiting off of frivolous
    litigation," and the rules restricting solicitation and advertising address what
    champerty was aimed at.
  - **The doctrine has already been abandoned piecemeal.** Contingency fees were once
    champertous and are now universal and understood to widen access — they let clients
    without means "assert their rights." Assignments of claims in action, once barred on the
    same reasoning, are valid.
  - **The market has changed and so have attitudes.** The court values litigation financing
    at **"$50 to $100 billion"**, and treats claims as assets rather than evils: "Businesses
    often seek financing to mitigate the risks associated with litigation and maintain cash
    flow."
  - **Freedom of contract.** "[W]e must not lightly disregard that basic principle."

**Rejected:**

- *That regulation is insufficient.* Financiers have their own economic reason to avoid
  frivolous cases — they profit only if the settlement exceeds the advance — and their
  valuation procedures mitigate the risk.
- *That tort victims need champerty's protection.* Compensation matters, but contractual
  freedom is not displaced by a general concern.
- *That funding deters settlement.* Maslowski offered **no empirical evidence**. The court
  notes scholarship suggesting the opposite — funding signals merit and may encourage
  settlement — and that contingency fees affect settlement calculations too and remain
  lawful.

**Not decided:** whether *this* agreement is enforceable. Remanded, with unconscionability
and equitable defences expressly preserved.

## Context

Maslowski is the case where a state supreme court says out loud that
[[Champerty and maintenance]] has no work left to do — and the reasoning is
[[Neuberger - From Barretry Maintenance and Champerty to Litigation Funding]] applied to an
American common-law doctrine that was never legislatively abolished. Neuberger explains why
the doctrine is obsolete; Minnesota acts on it.

What makes the case useful is that the **facts are terrible for the funder and the funder
still wins the doctrinal point**. A $6,000 advance compounding at 30% every six months, to
an injured individual for living expenses, is the paradigm the critics describe. The court
abolishes champerty anyway and sends the case back to be tested on unconscionability — which
is a bet that the right doctrine will do better work than the crude one.

Pair it with [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]], decided the same year. Minnesota removes
champerty and leaves unconscionability; the Ninth Circuit asks whether **usury** reaches the
same conduct by recharacterising the advance as a loan. Two courts, one year, two different
tools for the same problem — which is exactly the question in
[[Policing funding agreements]].

Note also the court's list of **eight states with consumer litigation financing statutes**
(Arkansas, Indiana, Maine, Nebraska, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Vermont). That was 2020; by
2026, [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]] and the 2025–26 wave in Georgia, Kansas,
Louisiana, Montana and Oklahoma have made legislative regulation the main channel — which is
what the Maslowski court predicted by implication.

The **$50 to $100 billion** market figure is the weakest sentence in the opinion. It is an
order of magnitude above anything Westfleet or GAO can support for the US commercial market,
and it is uncited in the court's own framing. Do not repeat it without a source.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Minnesota Supreme Court, **Hudson, J.**, 2020. Review granted **solely on the champerty
  question**.
- The path was long: Prospect sued for breach in **New York**; Maslowski sought declaratory
  relief in Minnesota challenging the forum-selection clause; two years of litigation in both
  states' appellate courts followed. The district court and court of appeals both held the
  agreement champertous and unenforceable.
- **Disposition: reversed and remanded.** The case returns to be decided on grounds other
  than champerty.
- The posture matters because the court is deciding a pure question of common-law policy with
  the narrowest possible record, and it says explicitly that it is not deciding whether this
  agreement survives.

### Facts

The agreement is styled a **"Sale and Repurchase Agreement"** — Prospect *purchased* a
portion of the proceeds rather than lending — which is the same characterisation the funder
in [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] relied on to escape usury. The label is doing legal
work in both cases.

Economics: $6,000 advanced, obligation rising **30% every six months**, capped at **$25,245**
— a cap of roughly 4.2× the advance. At fourteen months Prospect asserted $14,108, about
2.35×. Nothing owed if no recovery.

The attorney's review and signature matter twice over: it is the fact that makes the
agreement *not* uncounselled, which is the category the court says needs careful review; and
it is the same device that [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]] later makes mandatory
and enforcement-critical.

### Issue

Should Minnesota retain its common-law prohibition against champerty, or abolish it and
permit enforcement of litigation financing agreements subject to other doctrines?

### Holding

Abolished. Reversed and remanded, with unconscionability and equitable review preserved.

### Rule

- **Champerty defined**: "an agreement to divide litigation proceeds between the owner of the
  litigated claim and a party unrelated to the lawsuit who supports or helps enforce the
  claim."
- Minnesota recognised it in **Huber v. Johnson** (1897), applied in **Holland v. Sheehan**
  (1909) and **Hackett v. Hammel** (1932). Those are overruled in effect.
- What survives: **unconscionability**, available to "[p]arties like Maslowski"; equitable
  scrutiny, since "[d]istrict courts may still scrutinize litigation financing agreements to
  determine whether equity allows their enforcement"; professional conduct rules; and civil
  procedure sanctions.
- **Control is still policed.** A stipulation requiring the financier's permission to settle
  is one than which it is "difficult to conceive of any stipulation more against public
  policy."

### Reasoning

The structure is a common-law court retiring a doctrine by showing that each of its
functions is performed better by something else. Frivolous litigation — professional conduct
rules and Rule 11 analogues. Intermeddling — the court's own equitable power and the control
limit. Exploitation of the vulnerable — unconscionability, with a direction to scrutinise
uncounselled agreements between unequal parties.

The doctrinal-consistency argument is the strongest of the four. Contingency fees and
assignment of choses in action were both once champertous and are now unremarkable. Keeping
champerty for third-party funding alone is a line drawn by history rather than by principle.

The weakest is the market argument. "$50 to $100 billion" is unsupported, and the claim that
financiers screen against frivolous cases is asserted from first principles — the court
reasons that a funder profits only if the settlement exceeds the advance. That intuition is
sound for this product and is confirmed by
[[Avraham and Sebok - Empirical Investigation of Third Party Consumer Litigant Funding]]
(48% approval on 191,144 applications), which the court did not have. It does **not**
transfer to firm-level lending; see
[[Non-meritorious claims and claim aggregation]].

On settlement deterrence the court puts the burden squarely on the challenger and finds the
record empty. That allocation is worth noticing: in a policy-driven common-law decision,
whoever needs facts and lacks them loses.

### Dissent / concurrence

None reported.

### Cold-call notes

- **What exactly was abolished?** The common-law *prohibition*, not the concept. An agreement
  is no longer void merely because it is champertous.
- **What replaces it?** Unconscionability first, then equitable review, professional conduct
  rules, and civil procedure sanctions.
- **Did Maslowski win?** No, and neither did Prospect. Reversed and **remanded** — she still
  has unconscionability, on facts that look strong for her.
- **What is the one thing the court will not allow?** A funder's consent right over
  settlement — "difficult to conceive of any stipulation more against public policy." Put
  that against the Sysco–Burford consent right and the arbitral tribunal's contrary view.
- **Why does the attorney's signature matter?** It takes the agreement out of the
  "uncounseled" category the court flags for careful review, and it is the same mechanism New
  York later made mandatory.
- **Weakest point to be ready for:** the $50–100bn figure and the unsupported screening
  premise.
- **Contrast with [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]]:** same year, same underlying product,
  different doctrine. Minnesota retires champerty and keeps unconscionability; the Ninth
  Circuit asks whether the advance is really a **loan** and therefore usurious.
