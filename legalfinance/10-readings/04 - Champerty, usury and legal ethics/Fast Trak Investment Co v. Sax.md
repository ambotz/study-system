## Rule

- **Non-recourse form does not automatically defeat a usury claim.** New York requires that a
  true loan be repayable "absolutely and at all events," but the defence also reaches devices
  "disguise[d]" to conceal a usurious loan. Where the contingency is sufficiently certain to
  occur, the distinction between contingent and absolute obligation can collapse.
- The mechanism that collapses it here is **diversification across cases**: an obligation
  spread over roughly eighteen matters is repaid unless essentially every one of them fails.
  "Sax has made a colorable argument that repayment to Fast Trak is all but guaranteed."
- **The question was certified, not decided.** The Ninth Circuit sent it to the New York
  Court of Appeals. This case is a **framing** of the usury problem, not a holding on it.

## Facts

Fast Trak Investment Company, a Delaware LLC, made advances in spring 2013 under **two
different contracts**, and the structure is the whole case.

- **Primary contracts — with the clients of attorney Richard Sax.** Advances of **$3,000 to
  $15,000** per client, one client receiving **$96,000** in total, against a pledge of future
  litigation proceeds. Expressly styled **"nonrecourse purchase agreements"**, with no
  personal obligation if the litigation failed.
- **Secondary contracts — with Sax alone.** Sax pledged **his attorney fees from roughly five
  to ten unrelated cases** to cover any shortfall on the primary contracts.

Pricing ran on escalating **"Payment Schedules"** rather than a percentage of recovery. For
the client Gadow, a **$3,000** advance required **$4,716.51** if proceeds arrived immediately
— a **57.2%** return — rising to **$8,029.54** after twenty months, **167.7%**. New York caps
civil interest at **16%** and criminal usury at **25%**.

Sax also signed acknowledgments promising to hold proceeds as a fiduciary, notify Fast Trak
of recoveries, and pay Fast Trak first.

## Court Ruling

**Certified to the New York Court of Appeals** (Paez and Bea, Circuit Judges, and Jack,
District Judge, sitting by designation; decided June 11, 2020). The district court had
granted Fast Trak summary judgment on breach of contract and breach of fiduciary duty and
awarded **$323,611.11**.

- **The certified question**: "Whether a litigation financing agreement may qualify as a
  'loan' or a 'cover for usury' where the obligation of repayment arises…upon and from the
  attorney's fees the client's lawyer may recover in unrelated litigation? And, if so, what
  are the appropriate consequences…?"
- **The framework.** N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law **§ 5-501** caps interest at 16%; criminal usury is
  25%; a usurious contract is **void**. But "[i]f the transaction is not a loan, 'there can
  be no usury, however unconscionable the contract may be.'"
- **The pivot.** Fast Trak argued the non-recourse element meant there was no loan. The court
  did not accept that the label ends the inquiry: New York permits a usury defence against
  devices designed to disguise a usurious loan, and if the triggering condition is
  sufficiently certain the contingency is nominal.
- **Why this structure is different (at 465).** By spreading the secondary obligation across
  approximately **eighteen separate cases**, Sax would have to lose essentially all of them
  to escape payment. What is contingent case by case is close to certain in aggregate.
- **Proceedings stayed** and the case withdrawn from submission pending the New York answer,
  including on remedy — whether a successful usury defence requires disgorgement of principal
  or merely caps the rate.

## Context

Fast Trak and [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] are the same year, the same product
and **two different doctrines pointed at it**. Minnesota retires champerty and keeps
unconscionability. The Ninth Circuit asks whether the advance is a **loan**, because if it is,
usury voids it without any inquiry into fairness. That is the choice
[[Policing funding agreements]] is about.

The case also does something the rest of the syllabus mostly does not: it takes the
**non-recourse claim seriously and then tests it**. [[Non-recourse capital]] is treated
everywhere — by Burford, by Skiba and Xiao, by the funders' usury defence — as the structural
feature that makes the pricing defensible. Fast Trak shows the feature is a **question of
fact about portfolio construction**, not a drafting choice. Diversify enough and the
contingency stops being one.

The second half is a **fee-sharing** problem that the court does not reach. The secondary
contracts take a contingent interest in a *lawyer's* fees in unrelated matters — precisely
the arrangement [[NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2018-5]] concludes violates **Rule 5.4(a)**. The two
Class 4 group readings converge on this transaction from opposite directions, and the
convergence is not flagged on the syllabus.

Note the practical asymmetry the structure creates. The client's deal is genuinely
non-recourse; the **lawyer** is the credit support. That transfers risk from funder to
counsel and gives the funder a claim on the lawyer's economics across a book of cases — which
is [[Portfolio finance and cross-collateralisation]] built on the wrong side of the
attorney–client relationship.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Ninth Circuit, on appeal from summary judgment for Fast Trak in the district court
  ($323,611.11 in damages on breach of contract and breach of fiduciary duty).
- Sax raised **New York usury** and **champerty** as defences. New York law governed.
- **Certification to the New York Court of Appeals**, proceedings stayed, case withdrawn from
  submission. The Ninth Circuit decides nothing on the merits.
- The posture is the point: a federal appellate court confronted a question of state law it
  considered genuinely unsettled and important enough to refuse to guess at. That is a signal
  about how open the usury characterisation was in 2020.

### Facts

Two contract layers, and they must be kept apart:

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Counterparty | Sax's **clients** | **Sax** personally |
| Consideration | $3,000–$15,000 advances (one client $96,000 total) | Pledge of attorney fees from ~5–10 **unrelated** cases |
| Recourse | Styled non-recourse | Covers any shortfall on the primary |
| Effect | Client owes nothing if the case fails | Sax owes unless essentially all matters fail |

Pricing, Gadow: $3,000 → $4,716.51 immediately (57.2%) → $8,029.54 at twenty months
(167.7%). Against a 16% civil and 25% criminal usury ceiling.

### Issue

1. Can a litigation financing agreement be a "loan" or a "cover for usury" where repayment
   comes from attorney's fees the client's lawyer may recover in **unrelated** litigation?
2. If so, what follows — void the contract, disgorge principal, or cap the rate?

### Holding

No holding. Both questions certified to the New York Court of Appeals; proceedings stayed.

### Rule

- **N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-501**: 16% civil ceiling; 25% criminal usury; usurious contracts
  void.
- **Threshold**: "If the transaction is not a loan, 'there can be no usury, however
  unconscionable the contract may be.'"
- **The classical test**: a loan requires repayment "absolutely and at all events."
- **The anti-evasion principle**: a usury defence reaches transactions "disguise[d]" to
  conceal a usurious loan, so the parties' chosen label is not decisive.
- **The synthesis the court gestures at**: where the contingency is so likely to occur that
  repayment is "all but guaranteed," a nominally contingent obligation may be treated as
  absolute.

### Reasoning

The court's move is to shift the analysis from **form** to **probability**. Everyone agrees a
non-recourse advance is not a loan. Everyone agrees a disguised loan is still a loan. The
open space between them is filled by asking how contingent the contingency actually is — and
that is an empirical question about the structure, not a doctrinal one about the words.

Diversification is what does it. A single non-recourse advance against one personal injury
claim is genuinely at risk. An obligation secured on eighteen independent matters is not,
because the probability that all eighteen fail is negligible. The funder's own risk
management converts its product into something that looks like lending.

The court stops there. It does not decide whether the probability threshold is met, what the
threshold is, or what remedy follows — it says the questions are for New York. That restraint
is why the case is assigned: it isolates the question without resolving it.

### Dissent / concurrence

None. Unanimous certification order.

### Cold-call notes

- **What did the Ninth Circuit hold?** Nothing. It **certified**. Saying "the Ninth Circuit
  held these agreements usurious" is the error to avoid.
- **Why does non-recourse usually defeat usury?** Because a loan requires repayment
  "absolutely and at all events," and if the case fails nothing is owed.
- **So what is different here?** The obligation sits across ~18 matters, so "repayment to
  Fast Trak is all but guaranteed." Contingency in each, near-certainty in aggregate.
- **Who bore the risk?** The **lawyer**, not the client. The primary contracts were genuinely
  non-recourse; the secondary contracts made Sax the credit support.
- **What is the rate?** 57.2% at once, 167.7% over twenty months, against a 16% civil ceiling
  and 25% criminal usury.
- **What is the unasked question?** Whether a funder may take a contingent interest in a
  lawyer's fees at all — [[NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2018-5]] says Rule 5.4(a) forbids it.
- **Contrast with [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]]:** champerty out,
  unconscionability in, versus usury as the tool. Two courts, one year, one product.
