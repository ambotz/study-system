## Claim

- The disclosure debate has been mis-framed as a search for a rule. "The Essay aims to turn
  the debate inside out by proposing to abandon the quest for a bright line rule and to
  instead adopt a **flexible, discretionary standard: a balancing test**" (p. 1074).
- No rule can work because the scenarios are not comparable. What rule could account for the
  difference between "a corporate plaintiff whose legal costs are partially covered by a
  sophisticated investor who has arranged with the corporation's law firm to fund a portfolio
  of cases" and "a fired factory worker whose civil rights case is funded by a small startup"
  (p. 1094)?
- Disclosure is therefore **four variables, not one switch**: *what* is disclosed (existence,
  identity, terms), *to whom* (tribunal only, tribunal and parties, public), *when*, and *why*.
- The judge or arbitrator decides, weighing **three categories of interest** through **eight
  factors**, and may revisit as the case develops.

## Argument

- **The four variables, illustrated.** "[A]t the beginning of the process disclosure of the
  identity of the funder aimed only at the tribunal may be all that is needed for conflicts
  check purposes. Conversely, at the end of a case…disclosure of the funding terms to both the
  tribunal and opposing party may be warranted" (p. 1092). Disclosure is **iterative**.
- **Three categories of interest.**
  - **Public** (pp. 1096–1100): access to justice; development of law, so diverse litigants
    can "play for rules"; a level playing field; efficiency; and data for evidence-based
    policy. Plus the principle that "courts should be a place for the resolution of disputes
    and not a source of business profit" (p. 1097).
  - **Private** (pp. 1100–01): plaintiffs want access and financial privacy; defendants want
    material information and the real party in interest; funders want to protect their
    "intellectual property in the financial products they produce" and avoid discovery burdens
    and satellite litigation.
  - **Forum** (pp. 1101–02): conflicts checking, efficient resolution, avoiding satellite
    litigation, and detecting terms that incentivise prolonged or meritless litigation.
- **The eight factors** (pp. 1102–12):

| # | Factor | Why it moves the answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Plaintiff profile and motive** | "[C]onsumers are generally understood to require a higher level of protection than do sophisticated entities" (p. 1102); class members need court protection |
| 2 | **Funder profile and motivation** | For-profit funds, nonprofits, wealthy individuals, **foreign governments**, PACs, crowdfunding — motives run from profit to ideology to revenge to foreign policy |
| 3 | **Case type and forum** | "[C]lass and mass cases, wherein the lawyers rather than the clients drive and control the case, are very different from individual claims" (p. 1105) |
| 4 | **Subject matter** | Securities, antitrust, environmental, employment each differ |
| 5 | **Effect on development of law** | Funding that lets underrepresented constituencies shape doctrine weighs against disclosure |
| 6 | **Structure of the financing** | Contingency rates, portfolios, **control-over-settlement clauses** |
| 7 | **Purpose of the disclosure** | Conflicts check, real party in interest, fee-gouging, strategic delay — different purposes justify different scope |
| 8 | **Procedural posture** | Initial pleading versus settlement or fee-shifting |

- **The irony she plants** (p. 1104): "maintenance — funding without a profit motivation — may
  be more problematic than champerty…even though much of the contemporary consternation around
  the rise of litigation finance focuses on 'profiteering'." An ideological funder has reasons
  to prolong a case that a profit-seeking one does not.
- **Class actions and MDLs get heightened treatment** (p. 1105): class members are "unnamed and
  may even be unknown," courts already supervise settlements closely, and funding "is likely to
  exacerbate conflicts of interest in this context and so court involvement should be
  heightened as compared to individual cases." That is why the LFTA targets exactly those
  forums.

## Evidence

- **The baseline survey is the most useful factual content and is dated.** As of writing,
  **24 of 94 district courts** required disclosure — 14 by local rules amending Rule 7.1, two
  by standing order, ten by local form (p. 1079). **Six circuits** required disclosure beyond
  FRAP 26.1 of "any person or organization with a financial interest in the litigation"
  (p. 1080), the stated purpose being "to assist judges with evaluating possible issues of
  recusal and disqualification." **Wisconsin** enacted the first state disclosure statute in
  April 2018 (p. 1081). Recount all of this before relying on it — the 2025–26 state wave and
  the current Advisory Committee work postdate the essay.
- **It is an essay, not an empirical study.** The balancing test is not tested against worked
  examples beyond a brief reference to an order in *In re National Prescription Opiate
  Litigation* (p. 1114).
- **She does not resolve work product**, which is the gap. She notes courts have diverged on
  whether funding agreements prepared in anticipation of litigation are protected, citing
  Delaware cases (p. 1082 n.38), and leaves it there — while her whole proposal assumes a judge
  can calibrate disclosure without first settling what is discoverable at all.
- **The standard objection is the one every multi-factor test attracts** and the defence bar
  makes it directly: eight factors with no weighting is unpredictable, and unpredictability is
  expensive for the party pricing litigation risk. LCJ and ILR's answer is the 1970 insurance
  precedent — a bright line the Committee adopted over the same objections.

## Who is speaking

Maya Steinitz's second appearance on this syllabus, and the fuller statement of the position
she took to the House Oversight Committee four years later in
[[Steinitz - House Oversight testimony on third-party litigation funding]]. Then at Iowa, now
at Boston University; a former Latham litigator, elected to the ALI, author of a Cambridge
book on litigation finance and law firm ownership.

She is the most-cited academic on **both** sides of this fight, which is unusual and worth
noticing: the defence bar cites her documentation of the harms, the funders cite her rejection
of blanket disclosure. That is a sign the work is doing what scholarship should, and it also
means a citation to Steinitz alone establishes very little.

## Seminar hooks

- **Her proposal and the LCJ/ILR proposal are not really arguing about disclosure.** They are
  arguing about **who decides** — a judge case by case, or the Rules Committee once, for
  everyone. Steinitz's objection to a rule is that the scenarios are incommensurable; LCJ's
  answer is the **1970 insurance amendment**, adopted over identical objections when courts
  were similarly split. Whoever frames the question that way owns the room.
- **The maintenance-versus-champerty inversion is the best contrarian point in Class 8.** A
  funder seeking profit wants an efficient resolution; an ideological funder, a foreign state
  or a competitor may want the case to *continue*. So the profit motive that attracts all the
  criticism is the one most aligned with settling — which cuts directly against the
  case-prolongation charge in [[Swiss Re - US litigation funding and social inflation]].
- **Her framework assumes away the question it needs answered.** A judge cannot calibrate
  disclosure without knowing whether the agreement is work product at all, and she expressly
  leaves that unresolved. [[In re Nimitz Technologies]] shows how it actually gets handled —
  in camera review, privilege asserted document by document — which is neither a rule nor a
  balancing test but a third thing: **procedure substituting for doctrine**.
