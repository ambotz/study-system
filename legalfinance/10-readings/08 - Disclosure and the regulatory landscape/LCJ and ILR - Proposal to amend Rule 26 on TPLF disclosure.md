## Claim

- Amend **Rule 26(a)(1)(A)** to add a new subsection (v) requiring **automatic initial
  disclosure** of the funder's identity **and the funding agreement itself**, without awaiting
  a discovery request.
- The governing analogy is the **1970 insurance amendment**: courts were similarly split, the
  same objections were made, and "the Committee resolved this issue in favor of disclosure."
- Courts need to know "**who is in the courtroom**" — who benefits from the judgment and who
  holds settlement authority.
- Privilege is handled by ordinary Rule 26(c) machinery — protective orders, in camera review,
  redaction — not by a categorical exemption.

## Argument

- **The proposed text**, which is worth having exactly:

  > "the name, address, and telephone number of any non-party individual or entity (other than
  > counsel of record) that, whether directly or indirectly, is providing funding for the
  > action and has a financial interest therein and, for inspection and copying as under
  > Rule 34, **any agreements or other documentation concerning the terms of the funding** for
  > the action or the financial interest therein."

- **Scope and carve-outs.** "Funding" covers past or future payments for attorney's fees, costs
  or expenses — **including client recruitment** — whether direct or through intermediaries.
  "Financial interest" reaches any direct or indirect compensation, **including securitised
  interests in law firm income**. Excluded: contingency fee arrangements with counsel;
  insurance; ordinary personal or bank loans on standard terms; and pre-existing donors,
  shareholders, members or investors in the parties themselves.
- **The evidence they lead with is contractual, not statistical.** LCJ examined **nine actual
  TPLF agreements** and reports provisions letting funders take over the conduct of the
  litigation on alleged breach, **veto settlements**, direct replacement of counsel, require
  plaintiffs to pay penalties or sanctions, and mandate sharing of discovery documents with the
  funder.
- **"Zombie litigation."** Cases continuing after the parties want to settle, because a hidden
  funder holds the veto. Plus hidden conflicts involving counsel, and funder-imposed contractual
  terms that undercut court orders.
- **The split they cite comes from the funders' own data**: federal courts divide roughly
  **60–40 against** disclosure.
- **Answering the work product objection.** Disclosure of the operative agreement "does not
  encompass a funder's internal analyses—even if such information were included in a contract, a
  remote possibility at best," and **none of the nine contracts examined contained work
  product**. Agreements are treated like retainer agreements, insurance policies and corporate
  structure documents — not categorically privileged.
- **Answering the satellite-litigation objection.** Producing the operative documents up front
  *forecloses* the discovery forays, because the questions that would otherwise be litigated are
  answered on the face of the agreement. The flexible phrase "agreements or other documentation"
  lets courts scale the disclosure to the complexity of the arrangement.
- **Rejecting the ex parte alternative** proposed by funders: it would violate **Canon 3(A)(4)**
  of the Code of Conduct for U.S. Judges, which limits ex parte communications to scheduling,
  administrative and emergency matters; deprive the opposing party of a chance to respond;
  prevent precedential standards from developing; and create information asymmetries that
  distort settlement.
- **Revisions from their October 2, 2024 suggestion**: a tighter definition of funding and
  financial interest; the explicit carve-outs; heavier reliance on protective orders; the
  expanded insurance analogy; and direct answers to nine questions the Subcommittee posed.

## Evidence

- **Nine contracts.** That is the empirical core of the submission, and it is a **convenience
  sample of unknown provenance**. LCJ does not say how the agreements were obtained, whether
  they are representative, which funders wrote them, or from which market segment — and this
  market splits sharply between commercial, consumer and mass tort products with very different
  terms. Nine agreements showing veto rights establishes that such rights exist, which nobody
  denies after Sysco. It does not establish how common they are.
- **"None of the nine contained work product" is doing heavy lifting** and is a claim about the
  same nine. It is also close to circular: the objection is that producing the agreement reveals
  the *pricing*, which encodes the funder's merits assessment. Saying the document contains no
  internal memoranda does not meet that point.
- **The 60–40 split is cited to the funders' own data**, which is rhetorically effective and
  worth verifying independently — Steinitz counted 24 of 94 districts requiring disclosure in
  2019, a different denominator and a different question.
- **The insurance analogy is the strongest argument in the submission** and has a real
  disanalogy. Insurance coverage sets a **ceiling on the defendant's ability to pay**, which
  bears directly on settlement value and is the same for every case under the policy. A funding
  agreement's terms reveal an **assessment of the merits** made by a sophisticated party after
  diligence. Those are different kinds of information, and the 1970 Committee was not asked
  about the second.
- **No data on outcomes.** N.D. Cal. has run a disclosure rule since 2018 and D.N.J. since 2021.
  A submission arguing that automatic disclosure forecloses satellite litigation could have
  tested that claim against five years of New Jersey docket data. It does not.

## Who is speaking

**Lawyers for Civil Justice** is a defence-bar organisation of corporate counsel and defence
firms; the **U.S. Chamber Institute for Legal Reform** is the Chamber's tort-reform arm. Between
them they are the most sustained institutional force for restricting litigation funding in the
United States, and they have been pressing a version of this amendment since at least 2017.

This is **advocacy filed as a rules suggestion**, and reading it as such is the point. It is
also very good advocacy: the carve-outs are drafted to defuse the strongest objections
(contingency fees and insurance are out), the historical analogy is well chosen, and the
responses to the Subcommittee's nine questions are specific. The tell is the evidence base —
nine contracts of unstated provenance, and no engagement with the two districts that have been
running disclosure rules for years.

Note who benefits from the asymmetry the proposal leaves in place: a funded plaintiff discloses
its financing; a self-funded corporate defendant discloses nothing about its litigation reserves
or its defence budget.

## Seminar hooks

- **The 1970 insurance amendment is the whole ballgame, and the disanalogy is findable.**
  Insurance discloses the defendant's **capacity to pay** — a ceiling, identical across every
  case under the policy. A funding agreement discloses a **merits assessment** priced by a
  sophisticated party after diligence. A defendant reading the multiple and the waterfall learns
  what the funder thinks the case is worth. That is the funders' real objection, and the
  submission's "no internal analyses in the contract" answer does not touch it.
- **Nine contracts.** That is the evidentiary base for a national rule amendment, with no
  statement of how they were obtained or whether they are representative. Hold it against GAO,
  which approached twelve funders and got transaction data from four, and reported that it could
  not size the market. Both sides in Class 8 are arguing from the same shortage — and the side
  arguing for disclosure is the one whose case would be strongest if the data existed.
- **The proposal reaches "securitised interests in law firm income."** That is not
  case-by-case funding at all — it sweeps in portfolio facilities, law firm lending, and by
  implication the structures in Class 7. Whether the Committee intends to make a firm's capital
  structure an initial disclosure in every case it files is a question the submission does not
  confront, and it is the sharpest thing to ask about the drafting.
