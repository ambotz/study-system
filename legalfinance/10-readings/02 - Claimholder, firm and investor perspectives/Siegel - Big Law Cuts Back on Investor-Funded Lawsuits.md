## Claim

- The largest US firms are **pulling back** from litigation finance, reversing several years
  of growth. The AmLaw 200's share of new investor commitments fell to **just under 25%** as
  of June 30, 2025, from **37%** the year before.
- The cause offered is **regulatory scrutiny** plus internal operational friction —
  conflicts clearance, disclosure exposure, and management committee appetite — rather than
  a shortage of capital.
- The market itself grew over the same period: **$2.8bn** in new case commitments and
  **$16.1bn** AUM for the year ending mid-2024, against under $10bn five years earlier. So
  the money did not leave; it went elsewhere.

## Who is speaking

Emily Siegel is the Bloomberg Law correspondent covering this beat, and she appears four
times on this syllabus — the closest thing the course has to a running chronicle of the
industry. Bloomberg Law is trade press: fast, well-sourced, and reliant on named market
participants who talk to it for reasons of their own. The only source named in the visible
portion is **Charles Agee of Westfleet Advisors**, whose data is the report in
[[Westfleet Insider - 2025 Litigation Finance Market Report]]. The article is effectively
that report, interpreted.

## Seminar hooks

- The retreat and the growth are happening at once. Commitments rose and AUM rose, but the
  largest firms' share nearly halved — so the capital moved to smaller firms and boutiques.
  Ask what that does to the quality of screening, and whether a market where the most
  sophisticated repeat players are stepping back is one where pricing is getting better or
  worse.
- If scrutiny is the cause, the disclosure debate in Class 8 has already changed behaviour
  **before any federal rule exists**. That is a real argument against the defence bar's own
  position: the deterrent is working without the rule.
- Put this against Class 7. Big Law is stepping back from funding individual cases in the
  same period that firms are reaching for outside capital through MSO structures. Those are
  opposite movements in the same market, and the reconciliation is probably that one carries
  disclosure exposure in live litigation and the other does not.
