## Claim

- Law firms do not fail the way other businesses fail. They fail **suddenly and
  completely**, and often while still profitable. "No large law firm has ever managed to
  reorganize its debts in bankruptcy and survive."
- The cause is not financial distress. It is **ownership structure**: "Partner ownership
  makes a law firm fragile by exposing it to an accelerating spiral of decline that I call
  a **partner run**."
- A partner run is a bank run in a different asset. Once departures begin, staying becomes
  worse for everyone who remains, so the rational move is to leave early — and everyone
  reasoning that way makes it true.
- The fragility is therefore a **correctable organisational choice**, not an inevitable
  feature of professional services. The fixes run through capital structure, including
  outside ownership.

## Argument

- **No capital lock-in.** Investors in a corporation can sell shares but "cannot truly
  withdraw by demanding a return" of the underlying capital. Law firm partners can.
  "[U]nlike the investors who own conventional businesses, the partners who own a law firm
  can freely withdraw."
- **And they cannot be stopped.** Model Rule 5.6 bars restrictions on a departing lawyer's
  right to practise, so non-competes and meaningful capital retention are off the table.
  Morley notes this is a **US peculiarity** — "this rule is unique to the United States—it
  does not apply to lawyers in the United Kingdom or Australia."
- **Two mechanisms make the run rational**, and both reward leaving early:
  - **Profit sensitivity.** Partners take distributions, not salary. Each departure
    mechanically lowers what remains for those who stay, so every exit raises the incentive
    for the next.
  - **Personal liability that accrues with time served.** Fraudulent transfer liability for
    compensation taken before insolvency; preferential transfer liability for capital
    repaid; and **unfinished business** liability requiring departing partners to share
    profits on matters completed elsewhere. "These forms of liability all punish partners in
    direct proportion to how long they stay at a declining firm. Partners who leave late
    suffer more than partners who leave early."
- **He rejects the obvious alternative explanation.** The absence of hard assets is usually
  offered as the reason firms cannot survive insolvency; Morley says it cuts the other way.
  Having no collateral "makes liquidation less attractive, not more," because creditors do
  better if the firm keeps operating.
- **The natural experiment.** **Slater & Gordon**, an investor-owned Australian firm, hit
  insolvency and did not shatter. As owner-partners left it "continued to pay its lawyers
  and other employees their regular salaries and bonuses, just as other insolvent
  investor-owned businesses do." Salaried lawyers have no distribution to watch shrink, so
  they have no run incentive.
- **Accounting firms sit in between** — "not quite as vulnerable," because an accounting
  firm "can restrict its partners from leaving."
- **Three families of fix**: regulatory reform permitting investor ownership or withdrawal
  restrictions; changes to partnership agreements and compensation design; and non-financial
  bonding through loyalty, which he is frank about the limits of — "[i]f all partners care
  about is money, then partner ownership has a hard time creating financial incentives that
  can hold a firm together."

## Evidence

- The method is **structural and comparative**, not statistical: a mechanism, applied to
  the known collapses (Dewey & LeBoeuf, Heller Ehrman, Howrey and the rest), tested against
  organisations with different ownership rules.
- **Slater & Gordon is the load-bearing evidence**, and it is a single case. It is a strong
  one because it varies the right thing — same professional service, same insolvency, one
  difference in ownership — but it is an Australian firm in a market with different rules
  throughout, so the comparison is not clean.
- No base rate is offered. Firms that shrank painfully without collapsing, and firms that
  had runs and survived, would test the theory and are not systematically examined.
- The unfinished-business doctrine he leans on has been **narrowed by courts** since the
  wave of Dewey-era litigation, which weakens one of the three liability legs. Worth
  checking against current law before relying on it in the paper.

## Who is speaking

John Morley is a professor at Yale Law School writing on the economics of organisational
form. He has no position in the litigation finance market, and the piece is not about
litigation finance at all — it is about why law firms are structurally fragile.

That is precisely why Kelley assigned it. It supplies the **demand-side** explanation for
everything in Classes 2 and 7. A firm that cannot lock in capital, cannot restrain
withdrawal, and holds no assets but people has an obvious need for outside money and an
obvious reason it cannot get it on ordinary terms. The version assigned is the magazine
treatment in *The Practice*; the full academic article is worth pulling for the paper.

## Seminar hooks

- The fragility argument is an argument **for** litigation finance that has nothing to do
  with access to justice. A firm that cannot retain capital and cannot restrain partner
  exit needs a funding source that does not depend on partner equity — which is what a
  portfolio facility is. Nobody in Class 1 made this argument, and it is the strongest
  non-moral case on the syllabus.
- The US rule Morley identifies as the root cause is a **professional conduct rule**, not a
  market fact. Model Rule 5.6 bars the restraint; the UK and Australia allow it. So the
  fragility is a regulatory artefact, and every reform in Class 7 — outside ownership, ABS,
  MSOs — is an attempt to route around a rule the profession wrote for itself.
- Watch what the run mechanism implies for a **funder**. Portfolio finance to a law firm is
  secured on a fee book generated by partners who can all leave on any given Tuesday, and
  whose incentive to leave rises exactly when the firm's finances deteriorate. That is the
  collateral risk nobody in Class 1 mentions, and it is the reason law firm lending is
  priced the way it is.
