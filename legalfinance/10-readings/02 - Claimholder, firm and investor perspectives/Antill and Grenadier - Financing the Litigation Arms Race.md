## Claim

- Litigation is an **arms race**: both sides spend to improve their position, and much of
  that spending is pure deadweight loss because it transfers value rather than creating it.
- Third-party funding strengthens the plaintiff's side of that race. The first-order effect
  is **more spending and larger deadweight losses**.
- But there is a second-order effect running the other way: a credibly funded plaintiff
  **deters the defendant from making costly defensive investments** in the first place. A
  defendant who knows it cannot outspend the plaintiff into submission may not try.
- Net effect on total litigant surplus is therefore **ambiguous** — it can go either way
  depending on parameters, and the paper declines to resolve it.
- The separate finding, and the one that travels: funding "does not necessarily encourage
  high-risk frivolous lawsuits." The frivolous-litigation objection does not follow from the
  economics.

## Argument

- Model a dynamic litigation game in which both parties choose spending over time, and
  settlement can occur at any point.
- The plaintiff's constraint is **liquidity and risk aversion**; the defendant's advantage
  is the ability to spend the plaintiff into a cheap settlement. That asymmetry is what
  funding removes.
- **Channel one, cost-increasing.** A funded plaintiff spends more and resists longer, so
  observed litigation expenditure rises. This is deadweight — money burned on a transfer.
- **Channel two, cost-decreasing.** Defensive spending only pays if it can move the
  outcome. Against a plaintiff with committed capital, the marginal return to the
  defendant's own escalation falls, so the defendant escalates less. The arms race can
  de-escalate because one side is credibly armed.
- **Why the frivolous-claims objection fails on the model's own terms.** A funder is a
  residual claimant on a non-recourse position, so it is paid only on success. Its
  incentive is to screen *harder* than the plaintiff would alone, not to fund lottery
  tickets. Funding a weak case is simply a bad investment.
- The welfare conclusion is left **indeterminate**, which is the paper's honest position and
  the reason it is useful to both sides.

## Evidence

- **Theoretical.** This is a formal model in a finance journal, not an empirical paper. Its
  outputs are comparative statics, not measurements.
- That makes it the **methodological twin of
  [[Bedi and Marra - The Shadows of Litigation Finance]]** — both model behaviour and
  neither observes it. The difference is the conclusion: Bedi and Marra reach a clear policy
  recommendation, Antill and Grenadier refuse to.
- **The refusal is the strength.** A model built by finance academics with no position in
  the market, which could easily have produced a clean answer, reports that the sign of the
  welfare effect is ambiguous. That is worth more than either advocacy paper in Class 1.
- The deterrence channel is the novel claim and is entirely untested empirically. Nobody has
  measured whether defendants spend less against funded plaintiffs, and the data problem
  GAO identified makes it hard to see how they would.
- Model results depend on assumed parameters — the cost of capital, the elasticity of
  outcome to spending, the parties' risk preferences. A reader who wants the opposite result
  can usually get it by changing one.

## Who is speaking

Samuel Antill is at Harvard Business School and Steven Grenadier at Stanford Graduate School
of Business. Both are finance academics, not law professors, and the paper is published in
the *Journal of Financial Economics* — a top-tier finance journal with blind peer review,
which is a materially different quality filter from the student-edited law reviews that
carry most of this syllabus.

Neither has a stake in the industry. The paper's conclusion helps nobody's lobbying, which
is the best available signal that it is not written to help anybody's lobbying.

## Seminar hooks

- This is the cleanest rebuttal to the frivolous-litigation charge on the syllabus, and it
  is not a moral argument. A non-recourse funder is paid only on success, so it screens
  harder than the plaintiff would alone. Avraham and Sebok supply the number that confirms
  it in the consumer market: **funders rejected just over half of all applications.**
  Theory and data agreeing across two papers in the same week is rare here.
- The deterrence channel has never been tested and would be a strong final-paper subject.
  The prediction is specific and falsifiable — defendants facing funded plaintiffs should
  spend *less* on defence, not more — and it is the exact opposite of what the defence bar
  asserts in Class 1 and Class 8.
- Notice how the "ambiguous" conclusion gets used. Both lobbies cite this paper, because it
  contains a sentence each of them wants. Ask what it means that the most rigorous model on
  the reading list is the one nobody can deploy.
