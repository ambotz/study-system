## Claim

- This is the **only large-N empirical study of consumer litigation funding** on the
  syllabus, and it disconfirms the loudest claims made by both sides.
- **Against the critics**: the effective return funders actually realise is far below the
  contract rate. Median **embedded rate of 115%** over the funding period, but median
  **actual return of 50%** — roughly **43% annualised** — because of defaults and
  discretionary write-downs. Results are "significantly lower than some of the speculations"
  by critics.
- **Against the industry**: the contracts are genuinely opaque. The advertised rate
  understates the real price by a wide margin once compounding, interest buckets, minimum
  interest periods and processing fees are counted.
- **Against the tort reform lobby**: funding does not appear to generate claims. Funders
  reject over half of applications, and applications arrive a median of **308 days after
  the injury** — long after the decision to pursue a claim was made.

## Argument

- Obtain the transaction-level data nobody else has, from a single large funder, covering
  both funded **and rejected** applications. The rejected applications are what make
  selection analysis possible.
- **Separate three different "prices"** that the public debate runs together:
  - the **advertised monthly rate** (median 3.2%, implying 38% simple annual);
  - the **embedded contractual rate**, once monthly compounding (88% of cases), interest
    buckets, minimum interest periods and processing fees averaging **12.5% of the amount
    funded** are included — median **115% markup** over the funding period;
  - the **realised return**, after defaults and haircuts — median **50%**, about **43%
    annualised**.
- **The haircut finding is the study's most surprising contribution.** "More than half of
  the consumers who receive funding pay less than the amount due." The distribution: roughly
  **10% complete defaults, 49% haircuts, 34% pay in full, 5% overpay.** So the contract is
  routinely not enforced as written, which means the contract rate is not the price.
- **Test the frivolous-claims hypothesis** on timing and screening, and reject it.
  Applications come nearly a year after injury; the approval rate of about **48%** shows
  funders screening rather than shovelling. The authors conclude that "clients decide to
  file lawsuits for reasons that have nothing to do with funding."
- **Find a market-power result nobody predicted.** Law firms with 1,000+ funded cases
  obtained materially better terms for their clients — posted rates **2.5% against 3.4%**,
  smaller buckets, fees **5% against 10%**. Pricing is relational, so the individual
  plaintiff's price depends on which firm they walked into.

## Evidence

- **The best dataset in the course, by a wide margin.** Roughly 200,000 applications over a
  decade from "one of the largest consumer litigation financing firms in the United
  States"; after cleaning, **191,144 funding requests from 106,800 individuals across
  111,982 cases**, with underwriting decisions, contract terms, outcomes and actual
  repayments.
- **One firm.** That is the central limitation and the authors do not hide it. Pricing,
  screening and haircut policy are firm-level choices; a different funder could behave
  differently. Nothing here establishes an industry average.
- **Data obtained from the funder**, which raises the obvious question of selection in what
  was shared. The counterweight is that the findings are not uniformly flattering — the
  opacity results are damaging to the industry.
- **The haircut finding cuts both ways and should be handled carefully.** It shows realised
  prices below contract prices, which helps the industry. It also shows a market where the
  written contract is routinely unenforced, which means a consumer cannot know their price
  ex ante and is at the funder's discretion ex post. Discretionary forbearance is not the
  same as a fair price.
- Data predates the 2025 New York statute and the recent state wave, so the regulatory
  environment it describes is gone. Figures stand; the legal context does not.

## Who is speaking

Ronen Avraham (Texas and Tel Aviv) and Anthony Sebok (Cardozo) are academics who have
written extensively on litigation finance. Sebok is the field's leading defender of the
lawfulness of funding against champerty objections, and appears again in Class 5 with
[[Sebok and Wendel - Duty in the Litigation-Investment Agreement]] — so he is not a neutral
observer of the doctrinal question, though this paper is empirical rather than normative.

The access arrangement matters. A funder gave two academics a decade of its books, which no
other researcher on this syllabus obtained from anyone. That is the reason the study exists
and the reason its generalisability is limited, and both should be said in the same breath.

## Seminar hooks

- **Three prices, not one.** 3.2% monthly advertised, 115% embedded, 50% realised. Every
  argument about whether consumer funding is usurious is really an argument about which of
  the three is the price — and the answer determines whether a rate cap is a modest
  consumer protection or a prohibition. Nobody in Class 1 had this distinction.
- The **haircut** finding is the one to bring. More than half of funded consumers pay less
  than the contract requires, and about 10% pay nothing. The industry reads that as evidence
  the real price is moderate; the harder reading is that it is a market where the written
  price is a fiction and the actual price is set by the funder's discretion after the fact.
  A consumer cannot shop on a price that will be renegotiated.
- **Which law firm you walk into sets your price.** Mega-firms got 2.5% and 5% fees; small
  firms' clients got 3.4% and 10%. The plaintiff never negotiates and usually never sees the
  comparison. That is a distributional finding with no lobby behind it, and it points at a
  regulatory fix nobody on the syllabus proposes — disclosure of the *firm's* funding
  relationship to the client.
