## The question

The oldest charge against litigation finance is that money brings lawsuits — that capital
without a stake in the merits funds claims nobody would otherwise bring.

The answer turns out to depend entirely on **which product** you mean, and that is the
insight worth carrying. Where a funder is exposed to an individual claim, the economics
force screening. Where a lender is repaid out of a book of claims, they do not.

## The case for (capital generates weak claims)

- **The firm-level lending model rewards volume, not merit.**
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] describes the chain: a credit line to
  a plaintiff firm, spent on advertising and lead generation, converted into claim count,
  converted into settlement pressure, supporting further borrowing. A broker's own summary:
  borrow, buy ads, use the clients to borrow more, "lather, rinse, and repeat."
- **Volume is the lever.** Defendants "feel more 'pressure' to settle when up against a
  lawyer with a large 'volume of cases'." Nothing in that sentence refers to the strength of
  any claim.
- **The spending is real and documented.** Camp Lejeune drew "more than $145 million" in
  advertising in a year, projected to double.
- **Parikh's alchemist's inversion** alleges deliberate marshalling of weak and in some
  cases fabricated claims — the pelvic mesh account, where recipients were told removal was
  medically urgent when often it was not, the surgery was funded, and the claimant was
  routed to counsel.
- **Steinitz reports the same from the defendant's side**: funding of "non-meritorious cases
  when bundled in portfolios." Diversification is exactly what lets a weak claim ride.
- **The harm lands on real claimants**, not defendants: "every dollar received by a false
  victim is one less dollar received by a meritorious one."
- **Swiss Re's systemic version**: more claims, longer cases, larger awards, arriving as
  premiums.

## The case against (capital screens, and screens harder)

- **The economics of non-recourse force screening.**
  [[Antill and Grenadier - Financing the Litigation Arms Race]] model it directly: a funder
  is a residual claimant paid only on success, so it screens more stringently than the
  plaintiff would alone. Funding a weak case is a bad investment. Their conclusion — funding
  "does not necessarily encourage high-risk frivolous lawsuits" — comes from a peer-reviewed
  finance journal with no stake in the outcome.
- **The data agrees, where data exists.**
  [[Avraham and Sebok - Empirical Investigation of Third Party Consumer Litigant Funding]],
  on 191,144 applications: funders approved about **48%** and rejected just over half. That
  rejection rate "suggests that funded plaintiffs still have a very strong interest in the
  outcome of their case."
- **Timing rebuts the generation claim.** Applications came a median of **308 days after the
  injury** — long after the decision to pursue a claim. The authors conclude that "clients
  decide to file lawsuits for reasons that have nothing to do with funding."
- **Advertising is not funding.** Plaintiff firm advertising is lawful, predates litigation
  finance, and is protected commercial speech. Showing that a mass tort was advertised does
  not show that the claims were bad.
- **The pleading system already screens.** Rule 11, Daubert, MDL census orders and Lone Pine
  orders exist to filter unsupported claims, and the remedy for fabricated claims is
  sanctions against the lawyers who filed them, not a restriction on who may lend.
- **GAO recorded no finding** that funding generates meritless claims, despite asking.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The two bodies of evidence are not in conflict; they are about different products.** The
  screening findings come from markets where the funder is exposed to the **individual
  claim** — single-case commercial funding and consumer advances. Parikh describes lending
  against a **firm's book**, where no single claim must justify itself. Both can be true, and
  saying so reconciles the three strongest papers on the syllabus. Neither Parikh nor Antill
  and Grenadier draws the distinction.
- **The quantitative evidence is on the screening side** and the **qualitative evidence is on
  the generation side**, which is a familiar and unsatisfying shape. 191,144 observations
  against a set of named transactions and one quoted broker.
- **Parikh's most serious allegation is his least substantiated.** The pelvic mesh account is
  reported conduct, not an established finding; the crossholder scenario is expressly
  hypothetical. Check the underlying sources before repeating either as fact.
- **Nobody has studied mass tort financing empirically.** It is the largest segment — 38% of
  invested dollars — and Westfleet **excludes it** from the only recurring market
  measurement. So the segment where the theoretical case for weak-claim generation is
  strongest is the one with no data at all.
- **What would settle it**: claim-level outcomes in funded versus unfunded mass tort
  inventories, or dismissal and withdrawal rates in advertised versus unadvertised
  inventories. Neither exists.

## Where the law is now

- **No US jurisdiction restricts funding on the ground that it generates weak claims.**
- **Existing filters are procedural**: Rule 11, Daubert, MDL census and Lone Pine orders,
  and the inherent authority to sanction. These reach the **lawyer**, never the financier.
- **Consumer statutes now reach some of the acquisition chain.**
  [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]] bans referral fees to attorneys **and to
  medical providers**, bans referring consumers to specific attorneys, and bans false or
  misleading advertising — which is a direct legislative strike at the lead-generation model,
  though only in consumer funding.
- **Parikh's proposals are not enacted anywhere**: claims-marshalling regulation, filing
  fees, improved claim verification, fiduciary duties on financiers.
- **Disclosure proposals reach it indirectly**, on the theory that a court that can see the
  financing can police the inventory. Class 8.
