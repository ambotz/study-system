## The question

Not *whether* funding agreements should be constrained — almost nobody argues for none — but
**which instrument should do it**. Five are available, and they differ in what a court has to
decide, who bears the burden, and what happens when the constraint bites.

This is the organising question of Class 4 and it recurs through Classes 5 and 8.

## The five instruments

| Instrument | What a court must decide | Consequence | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Champerty** | Whether a stranger shared in proceeds of another's suit | Unenforceable, no fairness inquiry | Retained in some states; abolished in Minnesota; never recognised in Texas |
| **Usury** | Whether the advance is really a **loan** | Contract **void** in New York; remedy unsettled | [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] |
| **Unconscionability** | Whether *this* bargain was unfair to *this* party | Case-by-case, equitable | What Minnesota kept |
| **Professional conduct rules** | Whether the lawyer's independence is compromised | Discipline; contract may be unenforceable | [[NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2018-5]] |
| **Statute** — licensing, caps, disclosure, tax | Nothing; the legislature decides ex ante | Registration, price ceilings, penalties, or a 40.8% excise | [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]]; [[Tackling Predatory Litigation Funding Act - S 1821]] |

## The case for retiring the common-law doctrines

- **Champerty's rationale is gone.** It was feudal, aimed at barons using litigation as a
  substitute for private war. Neuberger: "the exact reverse of the prohibition is justified
  for the same reason" — both the old rule and modern funding are about an inclusive
  rule-of-law society.
- **It is already abandoned piecemeal.** Contingency fees were champertous and are now
  universal; assignment of choses in action was barred on the same reasoning and is now
  routine. Keeping the doctrine for third-party funding alone is a line drawn by history.
- **The abuses are policed elsewhere.** Minnesota: "rules of professional responsibility and
  civil procedure prevent both attorneys and parties from profiting off of frivolous
  litigation."
- **Bright-line voidness is a blunt remedy.** Champerty and usury both destroy the contract
  without asking whether this claimant was harmed. Unconscionability asks the right question.
- **Freedom of contract.** "[W]e must not lightly disregard that basic principle."
- **Screening happens anyway.** Funders profit only if the recovery exceeds the advance —
  confirmed empirically at a 48% approval rate in
  [[Avraham and Sebok - Empirical Investigation of Third Party Consumer Litigant Funding]].

## The case for keeping a bright line

- **Unconscionability is expensive and unpredictable.** It requires litigation about the
  litigation, by a claimant who by hypothesis lacked bargaining power and money. A doctrine
  that voids the agreement outright needs no such contest.
- **Maslowski itself shows the cost.** Pamela Maslowski took $6,000 and faced an obligation
  rising **30% every six months** to a cap of **$25,245**. She won on champerty below and
  lost that ground on appeal — and was sent back to litigate fairness against a funder, years
  in.
- **Usury reaches what fairness review misses.** It asks a structural question with a clean
  answer, and it caught something real in Fast Trak: diversification across ~18 matters made
  repayment "all but guaranteed," so the non-recourse label was doing less work than it
  appeared to.
- **Contract is not neutral between the parties.** If the agreement governs and there is no
  background duty, the protections must be negotiated — which assumes a claimant able to
  negotiate. [[Sebok and Wendel - Duty in the Litigation-Investment Agreement]] concede their
  frame suits **sophisticated** parties.
- **Control is where every court draws a line anyway.** Minnesota: it is "difficult to
  conceive of any stipulation more against public policy" than requiring a financier's
  permission to settle. If a residual public-policy limit is needed, the doctrines were doing
  something after all.
- **Even England kept a saving.** The Criminal Law Act 1967 abolished the crime and tort but
  preserved champerty as a matter of **public policy**, so courts still police arrangements
  that "undermine the ends of justice."

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The doctrines are in retreat and it is not close.** Minnesota abolished champerty in 2020;
  Texas never had it; England abolished it in 1967; the arbitral tribunal in the Sysco dispute
  held that "whatever remains of champerty does not appear to pose [] a barrier" to a
  settlement consent right. The live instruments are **statute** and **unconscionability**.
- **The choice of instrument is doing more work than the choice of outcome.** Compare the same
  transaction under each: a consumer advance at 39% effective is champertous in a retaining
  state, possibly usurious if recharacterised, probably unconscionable if uncounselled, and
  now simply **capped at 25% of gross proceeds** in New York. Four doctrines, four different
  questions, one product.
- **Forum selection decides the answer.** In [[Frankel - Sysco and Burford]] the claims ended
  up assigned to a Burford entity refiling in **Texas**, which has never recognised champerty,
  under a CPA originally governed by New York law. Where a common-law doctrine varies by
  state, choice of law and forum are substantive terms.
- **Fast Trak left the central question open.** The Ninth Circuit **certified** and did not
  decide — including on remedy. Nobody should describe it as holding these agreements
  usurious.
- **Legislatures have overtaken the courts.** Maslowski counted eight states with consumer
  funding statutes in 2020; by 2026 add New York's comprehensive regime and enactments in
  Georgia, Kansas, Louisiana, Montana and Oklahoma. The common-law question is being answered
  around, not resolved.
- **Taxation is the newest instrument and the most powerful.** A 40.8% excise on proceeds
  requires no court to decide anything about any agreement. It is champerty's objection —
  sharing in the fruits of another's lawsuit — administered by the IRS.

## Where the law is now

- **Champerty**: state law, no federal doctrine. Never recognised in Texas. Abolished in
  Minnesota, [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] (2020). Retained in varying strength
  elsewhere. Abolished as crime and tort in England by the Criminal Law Act 1967, ss. 13–14,
  with a public-policy saving.
- **Usury**: N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-501 — 16% civil, 25% criminal, usurious contracts void.
  Applies only if the advance is a **loan**; recharacterisation certified and unresolved in
  [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]].
- **Unconscionability**: preserved expressly in Maslowski, with a direction to scrutinise
  uncounselled agreements between parties of unequal bargaining power.
- **Professional conduct**: Rule 5.4(a) bars a funder's contingent interest in a **lawyer's**
  fees in specified matters; client-side funding is untouched.
  [[Fee sharing and Rule 5.4]].
- **Statute**: consumer regimes in at least a dozen states, most comprehensively
  [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]]. Commercial funding remains essentially
  unregulated.
- **Tax**: proposed only. [[Tax treatment of litigation funding]].
