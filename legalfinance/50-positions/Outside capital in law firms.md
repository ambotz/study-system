## The question

ABA Model Rule 5.4 bars nonlawyer ownership of law firms and fee sharing with nonlawyers in
every US jurisdiction except Arizona and, in limited form, Utah. Model Rule 5.6 bars
restraints on a departing lawyer's practice.

Together they mean a law firm cannot raise permanent capital and cannot stop its owners
walking out. The question is whether the professional-independence rationale for those rules
still justifies their cost — and whether litigation finance, MSOs and ABS structures are
legitimate adaptations or evasions.

Class 2 supplies the diagnosis. Class 7 is where the remedies are argued.

## The case for allowing it

- **The current structure is why firms die.**
  [[Morley - Why Law Firms Collapse]] shows that partner ownership, not financial distress,
  causes collapse: "[p]artner ownership makes a law firm fragile by exposing it to an
  accelerating spiral of decline that I call a partner run." "No large law firm has ever
  managed to reorganize its debts in bankruptcy and survive."
- **The natural experiment already happened.** Slater & Gordon, investor-owned, hit
  insolvency and kept operating — it "continued to pay its lawyers and other employees their
  regular salaries and bonuses, just as other insolvent investor-owned businesses do."
  Salaried lawyers have no distribution to watch shrink, so there is no run.
- **The restraint is parochial.** Morley: Rule 5.6 "is unique to the United States—it does
  not apply to lawyers in the United Kingdom or Australia." The UK has permitted alternative
  business structures since the Legal Services Act 2007 without the predicted collapse of
  professional standards.
- **Capital would go where it is needed.** Firms cannot fund technology, or carry
  contingency inventory, or survive a downturn, on partner capital that can be withdrawn on
  notice.
- **The market is already routing around the rule.** Litigation finance supplies outside
  capital without ownership; MSO structures supply management-company economics without a
  fee share. If the substance is permitted through structuring, the formal prohibition is
  buying less independence than it costs.
- **Arizona has done it since 2020**, and Steinitz treats its extension of professional
  conduct rules to nonlawyer owners as a model worth copying rather than a cautionary tale.

## The case against

- **Independent professional judgment is the whole point.** An owner with no duty to the
  client, no bar licence to lose, and a return target has a claim on decisions that should
  belong to the lawyer. This is the same concern that animates
  [[Funder control over the case]], scaled up from one case to the firm.
- **The conflict runs to case selection, not just case conduct.** An investor-owned firm has
  a reason to take profitable work and decline unprofitable work, including work it should
  take for other reasons.
- **The mass tort example is the warning.**
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] describes what happened when aggressive
  capital reached plaintiff firms: advertising-driven claim acquisition, debt-financed
  volume, and settlement decisions influenced by people who are not parties. That is outside
  capital in law firms, already, without ownership.
- **Leverage plus fragility is worse, not better.** Morley's run mechanism accelerates when a
  firm carries debt, because the payoff to leaving early rises.
- **The biggest firms are moving the other way.**
  [[Siegel - Big Law Cuts Back on Investor-Funded Lawsuits]] reports the AmLaw 200's share of
  new commitments falling from **37% to under 25%** in a year, on grounds of regulatory
  scrutiny and conflicts. The most sophisticated buyers are choosing less outside capital,
  not more.
- **No one has shown the UK reforms improved anything for clients**, as opposed to for firm
  owners.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **Morley's mechanism is well argued and thinly evidenced.** Slater & Gordon is one firm, in
  another country, under a different rulebook throughout. No base rate is offered for firms
  that declined without collapsing.
- **The unfinished-business leg has weakened.** Courts narrowed unfinished-business liability
  after the Dewey-era litigation, so one of Morley's three time-accruing liabilities is less
  potent than when he wrote. Check current law before relying on it.
- **Nobody has evaluated Arizona.** ABS entities have been licensed since 2020 and no study
  on this syllabus reports what happened to discipline rates, client outcomes, or the kind of
  work those entities take. That is an obvious and unclaimed final-paper subject.
- **The strongest fact on either side is structural, not empirical**: the rules that create
  the fragility are **professional conduct rules the profession wrote for itself**, not
  features of the market. Whatever the right answer, it is a choice rather than a constraint.
- Note the pattern across Classes 2 and 7: every structure in this space — litigation
  finance, ABS, MSO — is a different answer to the same question, which is how to get
  permanent capital into an entity forbidden to sell equity.

## Where the law is now

- **ABA Model Rule 5.4** — no nonlawyer ownership, no fee sharing. In force in essentially
  every US jurisdiction.
- **ABA Model Rule 5.6** — no restrictions on a departing lawyer's right to practise.
- **Arizona** — abolished Rule 5.4 in 2020 and licenses Alternative Business Structures with
  nonlawyer ownership, subject to professional conduct rules applied to the owners.
- **Utah** — a time-limited regulatory sandbox permitting nonlawyer ownership under
  supervision.
- **UK** — alternative business structures under the Legal Services Act 2007; publicly listed
  firms permitted.
- **Australia** — listed firms permitted; Slater & Gordon listed in 2007.
- **MSO structures** — a nonlawyer-owned management company contracting with a lawyer-owned
  firm, currently the live US workaround. Texas Ethics Opinion 706 (Feb. 2025) addresses fee
  arrangements between firms and nonlawyer-owned MSOs. Class 7.
