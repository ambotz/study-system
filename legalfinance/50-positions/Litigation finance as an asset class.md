## The question

An asset class needs more than capital and returns. It needs a tradeable asset, a way to price
it, enough correlation structure to diversify, and investors who can hold it. Whether
litigation finance has those decides whether the industry keeps growing or stays a niche.

This matters beyond finance: almost every regulatory argument on the syllabus assumes a large
and growing industry, and the market's actual size and depth is what that assumption rests on.

## The case for

- **The liquidity layer is being built.** Syndication under participation agreements,
  secondaries, and securitisation attempts are all present — the standard sequence by which a
  private asset becomes a traded one. [[Syndication, secondaries and securitisation]].
- **Insurance has arrived, which is the strongest evidence.** Carriers underwrite judgment
  preservation, adverse judgment and portfolio cover, and **insurers are hiring from funders**.
  An insurer entering means someone believes the risk is actuarially priceable — a costly
  signal, not an opinion. [[Litigation risk insurance]].
- **Returns are real and uncorrelated.** Reported IRRs of 24–35% by segment against long-run
  benchmarks of 13% private equity and 10% S&P 500, with outcomes driven by judicial process
  rather than markets — an uncorrelated return stream, which is what allocators are short of.
- **Portfolios work.** Cross-collateralisation converts binary case outcomes into something
  closer to a distribution, which is what makes institutional capital possible at all.
- **Institutional money is already in.** Pension funds and endowments are limited partners —
  which is why denying tax-exempt investors an exemption would bite so hard.
  [[Tax treatment of litigation funding]].
- **Growth through a downturn.** AUM rose from under $10bn to **$16.1bn** in five years, and
  commitments rebounded about 23% in 2025 after two contracting years.

## The case against

- **It is small.** About **39 active commercial funders**, of which **12–15** drive most deal
  flow, on **$2.8bn** of annual new commitments. That is one mid-size private equity fund.
- **There is no pricing infrastructure.** Valuation is "perhaps more art than science" and of
  expert valuers "few exist in the world." Private equity secondaries publish pricing as a
  percentage of NAV quarterly; this market has no benchmark, no index, and no mark anyone
  outside the manager can verify.
- **Marks are unauditable because the collateral is confidential.** An investor cannot check a
  manager's valuation without case-level privileged material, so reported value is assertion.
- **A secondary is not a realisation** — and managers raising follow-on funds have a reason to
  create arm's-length transactions that look like exits. A track record here can be partly
  manufactured.
- **Adverse selection is structural.** Winners resolve first, so every seasoned pool is worse
  than the portfolio it came from. That is arithmetic, and it poisons secondaries and any
  securitisation of a seasoned book.
- **Returns are not comparable across sources.** Swiss Re quotes IRRs; GAO reports funders
  self-reporting **93% ROIC since inception**. A 93% ROIC over four years is roughly an 18%
  IRR; over two, about 39%. Nobody states holding periods, so the headline figures in this
  debate are not measuring the same thing.
- **Loss rates are not public**, so nobody can say whether the returns compensate the risk.
- **Duration does not fit fund life**, which is why secondaries exist and why they trade at a
  discount nobody can benchmark.
- **The regulatory tail risk is existential, not marginal.** A 40.8% excise tax with no loss
  offset and no exemption for tax-exempt investors would remove the capital base outright.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The two sides measure different things and both are right.** Growth, insurance entry and
  product sophistication are real; so are the small absolute size, the absent pricing
  infrastructure and the unauditable marks. An asset class can be maturing in structure and
  immature in plumbing at once, and this one is.
- **Every published market figure has a different scope.** Westfleet measures **US commercial
  only**, excluding mass tort, consumer and non-US matters, on a July–June year. Swiss Re put
  **global** TPLF at $17bn including mass tort (38%) and personal injury (25%). Bloomberg
  quotes $13.5bn with no scope stated. **None of these are comparable**, and most arguments on
  this syllabus quote one without saying which.
- **The measurement is voluntary and unaudited.** Westfleet's data is blind third-party
  aggregation from funders who chose to participate; GAO obtained transaction data from four of
  twelve funders it approached. The industry's own size is self-reported.
- **What would settle it**: published loss rates, secondary pricing as a percentage of NAV, and
  a completed rated securitisation. None exists.

## Where the law is now

- **No securities regulation of the asset itself.** Funds are private placements under ordinary
  exemptions; the underlying positions are contracts, not securities.
- **No mandatory reporting**, which is why GAO could not size the market. Its six policy options
  included collection by the **SEC** or **CFPB**; none adopted.
- **No ratings.** No public rating on a US litigation finance securitisation.
- **Tax treatment is the live threat**, not securities law.
  [[Tax treatment of litigation funding]].
- **Insurance is regulated as insurance**, state by state — a more developed regime than
  anything governing funding, and part of why insurers offer the product at a lower cost of
  capital.
