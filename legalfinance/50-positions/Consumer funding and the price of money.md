## The question

A personal injury plaintiff takes $3,000 against a pending claim to cover rent. The advance
is non-recourse: if the case loses, nothing is repaid. The price is 18% of the amount funded,
applied every six months.

Is that a **loan**, to be measured against usury statutes and disclosed as an APR? Or is it
the purchase of a contingent interest, where the non-recourse feature justifies a price that
no lending statute would allow? The answer determines whether a whole industry is lawful in
a given state.

Marked `settling` rather than `live` because the legislative direction is now one-way, even
though the doctrinal question remains open.

## The case for regulating it as credit

- **The price is extraordinary by any credit measure.** GAO found rates "starting at 15
  percent of the amount funded" and, at another funder, "18 percent of the amount funded
  (applied every 6 months)" — compounding to roughly 39% a year. No APR is disclosed
  anywhere in the transaction.
- **The counterparty is the archetype of an unsophisticated borrower**: injured, out of
  work, needing money for living expenses rather than for the litigation, and usually
  without advice on the funding contract as distinct from the claim.
- **The non-recourse distinction is thinner than it looks in practice.** Advances are
  underwritten against cases already screened by a contingency-fee lawyer who has taken the
  case on its merits. The realised loss rate on a book of such advances is not the loss rate
  the pricing implies — which is the empirical heart of
  [[Skiba and Xiao - Consumer Litigation Funding as Payday Lending]].
- **The comparison is the argument.** Swiss Re: consumer contracts "should clearly disclose
  an annual percentage rate of interest, similar to credit cards and payday loans." If the
  product functions as short-term high-cost credit to distressed consumers, the regime
  designed for that product should apply.
- **The recovery gets consumed.** Where the advance runs for the life of a case averaging
  three to four years, compounding at these rates can exceed the settlement. That is the
  mechanism behind the claim that funded claimants net less.
  [[Pricing and returns in legal finance]].
- **Steinitz agrees intervention is needed**, though by a different route: **minimum
  returns** so "plaintiffs in consumer and aggregate litigation receive minimum returns to
  help combat predatory financing," expressly not extended to commercial parties. A floor on
  the claimant's share does directly what a rate cap does indirectly.
- **States have been acting**, which is itself evidence that legislatures find the credit
  characterisation persuasive.

## The case against

- **Non-recourse is a real difference, not a formality.** A lender is repaid regardless; a
  funder of a lost case receives nothing. Pricing that looks usurious on a single winning
  case is priced for a book that includes total losses, and capping the rate at a lending
  rate would make the product unavailable rather than cheaper.
- **The alternative is worse for the same person.** A plaintiff who cannot cover rent while
  a claim is pending settles early and cheap. The advance's function is to prevent exactly
  the distressed settlement that defendants otherwise obtain — a point the defence side's
  own access-to-justice critique cannot easily absorb.
- **APR is the wrong metric for a contingent instrument.** An annualised rate presumes a
  known term and a certain repayment. Here the term is the life of the case, which neither
  party controls, and repayment is conditional. Expressing it as an APR communicates
  precision that does not exist.
- **Rate caps drive out the regulated and leave the unregulated.** Arkansas's 17% cap and
  Tennessee's 10% annual fee cap are near or below what the product costs to provide at
  scale.
- **Nobody knows the loss rate**, so nobody can say whether the price is excessive or
  merely high. GAO could not obtain it; the funders do not publish it.
- **The commercial market is not this market**, and rules written here should not travel.
  [[Commercial versus consumer funding]].

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The prices are documented and not contested** — GAO has them from the funders
  themselves. This is the one place in the Class 1 reading where a hard number is available
  and both sides accept it.
- **The loss rate, which is the whole justification for the price, is not documented at
  all.** Everything turns on it and nobody has it. The productive version of this argument
  is: what loss rate would make 39% a year a fair price, and is there any reason to think
  the actual rate is that high on cases a contingency-fee lawyer has already accepted?
- **Avraham and Sebok** (Class 2) is the only empirical study of the consumer market on this
  syllabus, and **Skiba and Xiao** (Class 3) is the payday-lending comparison done
  seriously. Do not settle this file before reading them; the Class 1 material only frames
  it.
- **Swiss Re and Steinitz agree on the diagnosis and differ on the instrument** — rate caps
  and APR disclosure versus a minimum guaranteed share for the claimant. That is a real
  design choice: a cap regulates the price of the money, a floor regulates the outcome.

## Where the law is now

- **No federal regulation.** GAO identified the CFPB as a possible collection point for data
  but no existing authority is exercised over the product.
- **State statutes**, the operative regime:
  - **Arkansas** — interest capped at 17%.
  - **Tennessee** — annual fees capped at 10% of the original amount advanced.
  - **Maine** and **Nebraska** — registration with the state plus contract disclosure of the
    total amount the consumer must repay.
  - **West Virginia** — 18% cap (Swiss Re, p. 24).
  - **New York** — the Consumer Litigation Funding Act, 2025 N.Y. Laws ch. 645
    (S.1104-A/A.804-C) as amended by S.8808, is the newest and most detailed regime.
    Class 3. [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]].
- **Common law:** where champerty survives, it reaches consumer advances directly.
  [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] (Minn. 2020) abolished the doctrine in
  Minnesota; [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] (9th Cir. 2020) is the usury framing.
  Class 4.
