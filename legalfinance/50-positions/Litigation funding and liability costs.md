## The question

Liability claim severity has risen faster than economic inflation over the past decade, and
third-party funding grew over the same period. The question is whether funding **causes**
part of that rise — through longer cases, larger demands, or more claims brought — or
whether it is one correlate among several in a trend with other sources.

Note what turns on the answer. If funding merely finances claims that would have been
brought anyway, it is a private contract between the parties to a dispute. If it inflates
the cost of the liability system, the cost lands on policyholders who are not parties to
anything, and the argument for regulation stops depending on protecting claimants.

## The case for

- **The trend is real and large.** Awards over $5m rose from **29% to 37%** of large general
  liability awards (2010–2019); vehicle negligence from 22% to 29%; median general liability
  awards from **$8.2m to $10.3m**; trucking verdicts from **$2.3m to $22.3m** (2010–2018).
  2020 combined ratios above 100 across general liability, medical malpractice and
  commercial auto.
- **Funded cases last longer.** Swiss Re reports funded personal injury cases averaging
  **37 months** (2019) and **43 months** (2020) against **28.6 months** for typical civil
  cases. "[L]onger cases increase claim costs, on average, due to higher legal expenses and
  compound interest on the litigation finance."
- **Funding buys the inputs that raise awards.** It "is contributing to the surge in
  attorney advertising, and supporting plaintiff attorneys in investing in new lawsuit
  strategies, more discovery and mock trials." "Investing more time and money in discovery
  and advancing cases further through court proceedings can increase the likelihood of
  larger awards."
- **The incentive points the same way.** Where the funder's return is a multiple that steps
  up over time, and where its upside is a share of a larger award, the funder profits from
  duration and quantum in a way the claimant may not.
  [[Pricing and returns in legal finance]].
- **Portfolios finance claims that could not stand alone.** Steinitz reports funding of
  "non-meritorious cases when bundled in portfolios," and three stakeholders told GAO that
  funding "could increase litigation costs for defendants."
- **Settlements get blocked.** Both Steinitz and GAO record plaintiffs declining reasonable
  offers where a funder's return has to clear first — GAO: plaintiffs "may be inclined to
  reject a fair settlement offer."

## The case against

- **Nobody has shown causation, and the party arguing it concedes as much.** Swiss Re's own
  text acknowledges that funding interacts with "psychology-based strategies of the trial
  bar," changing juror attitudes and attorney advertising. What it offers is temporal
  correlation: "As TPLF has grown, so has the number of large motor and general liability
  verdicts."
- **The duration finding is probably selection.** Funders underwrite cases they expect to be
  large, contested and slow, because those are the cases worth the diligence cost. Funded
  cases lasting longer than the average civil case is what selection predicts, and no
  control for it is offered. This is the single strongest objection and it is available on
  the face of the report.
- **A residual is not a cause.** Social inflation is defined as what economic drivers do not
  explain, so it absorbs every unmodelled factor. [[Social inflation]].
- **Combined ratios are not a claims measure.** A ratio above 100 can reflect soft pricing,
  reserve strengthening for prior accident years, or reinsurance structure.
- **The same model that predicts more spending also predicts less.**
  [[Antill and Grenadier - Financing the Litigation Arms Race]] find that a funded plaintiff
  spends more and resists longer (deadweight loss up), **and** that a defendant facing a
  credibly capitalised plaintiff gets a lower marginal return on its own defensive spending,
  so it escalates less. The net effect on total surplus is **ambiguous** in a peer-reviewed
  finance journal by authors with no stake in the market. The deterrence channel has never
  been tested and predicts the opposite of what the defence bar asserts: defendants facing
  funded plaintiffs should spend **less** on defence.
- **The claim-generation channel is rebutted by the only large dataset that exists.**
  [[Avraham and Sebok - Empirical Investigation of Third Party Consumer Litigant Funding]]:
  funders rejected just over half of 191,144 applications, and applications arrived a median
  **308 days after injury** — "clients decide to file lawsuits for reasons that have nothing
  to do with funding." This bites on the consumer market, not on the firm-level lending
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] describes; see
  [[Non-meritorious claims and claim aggregation]].
- **The market is too small to carry the claim alone.** About **39 active commercial
  funders**, 12–15 driving most deal flow, **$16.1bn** AUM and **$2.8bn** of new commitments.
  Whatever is driving trucking verdicts from $2.3m to $22.3m, an industry of that size is not
  the whole of it.
- **Enforcement rising is not a cost — it may be the system working.**
  [[Bedi and Marra - The Shadows of Litigation Finance]] turn the argument around: if
  capital lets meritorious claims be brought that were previously priced out, more claims
  and larger recoveries are the correction of a prior under-enforcement, not an inflation of
  liability. A defendant who could previously rely on a claimant's inability to sue was
  receiving a subsidy.
- **The geographic evidence proves something else.** Swiss Re's finding that plaintiff-
  friendly counties correlate with low income, high unemployment and inequality is a
  finding about **juries and venues**, not about funding.
- **Burford's own threshold cuts against the mass-tort story.** Commercial funding at $3m
  minimum against $30m damages is not what drives volume in personal injury or trucking,
  which is where the verdict trend is sharpest.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The correlation is well documented; the causal channel is not tested.** Every mechanism
  Swiss Re asserts is plausible and none is measured against a counterfactual.
- **The duration number is the crux**, and it is confounded by selection in a way that is
  fatal unless controlled. Any serious use of it has to address this.
- **GAO could not obtain deal terms at all** from eight of twelve funders it approached, so
  the data needed to construct a matched comparison is not merely absent from the
  literature — it is not held by anyone who will share it.
- **The award-trend data is the most solid thing in the debate**, and it is also the least
  contested. Both sides agree verdicts are up. The disagreement is entirely about
  attribution.
- Watch the unit of analysis. Swiss Re's verdict data is **personal injury, auto and
  trucking**; its market-size and IRR data covers **all segments**; and much of the
  commercial-funding advocacy concerns a product that does not operate in those case types
  at all. [[Commercial versus consumer funding]].

## Where the law is now

- No jurisdiction regulates funding on the ground that it inflates liability costs. The
  legislative activity runs through **disclosure** and **consumer rate caps** instead.
- The federal proposals that would bite are in later classes: the **Litigation Funding
  Transparency Act** (Swiss Re endorses it at p. 22; the 2026 version is S. 3826, Class 8)
  and the **Tackling Predatory Litigation Funding Act**, S. 1821, which attacks the economics
  through an excise tax rather than through disclosure (Class 5).
- State tort reform measures aimed at verdict size — damages caps, bifurcation, evidentiary
  limits on medical billing — operate on the trend directly and without reference to
  funding.
