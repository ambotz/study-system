## The question

A funder with money at risk in a lawsuit has views about how it is run and when it settles.
The question is whether those views translate into **control** — over strategy, over
counsel, or over the decision to settle — and whether the answer is fixed by the funding
agreement, by professional conduct rules, or by the economics regardless of what the
documents say.

Two propositions are constantly run together and need separating: *funders do not take
control rights*, and *funders cannot take control rights*.

## The case for (funders do control, or effectively do)

- **The economics create an interest adverse to the client's.** A non-recourse funder is
  paid only on success and paid more on a larger recovery, often with a multiple that steps
  up over time. The client's interest is in the **net** recovery and in finality; the
  funder's is in the gross and in the multiple. Those diverge most sharply at exactly the
  moment that matters — a settlement offer that is good for the client and thin for the
  funder.
- **Documented interference.** Steinitz lists, among harms to plaintiffs, "interference with
  attorney-client relationships," "loss of client autonomy and control over settlement
  decisions," and "conflicts between funder and client interests." She is not describing a
  hypothetical.
- **Settlements blocked.** From the defendant's side she reports the "inability to settle
  when third-party funders block settlements." GAO records the same phenomenon from the
  other direction: plaintiffs "may be inclined to reject a fair settlement offer."
- **The New Jersey rule assumes it happens.** D.N.J. L. Civ. R. 7.1.1 requires a statement
  describing "whether the funder's approval is needed for litigation or settlement
  decisions" — a disclosure requirement drafted around the possibility that the answer is
  yes. [[New Jersey Local Civil Rule 7.1.1]].
- **Portfolio structures spread the pressure.** Where matters are cross-collateralised, a
  funder short of its return on the pool has an economic interest in outcomes across cases
  it never underwrote individually.
  [[Portfolio finance and cross-collateralisation]].
- **Informal influence is not covered by a "no control rights" clause.** A funder that
  controls further draws, or whose consent is needed to add matters to a portfolio, has
  leverage without ever holding a consent right over settlement.

## The case against (funders are passive investors)

- **The industry's own terms disclaim it.** Burford: "Use of legal finance does not alter
  control of decision-making or attorney-client relationships. Burford is a passive outside
  investor…we have no rights to manage the litigation in which we invest." And: no "rights
  to control settlement of the litigation, which remains wholly in the litigant's purview."
- **The leasing analogy.** "Just as a leasing company does not tell you how to drive your
  car, legal finance companies don't drive the litigation."
- **Professional conduct rules already bind the lawyer.** ABA Model Rule 5.4 protects the
  lawyer's independent professional judgment from a third party who pays, and Rule 1.2
  reserves the settlement decision to the client. Whatever a funding agreement says, counsel
  cannot take direction from the funder. Class 4 tests how far that carries.
- **Sophisticated commercial clients negotiate these terms.** A general counsel with outside
  advisers who concedes a settlement consent right has made a priced choice, not been
  imposed upon. The autonomy concern is real for an individual plaintiff and much weaker for
  a Fortune 100 claimant.
- **Alignment more often than conflict.** For most of a case's life the funder and the
  claimant want the same thing: to win, efficiently. The divergence is confined to a
  specific zone of settlement values.
- **No court has held the standard commercial structure improper on control grounds.**

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The disagreement is partly definitional.** Burford's statement describes the rights it
  takes in its own agreements. Steinitz describes the market including funders who take
  more. Both can be accurate.
- **Nobody has read a representative sample of funding agreements**, because they are
  confidential and GAO could not obtain deal terms. Every claim about what these documents
  contain is an extrapolation from a handful of disputes and from what the funders publish.
- **The clearest evidence is structural rather than empirical**: the New Jersey rule exists
  because courts thought consent rights plausible enough to ask about.
- **The live test case is on this syllabus.** The Sysco–Burford dispute in Class 4 is a real
  fight over a funder's role in settlement, ending with Sysco assigning its antitrust claims
  to a Burford affiliate. Read [[Burford - Legal Finance 101]]'s control language against
  what happened there. [[Frankel - Sysco and Burford]].
- **Parikh supplies two documented instances and a structural reason.**
  [[Parikh - Opaque Capital and Mass-Tort Financing]] reports Capital-Provision Agreements
  with **individual** claimants carrying settlement vetoes and restrictions on changing
  counsel, citing *Boling v. Prospect Funding Holdings*, and describes Sysco as "rendered
  helpless" by a settlement veto. His structural point is sharper than either anecdote: in
  mass tort financing the money goes to the **firm**, so the claimant is not a party to the
  agreement that constrains their case and has no one owing them a duty as to it.
- **Two industries, one word.** Burford underwrites single commercial claims at a $3m floor
  with a passive posture; the capital Parikh describes lends against a firm's book and takes
  veto rights over individual settlements. Both descriptions can be accurate, and most of the
  disagreement in the literature is people describing different products.
- **The remedy question is separate from the fact question.** Steinitz does not propose
  banning control; she proposes **fiduciary duties** on funders "to act in the best
  interests of the funded client" — which would restructure the economics rather than the
  documents.

## Where the law is now

- **No US statute** regulates funder control of **commercial** litigation.
- **Consumer funding in New York is now regulated by statute.**
  [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]] provides that the funder "shall have no role
  in deciding whether, when and how much" the claim settles for, that the attorney retains
  independent professional judgment, and that the company may not "interfere." It also bans
  colluding with lawyers to induce a client to terminate counsel. So for consumer funding in
  New York the question is answered by legislation rather than by contract or by a funder's
  self-description.
- **Professional conduct rules bind the lawyer, not the funder.** ABA Model Rules 5.4 and
  1.2; NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2018-5 addresses the related fee-interest question. Class 4.
- **Disclosure rules reach it indirectly.** D.N.J. L. Civ. R. 7.1.1 (June 2021) requires
  disclosure of whether funder approval is needed for litigation or settlement decisions.
  N.D. Cal. (Nov. 2018) requires funder identity in class, collective and representative
  actions but not the control terms.
- **Champerty doctrine**, where it survives, is partly about control of another's suit —
  which is why Class 4's [[Maslowski v. Prospect Funding Partners]] and
  [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] matter here and not only there.
- **Arizona** applies professional conduct rules to nonlawyers holding economic interests in
  law firms, the precedent Steinitz points to. Class 7 asks whether it transfers.
