## The question

A funder is paid only if the claimant recovers, so the interests are broadly aligned — until
they are not. At a settlement offer that is good for the claimant and thin for the funder,
the two want different things, and the funder often holds contractual leverage over the
outcome.

Should the law impose a duty on the funder to act in the claimant's interests, as it does on
the lawyer? Or is the relationship a negotiated commercial contract, to be enforced as
written?

This is the reform that the most sources independently converge on, and it is not the one
Congress is debating.

## The case for fiduciary duties

- **Steinitz proposes exactly this to Congress**, as one of three structural measures:
  fiduciary duties on funders "to act in the best interests of the funded client," analogised
  to Arizona's application of professional conduct rules to nonlawyers with economic interests
  in law firms.
- **Parikh arrives at the same instrument from the opposite direction.** His concern is the
  claimant, not the defendant, and his reform list ends with fiduciary obligations on
  financiers. **Two authors with different worries converging on one remedy** is the strongest
  signal on this syllabus that it is the live reform.
- **The harms are documented, not hypothetical.** Steinitz reports interference with the
  attorney–client relationship, loss of settlement autonomy, and funder–client conflicts.
  Parikh cites *Boling v. Prospect Funding Holdings* on Capital-Provision Agreements
  empowering financiers to "interfere with or discourage settlement," and Sysco "rendered
  helpless" by a consent right.
- **Sysco is the proof of concept and it involved a Fortune 100 company.** If a sophisticated
  claimant with elite counsel could not settle its own claims, the argument that negotiation
  protects claimants is weak everywhere below that. [[Frankel - Sysco and Burford]].
- **Nobody owes a duty where it is most needed.** In mass tort financing the money goes to the
  **firm**; the claimant is not a party to the agreement that constrains their case and has no
  one accountable to them for it. [[Mass tort financing and claim aggregation]].
- **The alignment argument is weaker than it looks.** A funder's return is often a multiple
  stepping up with time, so duration helps the funder and hurts the claimant. Alignment on
  *winning* is not alignment on *when to stop*.
- **The leasing analogy fails.** Burford: "[j]ust as a leasing company does not tell you how
  to drive your car, legal finance companies don't drive the litigation." But a leasing
  company is paid either way; a non-recourse funder is paid only on success and more on a
  bigger win. That is a contingent interest in the outcome, which is the classic trigger for a
  duty.
- **The profession already does this.** A contingency-fee lawyer has the same economic
  interest and is a fiduciary who can be disbarred. Treating funders differently is a
  distinction of form.

## The case against

- **The best-argued academic position is contract, not fiduciary.**
  [[Sebok and Wendel - Duty in the Litigation-Investment Agreement]]: the relationship "[is]
  sufficiently different from that of an insurer and an insured such that tort law should not
  be followed." These are **relational contracts** requiring renegotiation over the life of a
  case, not a duty fixed at signing.
- **The dependence runs the other way.** After closing, the funder is the vulnerable party —
  its capital is committed and illiquid, and it cannot observe the claimant's effort. The four
  risks Sebok and Wendel identify (information asymmetry, shirking, control, forgone
  opportunities) all describe the **claimholder's** ability to hurt the **funder**. A duty
  running only toward the claimant misdescribes the relationship.
- **Sophisticated parties can price these terms.** A general counsel with outside advisers who
  accepts a settlement consent right has made a priced choice.
- **A duty would break the pricing model.** If the funder must act in the claimant's best
  interests, it cannot press for the multiple its investors were promised. The non-recourse
  structure exists because the funder is an investor; make it a fiduciary and the economics
  need rebuilding from scratch. Steinitz proposes this without addressing it.
- **Duties invite satellite litigation** over every declined settlement, at cost to both
  parties.
- **The industry's position is that no duty is needed because no control is taken.** Burford:
  "Burford is a passive outside investor…we have no rights to manage the litigation in which
  we invest."
- **Legislatures are choosing a narrower tool.** New York did not impose a fiduciary duty; it
  **banned the control right** for consumers outright and made the plaintiff's own lawyer the
  gatekeeper. That is a cheaper fix aimed at the same problem.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The disagreement tracks claimant type, not doctrine.** Sebok and Wendel model
  **sophisticated commercial** parties and reach contract. Steinitz and Parikh are concerned
  with **consumers and mass tort claimants** and reach fiduciary duties. Both may be right; a
  general theory of the litigation-investment agreement that ignores who is on the other side
  is not really general. Notice that Sebok and Wendel concede their frame suits parties who
  negotiate.
- **Control rights do exist in the market**, whatever the marketing says. The Sysco CPA's
  amendment — Sysco "shall not accept a settlement offer without [Burford's] prior written
  consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld" — is documented, and an LCIA tribunal
  **enforced it by injunction**, reasoning that parties may "contract that right away."
- **"Unreasonably withheld" is not a solution.** Sysco wanted to settle to preserve supplier
  relationships; Burford wanted to maximise the recovery. Reasonableness has no content when
  the parties are optimising different variables — which is an argument that consent rights
  need *status* regulation rather than contractual qualification.
- **Nobody has sampled the agreements.** They are confidential, GAO could not obtain deal
  terms, and every claim about what funding contracts contain is extrapolated from a handful
  of disputes. The strongest evidence remains **structural**: the D.N.J. rule requires
  disclosure of whether funder approval is needed for settlement, which means courts thought
  it plausible enough to ask.
- **The remedy question is separable from the fact question.** Even granting the harms, the
  choice is between a fiduciary duty, a statutory ban on control rights, mandatory attorney
  gatekeeping, and disclosure. New York picked the last three and skipped the first.

## Where the law is now

- **No US jurisdiction imposes fiduciary duties on litigation funders.**
- **New York bans the control right for consumer funding**: the funder "shall have no role in
  deciding whether, when and how much" the claim settles for, and may not "interfere" with the
  attorney's independent professional judgment. Contract **void** without an attorney
  acknowledgment. [[New York Consumer Litigation Funding Act]].
- **Commercial funding is governed by contract**, subject to champerty and unconscionability
  where they survive. [[Policing funding agreements]].
- **Courts police control at the margin.** Minnesota: it is "difficult to conceive of any
  stipulation more against public policy" than requiring a financier's permission to settle —
  though an LCIA tribunal applying New York law reached the opposite result in the Sysco
  arbitration.
- **Arizona** applies professional conduct rules to nonlawyers holding economic interests in
  law firms, the precedent Steinitz points to. Class 7 tests whether it transfers.
- **Proposed but not enacted**: Steinitz's fiduciary duties, minimum returns for consumer and
  aggregate claimants, and non-arbitrability of funding disputes — the last of which would
  have changed the Sysco outcome directly.
