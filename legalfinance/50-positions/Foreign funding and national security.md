## The question

Some litigation funding capital comes from outside the United States, and some of that from
foreign states or sovereign wealth funds. The question is whether that warrants a separate
regime — identification by status, a public register, restrictions on who may fund — or whether
it is an ordinary conflicts and disclosure problem being used to carry a broader agenda.

It matters procedurally as much as substantively. National security broadens the coalition for
measures that would otherwise split along plaintiff and defendant lines, so the framing itself
is doing political work regardless of whether the underlying concern is real.

## The case for a distinct regime

- **Litigation is an instrument of state power, and the federal courts are the venue.** A
  foreign state funding suits against US companies — in patent, antitrust or trade-adjacent
  disputes — can impose costs, extract discovery, and shape doctrine without ever appearing.
- **The existing machinery does not see it.** Nothing in the FRCP requires a party to say who is
  funding it, so a foreign sovereign interest is invisible unless a judge asks.
  [[Disclosure of funding agreements]].
- **Discovery is the specific exposure.** LCJ found contracts requiring plaintiffs to share
  discovery material with funders. Where the funder is a foreign state-controlled entity, a
  protective order becomes a channel rather than a barrier — which is why S. 3826 restricts
  funder access to protected discovery material without court authorisation.
- **Steinitz's own framework makes funder identity relevant.** Her factor 2 lists "foreign
  governments" among funder profiles whose motivations — including **foreign policy** — bear on
  disclosure. She does not treat foreign status as irrelevant; she declines to make it the
  organising principle.
- **Legislation exists and is bipartisan-adjacent in framing.** S. 3826 requires identification
  of foreign states, foreign persons, sovereign wealth funds and foreign-controlled enterprises,
  with an Administrative Office register published every **120 days** naming cases, courts and
  amounts.
- **Disclosure by status is cheap and narrow** compared with producing every agreement in every
  case.

## The case against

- **The leading independent expert, asked directly by the committee convening on this subject,
  declined to endorse the premise.** Steinitz told House Oversight that anticompetitive and
  foreign-interest concerns are "**hypothetical and future-looking**," that these "outlier
  scenarios" "**should not drive regulation**," and that judges should retain authority to
  address them when they actually arise.
- **No evidence is cited.** Neither the bill's coverage nor the advocacy identifies a case in
  which foreign sovereign funding harmed US interests. The premise is asserted, and GAO — which
  went looking for market data — reported that the information does not exist.
- **Status is not conduct.** A public register keyed to *who* the funder is rather than *what
  the funding does* regulates identity. A domestic hedge fund with a settlement veto and a
  short position in the defendant, which is Parikh's **crossholder** scenario, is a more
  concrete harm than an offshore pension fund with a passive interest — and the bill reaches the
  second, not the first.
- **A public register is the most intrusive disclosure setting Steinitz identifies**, and the
  bill selects it categorically rather than case by case. Publishing case names, courts and
  funding amounts every 120 days is a permanent public consequence attached to the identity of a
  capital source.
- **Capital is fungible and structures are easy.** A fund with foreign limited partners, a
  domestic manager and a Delaware vehicle is not obviously "foreign-funded," and drafting to
  catch it reaches ordinary institutional capital — including the pension money already exposed
  by the excise tax proposal.
- **The coalition is the tell.** The same four senators sponsor the disclosure bill and the
  **40.8% excise tax**. Both instruments burden the same industry, and only one of them is about
  foreign states.
- **Access to justice runs the other way.** Foreign claimants with meritorious claims against US
  defendants need funding more than domestic ones, not less.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **Almost nothing, in either direction.** No public data on what share of US litigation funding
  is foreign-sourced, let alone state-sourced. Westfleet excludes non-US matters entirely and
  does not report investor domicile; GAO could not obtain deal terms from eight of the twelve
  funders it approached.
- **The one specific harm anyone documents is contractual, not national.** Funder access to
  discovery material appears in LCJ's nine contracts. That is a real problem and it is
  **nationality-neutral** — the fix is a restriction on discovery sharing, which S. 3826 also
  contains and which would work identically against a domestic funder.
- **The strongest version of the concern is conflicts, not espionage.** A judge cannot recuse
  from an interest they cannot see, which is the rationale six circuits already give for
  requiring disclosure of "any person or organization with a financial interest in the
  litigation." That argument does not depend on foreignness at all.
- **What would settle it**: any systematic accounting of funder domicile in US litigation. None
  exists, and the register S. 3826 creates would be the first — which is an argument for the
  disclosure and against the conclusions currently drawn without it.

## Where the law is now

- **No federal statute** regulates foreign litigation funding. S. 3826 is the proposal.
- **Six circuits** require disclosure of any person or organisation with a financial interest,
  for recusal purposes — nationality-neutral.
- **N.D. Cal.** (2018) requires funder identity in class, collective and representative actions;
  **D.N.J.** (2021) requires identity, control terms and the nature of the interest.
  [[New Jersey Local Civil Rule 7.1.1]]. Neither asks about domicile.
- **CFIUS and FARA reach foreign influence in other channels** and neither is directed at
  litigation funding.
- **The Advisory Committee's TPLF Subcommittee** is considering disclosure generally; the
  LCJ/ILR Rule 26 proposal is nationality-neutral and would reach foreign funders incidentally.
- **State statutes** regulate consumer funding and do not address funder domicile.
