## The question

Litigation is expensive, and some people with meritorious claims cannot afford to bring
them. Third-party capital changes who can sue. The question is whether the claimants who
gain access are better off for it once the price of that capital is counted — and whether
the claimants who gain access are the ones the access argument is about.

## The case for

- **Some claims are simply not brought without it.** A party that cannot fund a case either
  does not sue or settles early and cheap against a better-capitalised opponent. GAO
  recorded this as the most commonly asserted benefit: eight funders, three trade
  associations and one academic researcher told it that "TPLF can help ensure that
  plaintiffs with limited resources have the funding they need to litigate their cases."
- **Risk transfer is itself the benefit**, separate from cash. Non-recourse capital moves
  downside risk to a party that can diversify it. A claimant who could technically afford
  the case but cannot afford to lose it is just as constrained as one with no money.
  [[Burford - Legal Finance 101]]; [[Non-recourse capital]].
- **Better counsel, not just any counsel.** Steinitz lists this first among the benefits:
  funding lets a plaintiff hire lawyers the defendant already has.
- **The pre-claim shadow.** [[Bedi and Marra - The Shadows of Litigation Finance]] argues
  the access gain is larger than the litigated cases show, because a credible prospect of
  enforcement deters inefficient breach, increases contracting, and lowers the enforcement
  risk premium in contract design. On this view, cases never brought because the breach
  never happened are the largest access benefit and are invisible.
- **It is not new in kind.** Contingency fees, pro bono representation and ordinary
  corporate borrowing all shift enforcement capacity to the under-resourced. Objecting to
  funding on access grounds requires explaining why those are different.
- **Ideological breadth.** Steinitz notes funding supports impact litigation across the
  political spectrum — a point that cuts against treating it as a plaintiffs'-bar
  phenomenon.

## The case against

- **Claimants net less.** Swiss Re estimates plaintiffs receive about **43%** of costs and
  compensation in funded cases against **55%** unfunded — "TPLF reduces plaintiff
  compensation by more than a fifth (21%)." If access means recovery, funded access buys
  less of it.
- **The product does not reach the people the argument is about.** Burford's own threshold
  is **$3m in financing against roughly $30m of expected damages**. Commercial funding is
  unavailable to any individual and to most small businesses. Defending it on access
  grounds describes a different product than the one being sold.
- **Neuberger identified the same gap in 2013 and nothing has closed it.** CFAs, DBAs and
  third-party funding "cannot do the business where the claim is small," and for those claimants
  his answer was "quick and rough justice" rather than capital.
- **Class 9 supplies the first proposed answer, and it is technological rather than legal.**
  [[Marra - AI and the Future of Litigation Funding]] argues the binding constraint was never
  claim size but **diligence cost per matter**, which is roughly fixed — and that machine
  learning relaxes it, letting funders "profitably finance smaller cases previously overlooked
  due to review costs." Qanlex already writes **$100,000 to $3m** per case using AI screening,
  an order of magnitude below the incumbent floor. If that generalises, the access argument
  stops being a category error; if it does not, the gap is permanent.
  [[Can AI price legal claims]].
- **Where it does reach individuals, the price is the problem.** Consumer advances of
  $1,000–$10,000 at 15% to 18% of the amount funded applied every six months are access on
  terms that consume the recovery. [[Consumer funding and the price of money]].
- **The cost reaches people who never contracted.** Swiss Re's framing: an "opaque,
  bottom-up wealth transfer from consumers to sophisticated investors and law firms,"
  because liability costs arrive as premiums. Access for one claimant is funded by
  policyholders generally.
- **Access is not the same as a good outcome.** Steinitz documents loss of client autonomy,
  interference with the attorney–client relationship, and settlements the client wants but
  cannot take. A plaintiff who gets into court and loses control of the case has been given
  access to something less than their own claim.
- **The cheaper alternatives are not tried.** Swiss Re argues legal aid and legal expense
  insurance deliver access at lower cost, citing Germany and Ireland, and markets where
  legal expense insurance is widespread showing "higher levels of access to justice."

## What the evidence actually shows

- **Very little, and both sides overstate.** GAO — the only disinterested body to
  investigate — could not determine market size, deal terms or returns, and reported that
  "there are gaps in the availability of market data." Steinitz, from the opposite
  institutional position, says the same: "there is almost no empirical data about litigation
  funding."
- **The 43%/55% comparison is an estimate by the reinsurer with the most to gain from it.**
  A defensible version would require matched funded and unfunded cases with similar injuries
  in similar jurisdictions. That study does not exist. Do not cite the 21% as a finding.
- **The pre-claim effects are modelled, not measured.** No data is offered on breach rates,
  contracting volume or risk premia.
- **The one claim with real support is the narrowest**: commercial funding is unavailable
  below a high threshold. That comes from the funder's own marketing and is not contested by
  anyone.
- Note that both the strongest pro claim (pre-claim shadow) and the strongest con claim
  (21% reduction) are unverified, and that the parties making them have opposite interests.

## Where the law is now

- **No federal regulation of the industry as such.** GAO: TPLF "is not specifically
  regulated under U.S. federal law."
- **Consumer funding is regulated by some states**: Arkansas caps interest at 17%,
  Tennessee caps annual fees at 10% of the advance, and Maine and Nebraska require
  registration plus contract disclosure of the total repayable. Class 3 adds New York's
  2025 Consumer Litigation Funding Act.
- **Commercial funding is left to contract**, subject to champerty and usury doctrine in
  those states that retain it. Class 4.
- No US jurisdiction conditions funding on a minimum share reaching the claimant. Steinitz
  proposes one for consumer and aggregate litigation.
