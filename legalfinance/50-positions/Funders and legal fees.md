## The question

Client-side funding is uncontroversial as a matter of professional responsibility: the client
borrows, the lawyer is not a party, Rule 5.4 is not engaged.

The contested case is the other one. May a **law firm** finance itself by granting a funder a
return that rises and falls with the legal fees it earns — in one matter, in a defined set of
matters, or across its book?

[[NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2018-5]] says no. Most of the commercial market wants yes, and is
structuring around the answer.

## The case against (the current rule)

- **Rule 5.4(a) is categorical**: "A lawyer or law firm shall not share legal fees with a
  nonlawyer." NYC Bar Formal Op. 2018-5 concludes that a financing agreement under which "the
  lawyer's future payments to the funder are contingent on the lawyer's receipt of legal fees
  or on the amount of legal fees received in one or more specific matters" violates it.
- **Form does not save it.** Calling the arrangement a non-recourse loan rather than a fee
  split describes the same economics: the funder is paid out of, and in proportion to, legal
  fees.
- **The purpose is prophylactic** — to "remove incentives for nonlawyers to interfere with
  professional judgment." It forbids the structure because of the incentive, not on proof that
  anyone interfered, which is how conflict rules are normally built.
- **The interference risk is not theoretical.** In [[Frankel - Sysco and Burford]] Sysco
  terminated Boies Schiller believing the firm was "violating its fiduciary duties by
  assisting Burford in impeding the settlements." Whatever the truth, that is the scenario the
  rule exists to prevent — and the firm was later engaged by the funder's own entity.
- **A clean alternative exists.** Recourse lending at a fixed rate is permitted and gives
  firms outside capital without any fee link.
- **The committee declined to fix it by interpretation.** "That is a matter to be decided by
  the state judiciary" — a deliberate refusal to create a funder carve-out.

## The case for allowing it

- **The line is about privity, not incentives.** A client-side funder and a fee-side funder
  have the same economic interest in the same lawsuit's outcome. The only difference is
  whether the lawyer signed. If independence is the concern, the rule is drawn in the wrong
  place.
- **Firms cannot raise capital any other way.** Rules 5.4 and 5.6 together mean a US firm
  cannot sell equity and cannot stop its owners walking out, so its only permanent capital is
  partner capital that can be withdrawn. [[The law firm as a capital structure]];
  [[Morley - Why Law Firms Collapse]].
- **Contingency fees already do this.** A lawyer's own compensation varies with the outcome,
  and that is thought to *align* rather than corrupt. It is hard to explain why a nonlawyer
  sharing the same variable is categorically worse.
- **Recourse lending does not fit the asset.** A contingency book generates no cash until it
  resolves; fixed-rate recourse debt has to be serviced meanwhile. The permitted alternative
  is the one that does not match the business.
- **The prohibition is parochial.** England and Australia permit outside ownership of firms;
  Arizona abolished Rule 5.4 in 2020; Utah runs a sandbox. None has reported the collapse of
  professional independence.
- **The market has routed around it anyway.** Revenue-based facilities, recourse structures
  and **MSO** arrangements deliver similar economics. A rule that changes the paperwork but
  not the substance buys less independence than it costs.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The scope of 2018-5 is narrower than it is usually reported.** The prohibition attaches
  where repayment is contingent on fees "in one or more **specific matters**." A facility
  secured on a firm's **general revenues** is a different case, and the opinion does not decide
  it. Much of the post-2018 structuring lives in that gap.
- **It is persuasive authority, not binding.** A city bar committee interpreting the New York
  rules — no court has ruled, and no discipline has followed on these facts. It nonetheless
  shapes how New York deals are papered, which is most commercial deals.
- **Fast Trak is the unlitigated test case.** The secondary contracts in
  [[Fast Trak Investment Co v. Sax]] took a contingent interest in attorney Sax's fees across
  five to ten **unrelated** matters — squarely the structure 2018-5 forbids. The Ninth Circuit
  certified a **usury** question and nobody reached Rule 5.4. The two Class 4 group readings
  converge on one transaction and the syllabus splits them into different groups.
- **No empirical claim is made on either side** that fee-side funding has caused interference,
  or that its absence has protected anyone. The debate is entirely about incentive design.
- **Follow the capital after 2018 to see what the rule actually did.** If firms cannot pledge
  fees in identified matters, money reaches them through recourse debt, revenue facilities, or
  the MSO structures in Class 7 — where a nonlawyer-owned management company charges for
  services instead of taking a share of legal fees. Texas Ethics Opinion 706 is the next
  regulator to look at whether that survives the rule.

## Where the law is now

- **ABA Model Rule 5.4(a)** and its state analogues bar fee sharing with nonlawyers; Rule
  5.4(d) bars nonlawyer ownership. In force in every US jurisdiction except Arizona and, in
  limited form, Utah.
- **NYC Bar Formal Op. 2018-5** (2018): a funder's contingent interest in a lawyer's fees in
  specified matters violates 5.4(a). Client-side funding does not. Recourse loans at fixed
  rates are permitted. No rule change recommended.
- **ABA Model Rule 1.8(e)**: a lawyer shall not provide financial assistance to a client in
  connection with pending or contemplated litigation, subject to the court-costs and
  indigent-client exceptions — the mirror-image restriction that pushes capital toward third
  parties.
- **Arizona**: Rule 5.4 abolished in 2020; ABS entities licensed with nonlawyer ownership,
  subject to professional conduct rules applied to the owners.
- **Utah**: time-limited regulatory sandbox.
- **UK and Australia**: outside ownership permitted; listed firms exist.
- **MSO structures**: the live US workaround, addressed by **Texas Committee on Professional
  Ethics Opinion No. 706** (Feb. 2025) on fee arrangements between firms and nonlawyer-owned
  MSOs. Class 7.
