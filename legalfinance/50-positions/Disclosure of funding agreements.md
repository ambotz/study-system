## The question

There is no general requirement in US federal litigation to reveal that a case is funded,
who is funding it, or on what terms. The question is whether there should be — and if so,
what exactly is disclosed (existence, funder identity, or the agreement itself), to whom
(the court, the opposing party, or the public), and in which cases.

Class 8 is built on this. Class 1 establishes the three starting positions, and they are not
the two you would expect: the defence side wants disclosure, the funders oppose it, and the
leading academic opposes a **uniform rule** while favouring intervention.

## The case for

- **Courts need to know who is really before them.** Steinitz, who opposes blanket
  disclosure, still grants the premise: "how, why, and to whose benefit judicial resources,
  as taxpayer funded resources, are used is a matter of public concern," and the real parties
  in interest should be identifiable for process integrity.
- **Due process.** Defendants have an interest in identifying their actual adversary,
  including for conflicts and recusal. Steinitz lists "potential violations of due process
  rights when funders are undisclosed" among harms to defendants.
- **Judicial conflicts are unmanageable otherwise.** A judge cannot recuse from an interest
  they cannot see. This is the argument that carried in
  [[In re Nimitz Technologies]] (Class 8).
- **Settlement cannot be evaluated blind.** If funder approval is required for settlement, a
  defendant negotiating in good faith is negotiating with someone who is not in the room.
  [[Funder control over the case]].
- **It is the precondition for everything else.** GAO's central finding is that the data to
  evaluate this market does not exist. Disclosure is the only mechanism that generates it as
  a by-product of litigation rather than depending on voluntary cooperation — and voluntary
  cooperation produced deal data from four of twelve funders.
- **Swiss Re's version**: "The mere fact that litigation funding may be abused suggests that
  such agreements should be scrutinised," endorsing the Litigation Funding Transparency Act.
- **The 1970 insurance amendment is the precedent, and it is the strongest argument for a
  rule.** [[LCJ and ILR - Proposal to amend Rule 26 on TPLF disclosure]]: courts were similarly
  split before 1970, the same objections were raised, and "the Committee resolved this issue in
  favor of disclosure."
- **Control rights are documented, not hypothetical.** LCJ examined **nine actual funding
  agreements** and reports terms letting funders take over the litigation on alleged breach,
  **veto settlements**, direct replacement of counsel, require plaintiffs to pay penalties and
  sanctions, and **mandate sharing of discovery documents with the funder**. "Zombie
  litigation" is their name for cases that continue because a hidden funder holds the veto.
- **Courts are already doing it without waiting.** [[In re Nimitz Technologies]] upheld a
  district judge's authority to investigate ownership, funding and real parties in interest —
  concerns "related to potential legal issues in the case" — against a mandamus petition
  seeking to shut the inquiry down.
- **It already works in two districts.** N.D. Cal. and D.N.J. have operated disclosure rules
  since 2018 and 2021 without the market collapsing.

## The case against

- **Commercial finance is not ordinarily discoverable.** Burford's framing: courts have
  implicitly recognised that "commercial legal finance arrangements are like any other type
  of corporate finance," and a defendant does not get to see how a plaintiff financed its
  operations.
- **Work product.** Burford argues that diligence materials are protected, and that the
  agreement itself is too, because its terms "reflect the information provided in work
  product protected documents, such as lawyers' mental impressions, theories and
  strategies." Disclosing the price of a funding deal reveals an assessment of the case's
  merits and expected value.
- **Asymmetry.** Disclosure is demanded of funded plaintiffs and not of self-funded
  defendants or their insurers, whose reserves are equally informative and equally
  protected.
- **It becomes a satellite litigation industry.** Every disclosure rule generates motions
  about scope, privilege and completeness, which raises cost for the party with less of it.
- **Steinitz's objection is different and more interesting.** She does not defend opacity;
  she says the phenomenon "is too variable to be addressed by a one-size-fits-all disclosure
  rule." What rule could account for the difference between "a corporate plaintiff whose legal
  costs are partially covered by a sophisticated investor who has arranged with the
  corporation's law firm to fund a portfolio of cases" and "a fired factory worker whose civil
  rights case is funded by a small startup"? Her answer in
  [[Steinitz - Follow the Money]] is a **balancing test** over eight factors, deciding four
  variables separately — *what* is disclosed, *to whom*, *when*, and *for what purpose* —
  revisited as the case develops.
- **The disclosure asymmetry is real and unaddressed.** A funded plaintiff discloses its
  financing; a self-funded corporate defendant discloses nothing about its litigation reserves
  or defence budget. Steinitz's version: mandatory disclosure gives defendants an "unfair
  advantage by getting a free look at plaintiffs' financial affairs" and imposes "more barriers
  to entry for claimants trying to bring meritorious lawsuits."
- **The premise of the security framing is weak.** She calls the anticompetitive and foreign-
  interest concerns "hypothetical and future-looking," and says these "outlier scenarios"
  "should not drive regulation" — notable because she said it to the committee convening the
  hearing on that subject.

## What the evidence actually shows

- **The baseline is not in dispute.** GAO: "There is no nationwide requirement to disclose
  litigation funding agreements to courts or opposing parties in U.S. federal litigation."
  Burford concedes the same descriptively: "The vast majority of courts do not require
  disclosure of legal finance arrangements in commercial matters."
- **Nobody has evaluated the two operating rules.** N.D. Cal. has run since November 2018
  and D.N.J. since June 2021, and no reading on this syllabus reports what they produced — how
  often funding was disclosed, whether (b) motions followed, whether satellite litigation
  materialised. **LCJ and ILR argue that automatic disclosure forecloses discovery forays and
  cite no data from either district**; Steinitz argues a rule will misfire and cites none
  either. Five years of New Jersey dockets sit unexamined while both sides argue predictively.
  This is the cleanest empirical opening in the course.
- **The evidence base on both sides is thin in the same way.** LCJ's **nine contracts** are a
  convenience sample of unstated provenance in a market that splits sharply between commercial,
  consumer and mass tort products. Nine agreements containing veto rights establish that such
  rights exist — which nobody disputes after Sysco — not how common they are. Meanwhile GAO
  approached twelve funders and obtained deal terms from four.
- **The work product question is unresolved and load-bearing.** Steinitz flags the split and
  declines to settle it (p. 1082 n.38); [[In re Nimitz Technologies]] routes it into **in camera
  review, document by document**, without deciding it. So the operating answer is **procedure
  substituting for doctrine** — which works case by case and settles nothing.
  [[Work product and privilege in litigation funding]].
- **Watch what "the agreement" would actually reveal.** LCJ's answer to the work product
  objection — the contract contains no "funder's internal analyses" — does not meet the real
  point, which is that a **multiple and a waterfall encode a sophisticated party's merits
  assessment after diligence**. That is also where the insurance analogy strains: an insurance
  policy discloses the defendant's **capacity to pay**, identical across every case under it; a
  funding agreement discloses **what someone thinks this case is worth**.
- **The proposals differ in scope far more than the debate admits.** Disclose that funding
  exists; disclose the funder's identity; disclose whether the funder controls settlement;
  produce the agreement. D.N.J. requires the first three and not the fourth. LCJ would require
  all four in every federal case. S. 3826 would require all four in aggregate cases only, plus a
  public register for foreign funders. Arguing about "disclosure" without saying which is
  arguing past the question.
- **The strongest fact for disclosure is the weakest for everyone else.** The market cannot
  be evaluated because the data does not exist, and it does not exist because the agreements
  are private. That is a real argument, and it is not answered by "corporate finance is
  private too" — because corporate finance is not deployed inside a public adjudicative
  process.
- **Watch the scope slide.** "Disclose that funding exists," "disclose the funder's
  identity," "disclose whether the funder controls settlement," and "produce the agreement"
  are four different rules with different costs. D.N.J. requires the first three and not the
  fourth. Most advocacy on both sides argues as if there were only one option.

## Where the law is now

- **Federal, general:** nothing. No FRCP provision requires it. The Advisory Committee has a
  **TPLF Subcommittee**, which as of Steinitz's writing "continue[d] to gather information and
  ha[d] not yet attempted to develop recommendations."
- **The live rules proposal**: [[LCJ and ILR - Proposal to amend Rule 26 on TPLF disclosure]],
  Rules Suggestion 26-CV-8 (Mar. 10, 2026), revising an October 2024 submission. It would add
  Rule 26(a)(1)(A)(v) requiring the funder's identity **and the agreement itself**, produced as
  under Rule 34, with carve-outs for contingency fees, insurance, ordinary loans and pre-existing
  shareholders — and reaching "securitised interests in law firm income."
- **The baseline survey**, from Steinitz at pp. 1079–80: **24 of 94 district courts** required
  some disclosure (14 by local rule amending Rule 7.1, two by standing order, ten by local
  form), and **six circuits** required disclosure beyond FRAP 26.1 of "any person or
  organization with a financial interest in the litigation," to assist with recusal. Recount
  before relying on it.
- **N.D. Cal.**, since November 2018: in any class, collective or representative action,
  disclose to the court the identity of any person or entity funding the prosecution of a
  claim or counterclaim.
- **D.N.J. L. Civ. R. 7.1.1**, since June 21, 2021: within **30 days**, identify the funder;
  state **whether the funder's approval is necessary for litigation or settlement decisions**
  and on what terms; and describe the nature of the financial interest. The **agreement itself**
  comes only on a **showing of good cause** under (b). Covered funding must be **non-recourse**
  and is not "a personal or bank loan, or insurance" — so a **recourse** facility to a law firm
  falls outside the rule entirely. [[New Jersey Local Civil Rule 7.1.1]].
- **Federal Circuit:** [[In re Nimitz Technologies]] (Dec. 2022) on a district judge's
  standing order requiring disclosure of funding and real parties in interest.
- **Delaware** and several other districts operate standing orders by individual judges.
- **Legislative:** [[Litigation Funding Transparency Act - S 3826]] (119th Cong., introduced
  Feb. 2026; Grassley, with Tillis, Kennedy and Cornyn). Reaches **class actions, MDL and
  coordinated proceedings of 100+ cases**; requires funder identity, **whether the funder is a
  foreign state, foreign person, sovereign wealth fund or foreign-controlled enterprise**, and
  copies of all agreements, to the court and named parties; transmits to the Administrative
  Office, which publishes a foreign-funder register every **120 days**; **restricts funders from
  influencing litigation strategy** or accessing protected discovery material without court
  authorisation; contempt for violations. Swiss Re endorsed an earlier version in 2021. See
  [[Foreign funding and national security]].
- **State:** 2025–2026 enactments in Georgia, Kansas, Louisiana, Montana, Oklahoma and New
  York, surveyed in Class 8. This is moving quarterly — date every statement of the law.
