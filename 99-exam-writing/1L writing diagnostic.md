# 1L writing diagnostic

Built from four of your own graded answers (CivPro 175, Elements 177, Contracts
179, Transactions 184) read against seven circulated Baude exemplars, two
Rappaport model answers, three Levmore memos, the Bray memo, and the Fennell
sample set. Substance is held aside throughout: every finding below is about
what the prose does on the page.

## 1. The hedging hypothesis is wrong

You hedge **less** than the answers professors circulate as models.

| | narrow hedges /1k | commitment markers /1k |
|---|---|---|
| Rappaport model 1 | 1.50 | 18.8 |
| Rappaport model 2 | 3.34 | 20.1 |
| Baude exemplars (soft modals) | 5.7 – 14.2 | — |
| **You, 175 CivPro** | 0.96 | 5.5 |
| **You, 177 Torts** | 0.36 | 3.2 |
| **You, 177 Elements** | 0.31 | — |
| **You, 179 Contracts** | 0.76 | 9.5 |
| **You, 184 Transactions** | 0.30 | 6.6 |

The gap is not hedging. It is **verdict labelling**. Rappaport opens nearly every
issue with a graded verdict word — *likely guilty*, *plausibly but not likely
guilty*, *likely not guilty*, *most likely to succeed* — so the reasoning that
follows reads as support for a stated holding. Your answers run the analysis and
leave the disposition to be inferred, or place it at the end of the paragraph
where it reads as summary. The prose is not mushy. It is **unlabelled**.

The strong answers also hedge in a specific grammatical place: **hedged subject,
unhedged verb**. "One could argue that… the court *should*." You do the reverse
— "We *should probably* follow the district court's ruling," "the 9th circuit
… *will probably* give us authority" — which softens the one clause that was
supposed to carry weight.

## 2. Your grade tracks how much visible structure the answer has

Across four exams this correlation is exact.

| Grade | Course | Headings | Enumerated elements |
|---|---|---|---|
| 184 | Transactions | numbered sub-answers mirroring the prompt, plus named sections | Howey prongs as `(1)…(4)`; every Reg D exemption swept and killed by number |
| 179 | Contracts | self-made issue headings: Formation / April and May / Damages | UCC and common-law tracks run in parallel, both resolved |
| 177 | Elements | prompt's `A.`/`B.` on Q1 only; Q2 and Q3 are undifferentiated blocks of 748 and 1,042 words | none |
| 175 | CivPro | none anywhere | test elements named in passing, never marched |

Buccola's strong answers invent a standard-of-review taxonomy the prompt never
supplies — *Direct or Derivative · Controlling Stockholder · Conflicted
Director · Entire Fairness · Revlon · Unocal*. Baude's strong answers convert
the prompt's own verbs into headings; both 2023 exemplars independently turned
"could … should" into `Can?` / `Should?`. This is the cheapest available fix in
the whole diagnostic, and it is the one your 184 already does.

## 3. Doctrinal narration displaces application

The signature failure of the 175. CivPro Q1B spends 563 words touring the
pleading standard chronologically — Conley, Twombly, Souter's majority, Iqbal,
Souter's dissent, Swanson, Wood, Posner's dissent, Rule 8(d)(2) — and mentions
Barbara twice. Q3 announces the method outright:

> "we must account for developments in personal jurisdiction chronologically to
> ensure proper predictions"

and then walks Pennoyer → International Shoe → World-Wide Volkswagen → McIntyre
→ Ainsworth in order. The doctrine's history is accurate and it is in the
service of nothing. Bray's memo says the strongest answers "grapple with this
difference," and warns in terms: "don't go to the stream of commerce cases."

The 184 never does this. It states the test and marches it: "Thus, all four
prongs of the Howey Test are met and this is an investment contract."

## 4. You front-load the losing side

CivPro Q3 opens with the holding, then spends the entire first paragraph on four
arguments *against* it — Pennoyer, Mallory, International Shoe, WWV/McIntyre —
before the turn: "However, none of these arguments will likely be enough." A
grader has read 150 words of the loser's case before learning it loses.

In 28 of roughly 30 answer units across the Baude exemplars, the first sentence
is the bottom line. Three make it a standalone one-sentence paragraph:

> "The Court should stand by the Standard Oil interpretation."
> "The Court of Appeals should not overturn Riggs."

## 5. Thesis sentences arrive garbled

Elements Q1A, the opening two sentences:

> "The district court in CA should be bound by 9th circuit precedent from
> ChatGPT v. Lutnick. Instead of following the decision from ChatGPT, we should
> not allow the seizure of Anthropic's servers by Lutnick (SoC), citing
> Youngstown."

Sentence one says bound; sentence two says do not follow. The actual position —
not strictly bound, because the facts differ — is sentence three. CivPro Q3 has
the same defect in miniature:

> "This proves Ajax does not have the expectation to be hauled into specific
> courts in the US. Rather, it supports the idea that Ajax *does* have the
> 'reasonable expectation' of being hauled into court anywhere in the US."

A grader reading at speed scores the sentence, not the intention behind it.

## 6. You never grade your own arguments

The move that recurs most in every benchmark set and almost never in yours: an
explicit verdict on an argument's **weight**.

- Baude exemplars: "This is a fairly weak argument, however" · "On the other hand, and more convincingly" · "the arguments against overruling Standard Oil are likely stronger"
- Rappaport: "The inducement argument is the weakest link of the defense" · "The felony murder claim is weaker, however" · "and most likely to succeed"
- Baude Con Law answers: "California's strongest challenge is based on the 'ambiguity' factor" · "There's a slightly weaker theory"

You do it once across four exams, in Contracts — "Lexi's best argument is
limiting all consequential damages is not in good faith" — which is your highest
grade outside the 184.

## 7. One section per exam goes under-written

Not a habit. A triage failure, and always confined to a single section.

| Exam | Words per section | Against cap |
|---|---|---|
| Elements 177 | 1,173 / 748 / 1,042 | 98% / 99.7% / 99.2% |
| CivPro 175 | 748 / **563** / 746 / 750 | ~100% except Q2 at **75%** |
| Contracts 179 | 1,352 / 750 / **297** | Q3 at roughly **40%** |

Baude exemplars land at 98.1–100.0% of cap on fifteen of fifteen questions,
seven of them exactly at cap. On Elements you match that. On CivPro you left
~190 words unclaimed and on Contracts ~450 — in both cases on a question you
reached last.

## 8. What the 184 did that nothing else did

Worth isolating, because it was **closed-book**, so none of it depended on
lookup:

- Sub-answers numbered to the prompt's own numbering, with named sections.
- Provisions cited by number throughout: §5, Rule 506(b), 506(c), 504, DGCL 101, 102(a), 144(a)(1), 144(b)(1), 144(b)(2), 102(b)(7), ICA 3(c)(1), 3(c)(7).
- Every alternative swept and killed with a reason rather than ignored: "Regulation crowdfunding is not viable since no registered funding portal was used."
- Explicit forks instead of hedges: "Still, it's worth it to analyze it both ways" → "If she owns at least 50%…" → "If Enya is somehow not a controlling stockholder…"
- The assigned register adopted: "As counsel, I'd begin by explaining a $30M valuation at $4M ARR in two years is too low."
- A policy answer that proposes, counters itself, and resolves: carry taxed as ordinary income → "but that might backfire as these funds will basically become a mutual fund" → "A good middle ground would be to tax carry as ordinary income."

## The six habits to drill

Ordered by points per unit of effort.

1. **Sentence one is the holding.** One sentence, one clause, no second thought appended.
2. **Headings on every question**, taken from the prompt's labels where it has them and from the governing test where it does not.
3. **March the elements by number** rather than naming the test and moving on.
4. **Grade every counterargument** — state it at its strongest, then say in terms whether it is weaker or stronger and why.
5. **Hedge the argument, commit the verb.** "One could argue X, but the court should Y."
6. **Spend the whole budget**, and triage before writing so the last question is not the starved one.

## Calibration

The Torts 177 was held out of everything above and then graded blind on the six
axes, from the metrics plus one read. It scored **8 of 18** — median, which is
what it got. Levmore's own memos predict the failure exactly: he rewards breadth
("Do not stop after the first strategy or claim you identify") and penalises the
refusal to engage, and that answer lists seven strategies California "could"
pursue while ranking almost none of them. Q1 carries 1.70 verdict markers per
1,000 words, the lowest section in the corpus; Q2 opens by restating what Patsy
is attempting and never says whether she recovers.
