# Baude 2018, Question Three — 20 points, 700 words

3 min read · 4 min outline · 7 min opening · stop.

This is the archetype worth the most preparation in the whole course. A
normative reform essay, no fact pattern, 20–30 points, **every single year**:
2018 asks which cases you would overrule, 2021 asks what insights the
chronology gives, 2023 asks you to draft a constitutional amendment and then
attack your own draft.

## Prompt

Consider the following ten cases that we studied in class:

- McCulloch v. Maryland
- Gibbons v. Ogden
- United States v. Darby
- Wickard v. Filburn
- Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States
- United States v. Lopez
- Gonzales v. Raich
- United States v. Comstock
- Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority
- Printz v. United States

Suppose you wanted to improve this body of law to create one that was both
(1) better justified as a matter of constitutional interpretation, and
(2) much more coherent and consistent. **Which of these cases would you overrule
in order to do that? Explain.**

## Opening — 7 min

The circulated answer to this question opened *"First, I uphold McCulloch:
Congress needs a legitimate end within its enumerated powers, but may use any
'means appropriate'…"* — first word "First", first verb "I uphold", no preamble.

Position is free here; the defence of it is not. The exemplars across years
disagree with each other on the merits and all circulated.



---

## Afterwards, not before

The highest-quality Q3 opening in the corpus, from 2021 — it names the received
view in order to demolish it:

> "The conventional wisdom we learned in class is that over the course of the twentieth century, whatever limitations were initially placed on the powers of the President… gradually eroded in favor of… an expanded role for the Executive branch and federal authority more generally."

The next sentences run: *"But, looking at the cases in chronological order, it
strikes me that another version of the story is at least as plausible: that
contemporary judges and commentators have inaccurately romanticized the
jurisprudence of the past."*

Also note what Baude licenses on this question and nowhere else in your two
classes — arguing the Court is simply wrong is graded content. The circulated
answers call doctrine "exasperating and insipid" and say the Court "talks out of
both sides of its mouth—and maybe also a nostril." There is no Buccola question
that rewards this.
