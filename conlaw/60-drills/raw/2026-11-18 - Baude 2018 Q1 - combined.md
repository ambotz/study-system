# Baude 2018, Question One — 30 points, 1050 words

3 min read · 4 min outline · 7 min opening · stop.

## Prompt

Congress has become concerned that western states are not doing enough to control wildfires, which in recent years have appeared with devastating regularity. Mitigating the costs of these wildfires can require restrictions on development, changes in forest management, water allocation, and aggressive fire response and prevention. Out of deference to federalism, Congress decides not to try to impose comprehensive regulations directly on these things. Instead, it passes new legislation (with the President's enthusiastic signature) to give states and people more incentive to work to prevent forest fires. That new legislation has two components.

**First, in all states, the law imposes new conditions on federal spending.** A state may only receive certain funding if it "passes regulation to substantially mitigate the risks of wildfires." A state is automatically in compliance with this condition if it has no major wildfires in a given year. If it does have a major wildfire, the Secretary of the Interior (a cabinet-level officer of the United States) must decide whether the state's regulations were enough to "substantially mitigate the risk[]." The Secretary's determination is subject to judicial review.

If a state is not in compliance with this condition, then it loses all support for fire suppression. That federal support comprises two major pre-existing programs: First, there is the Fire Management Assistance Grant program, which reimburses a state for 75% of the money it spends on firefighting. (Last year, California spent about $800 million in firefighting, and so received a grant of about $600 million.) Second, there is the direct assistance of the United States Forest Service, which works directly to fight and prevent fires, on both federally-owned land and other land. The exact economic value of these services is difficult to quantify, but the United States Forest Service spends about $2 billion annually on firefighting nationwide. Under the new law, if a state is not in compliance, it cannot receive a Fire Management Assistance Grant, and the United States Forest Service cannot fight any fires in that state, and will instead be instructed to stand by and "watch it burn."

**Second, in "fire-prone states," the law imposes an annual wildfire tax** on all persons in the state if an uncontained wildfire has raged in their state that year. The amount of the tax for each person is $1 for every 1000 acres destroyed by the wildfire. (One of the most destructive wildfire seasons in recorded memory affected 1,824,505 acres of land in California, so Congress reasonably expects that the tax will never be more than $2000 per person per year.) Persons who have low enough income that they are exempt from filing an income tax return are also exempt from the wildfire tax.

The "fire-prone states" are specifically designated in the statute as: Alaska, California, Idaho, Nevada, and Texas. According to generally accepted figures, these are the five states that have seen the most wildfire destruction in the past fifteen years.

The governor of California is very concerned about the new law and its effect on the state (whose budget is about $200 billion a year). He calls you for advice. He asks: **Could our state or our citizens challenge the law in federal court? What would our arguments be, and which of them would be strongest?**
