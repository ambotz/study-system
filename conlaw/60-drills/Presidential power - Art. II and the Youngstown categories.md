> [!info] Art. II § 1 — the Vesting Clause and the Youngstown categories of presidential power

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — State the rule.
?
The President needs authority from a statute or from the Constitution itself, and when he relies on the Constitution alone, the strength of his claim depends on what Congress has said.

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Where is that rule contested, or what is the competing formulation? (1 of 2)
?
Under Justice Jackson's concurrence in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]], which supplies the operative framework, authorization puts him at his "maximum," silence leaves him in a "zone of twilight," and action against Congress's express or implied will puts him at his "lowest ebb." At the lowest ebb a court can sustain him only by holding the power "conclusive and preclusive," which disables Congress from acting on the subject.

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Where is that rule contested, or what is the competing formulation? (2 of 2)
?
The contest is textual, because Article II vests "the executive Power" without Article I's "herein granted." Jackson denied that the clause is "a grant in bulk of all conceivable executive power"; Vinson, Miller in [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]], and the Office of Legal Counsel in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] read it closer to Locke's prerogative to act "without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it" ([[Introduction to Article II]]).

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Name the steps of the inquiry, in order (8).
?
1. What source does the President claim: a statute, or the Constitution alone
2. Into which of Jackson's three categories does Congress's action put the President
3. Has Congress refused, or merely stayed silent
4. In category three, is the asserted power exclusive to the President
5. In category two, does executive practice supply a "gloss" on executive power
6. Is the act lawmaking in character (Black's independent route)
7. Is force turned outward against an enemy, or inward against Americans
8. Is an emergency doing work the Constitution denies it

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 1 is *What source does the President claim: a statute, or the Constitution alone*. What must be shown?
?
- If a statute authorizes the act, the inherent-power question never arises. The Youngstown government disclaimed its statutory route as "much too cumbersome, involved, and time-consuming for the crisis," which forced the constitutional question.
- Authorization cannot give the President a share in lawmaking itself ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]); see [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 2 is *Into which of Jackson's three categories does Congress's action put the President*. What must be shown?
?
- *Category one (express or implied authorization):* power "at its maximum," comprising "all that he possesses in his own right plus all that Congress can delegate." The 2001 AUMF is the model ([[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 3 is *Has Congress refused, or merely stayed silent*. What must be shown?
?
- A considered refusal is category three: Congress weighed emergency seizure in the 1947 Taft-Hartley debates and chose other remedies, which Frankfurter reads as Congress saying "You may not seize."
- Occupying the field is category three: Jackson placed the seizure there because Congress had "covered" seizure "by three statutory policies inconsistent with this seizure."

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 4 is *In category three, is the asserted power exclusive to the President*. What must be shown?
?
- Test each clause, as Jackson did, by asking whether Congress is disabled from acting on the subject at all.
- Commander in Chief: the President is not "Commander in Chief of the country, its industries and its inhabitants."
- Take Care: the clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker" (Black) and is matched by the Due Process Clause (Jackson).

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 5 is *In category two, does executive practice supply a "gloss" on executive power*. What must be shown?
?
- Frankfurter's test: the practice must be "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned."
- Three seizures in six months of 1941 fail; the 252 land withdrawals over 80 years in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.* passed.
- A practice Congress has contested fails, as unilateral war-making did once Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over a veto.

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 6 is *Is the act lawmaking in character (Black's independent route)*. What must be shown?
?
- An order that sets out policy reasons, proclaims rules of conduct and, "like a statute," authorizes subordinate rulemaking is legislation, which Article I, § 1 vests in Congress.
- The editors' ideal of executive power "includes the action, strength, or force of the government but not its lawmaking power" ([[Introduction to Article II]]).

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 7 is *Is force turned outward against an enemy, or inward against Americans*. What must be shown?
?
- Jackson would give the "widest latitude" to the President's command of "the instruments of national force, at least when turned against the outside world," but none when it is turned inward "not because of rebellion but because of a lawful economic struggle between industry and labor."

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Step 8 is *Is an emergency doing work the Constitution denies it*. What must be shown?
?
- "[T]he emergency did not create power; it merely marked an occasion when power should be exercised" (Douglas).
- The only express emergency provision is the Suspension Clause, and it sits in Article I; see [[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]].

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Standard of review, and who bears the burden?
?
- **Presumption:** In category one, the act is "supported by the strongest of presumptions and the widest latitude of judicial interpretation, and the burden of persuasion would rest heavily upon any who might attack it"; a loss there "usually means that the Federal Government as an undivided whole lacks power" (Jackson).

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Carve-out 1 of 4?
?
- **Conclusive and preclusive powers.** Congress cannot regulate the pardon, removal of executive officers, or the decision whom to investigate and prosecute ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]); see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Carve-out 2 of 4?
?
- **Defensive force.** When war is made on the United States by invasion or organized rebellion, the President is "bound to resist force by force" without waiting for Congress, though "[h]e has no power to initiate or declare a war" ([[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]]); see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Carve-out 3 of 4?
?
- **Protection of federal officers and instrumentalities.** The Take Care duty reaches "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself," so the President may protect a federal judge, or the mails, without a statute ([[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]]).

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Carve-out 4 of 4?
?
- **Enforcement discretion in particular cases.** Declining to prosecute is executive and unreviewable ([[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]]); the limit is a general policy that amounts to "an abdication of its statutory duty" ([[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]]). See [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Trap 1 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **"Youngstown holds that the President was at his lowest ebb."** Black's test is whether the act is legislative in character; Jackson's test is what Congress has said. Name the opinion you are applying.

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Trap 2 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **"Congress did not authorize it, so the President is at his lowest ebb."** Silence is category two. Category three requires an express or implied denial: a rejected proposal, an occupied field of inconsistent schemes, or a prohibition. [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]], [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]] and [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] are silence cases, and the executive won all three.

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Trap 3 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **"Youngstown denies the President emergency power."** The Court held only that this seizure, against Congress's expressed will, was unlawful. It did not deny emergency power in general or all domestic reach of the Commander in Chief Clause, and Frankfurter reserved the short, self-terminating seizure.

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Trap 4 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **"Category three means automatic defeat."** It means defeat unless the power is conclusive and preclusive. [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] turns that same category into a zone of absolute criminal immunity, so the move from "Congress cannot forbid this act" to "Congress cannot punish this act" is the point to argue.

[[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]

**Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories** — Best counter-case, and how do you argue each side?
?
[[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]]
- **Distinguishing line:** Where Congress has said nothing and the President acts to protect the government's own officers or functions, Neagle sustains him; where Congress has addressed the subject and the President regulates private property or private economic relations, Youngstown condemns him.
