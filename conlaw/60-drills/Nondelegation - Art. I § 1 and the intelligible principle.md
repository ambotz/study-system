**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Where is that rule contested, or what is the competing formulation? (2 of 2)
?
Intelligible principle = "Congress must give a clear general goal, policy boundary, or standard." The test is "not demanding": only two delegations have ever failed, both in 1935, and the inquiry "always begins (and often almost ends) with statutory interpretation." The contest is whether the test tracks the Constitution at all: three Gundy dissenters would replace it, Alito would reconsider it with a full Court, and Madison's anti-delegation side won the vote in [[The Post Roads Debate]].

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Name the steps of the inquiry, in order (7).

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 1 is *Does the statute, properly construed, delegate as much as the challenger says*. What must be shown?
?
- Read the delegating text with its context, purpose and history, and adopt the narrower reading if one is available.
- In Gundy, "specify the applicability" of SORNA to pre-Act offenders was read to mean *how* to apply the statute during the transition, not *whether* to apply it, so the Attorney General had to apply the registration requirements to pre-Act offenders "as soon as feasible."

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 2 is *Is the delegated power legislative at all*. What must be shown?
?
- Gorsuch's definition of legislative power is the power to adopt "generally applicable rules of conduct governing future actions by private persons."
- Discretion over the government's own operations or property, such as the route of a post road, may tolerate broader delegation (casebook Note 3 to [[The Post Roads Debate]]).

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 3 is *Did Congress supply an intelligible principle*. What must be shown?
?
- Two formulations are in use: the J.W. Hampton "intelligible principle," and a statement of "the general policy" the delegee must pursue and the "boundaries of [his] authority" (American Power & Light, quoted in Gundy).
- A delegation fails only where "Congress had failed to articulate any policy or standard" to confine discretion (Mistretta, quoted in Gundy).

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 4 is *Is the challenged standard at least as definite as standards already upheld*. What must be shown?
?
- Compare against "public interest," "fair and equitable" prices, "just and reasonable" rates, and air quality standards "requisite to protect the public health."
- Feasibility standards are "ubiquitous in the U.S. Code," so a feasibility limit like SORNA's clears the bar.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 5 is *In the alternative, does the delegation fit one of Gorsuch's three permissible categories*. What must be shown?
?
- Congress makes the policy and lets the executive "fill up the details" (*Wayman v. Southard*).
- Congress prescribes the rule and makes its application depend on executive fact-finding (*The Brig Aurora*).
- Congress assigns non-legislative responsibilities, or discretion over "matters already within the scope of executive power."

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 6 is *Is the same concern being policed under another name*. What must be shown?
?
- Gorsuch's "hydraulic pressures" point: the major questions doctrine and void-for-vagueness do nondelegation work.
- The structural cases do too: [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] (delegation to one House) and [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] (the line-item veto). See [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Step 7 is *If Congress reserved a check on the delegation, is the check lawful*. What must be shown?
?
- A one-House or two-House veto is void after [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]].
- A sunset clause, a report-and-wait provision, and a joint resolution of disapproval presented to the President are valid, because nothing changes the law except a law passed under Article I, § 7.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Standard of review, and who bears the burden?
?
- **Presumption:** Congress's choice of how much discretion to leave is almost never disturbed: "[W]e have 'almost never felt qualified to second-guess Congress regarding the permissible degree of policy judgment that can be left to those executing or applying the law'" (Gundy plurality, quoting Whitman and Scalia's dissent in *Mistretta*).
- **Burden:** The challenger must show the statute supplies no policy or standard at all; a thin standard is enough.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Carve-out 1 of 4?
?
- **Filling up the details.** Congress makes the policy and the executive works out its application (*Wayman v. Southard*). Sedgwick's formulation in 1791 is the ancestor: "it [is] sufficient that the House should establish the principle, and then leave it to the Executive to carry it into effect."

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Carve-out 2 of 4?
?
- **Contingent legislation.** Congress prescribes the rule and conditions its operation on an executive finding of fact, as with the embargo in *The Brig Aurora*, triggered by a presidential finding about British or French interference with American trade.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Carve-out 3 of 4?
?
- **Powers that overlap Article II.** Foreign affairs and command tolerate delegations that would fail elsewhere. The 2001 AUMF's "those nations, organizations, or persons he determines" survives the intelligible-principle test (the principle being the September 11 perpetrators and harborers) and fits Gorsuch's third category ([[Modern Applications of the War Power]]); the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution's "as the President determines" raised the same question in 1964 ([[Declarations of War]]).

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Carve-out 4 of 4?
?
- **The government's own operations.** Delegations about government property and internal administration, rather than rules for private conduct, fall outside Gorsuch's definition of legislative power, which is why the post roads debate is harder for his position than it looks.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Trap 1 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?

- **"Gundy reaffirmed the intelligible-principle test."** Only four Justices applied and endorsed the test; three would replace it, Alito concurred only in the judgment and endorsed neither analysis, and Kavanaugh did not sit. The test survived by default, not by a majority opinion.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Trap 2 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **Reciting the intelligible principle and stopping.** The plurality spends most of its opinion on statutory interpretation, because the constitutional answer follows from the construction; an answer that never narrows the statute skips the step that decides most cases.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Trap 3 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **Treating the 1935 cases as a live general doctrine.** They are the only two invalidations, in one year, and the Schechter statute let private trade groups write codes with the President's discretion "virtually unfettered." Sunstein's frame: "one good year, and 211 bad ones (and counting)."

Consumers' Research* applied the intelligible principle instead. Label the categories as a dissent, then use them for Baude's question about where the doctrine may be off track.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Leading case, and the fact that drove it?
?
[[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy v. United States]]
- **Holding:** SORNA § 20913(d), which gives the Attorney General "the authority to specify the applicability" of the registration requirements to pre-Act offenders, is a valid delegation; the judgment is 5–3 and affirms Gundy's conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 2250.

**Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle** — Best counter-case, and how do you argue each side?
?
[[The Post Roads Debate]]
- **Distinguishing line:** Ask whether Congress kept for itself the choice that decides the matter (which roads, which offenders) or handed that choice to the executive; Madison's side said Congress must keep it, and the Gundy plurality upheld SORNA only by reading the statute to keep the whether-to-register choice in Congress.
