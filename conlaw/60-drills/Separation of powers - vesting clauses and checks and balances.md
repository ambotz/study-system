> [!info] Art. I § 1, Art. II § 1 and Art. III § 1 — the vesting clauses, the asymmetry of "herein granted," and the system of checks

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — State the rule.
?
Each branch may exercise only the power the Constitution gives it, and one branch cannot take over another's power even with that branch's consent. Art. I, § 1 vests "[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress; Art. II, § 1 vests "[t]he executive Power" in a President; Art. III, § 1 vests "[t]he judicial Power of the United States" in the courts.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Where is that rule contested, or what is the competing formulation? (1 of 2)
?
Where the Constitution shares a power, the shared procedure is the only lawful route ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]], [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]). Because paper boundaries are mere "parchment barriers," each branch gets "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others" (The Federalist Nos. 48 and 51, in [[A Map of Article I]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Where is that rule contested, or what is the competing formulation? (2 of 2)
?
The contest is formalism, which asks who holds the power (Burger in Chadha; Black in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]), against functionalism, which asks whether one branch gains at another's expense (White in Chadha; Jackson in Youngstown). Beneath both lies the original-meaning question whether Article II's omission of "herein granted" vests a residual executive power.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Name the steps of the inquiry, in order (8).
?
1. Is the act legislative, executive or judicial in character and effect, whatever its label or author
2. Which vesting clause, and which specific clause, carries the power
3. Does the text share the power, and was the shared procedure followed
4. If one House acted alone, does an express exception authorize the solo action
5. Did the affected branch consent? Consent does not cure
6. Where the text is silent or the power concurrent, where does the act fall in Jackson's scheme
7. Does practice settle the allocation
8. Which branch gains, and at whose expense

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 1 is *Is the act legislative, executive or judicial in character and effect, whatever its label or author*. What must be shown?
?
- Action is legislative when it "alter[s] the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch" ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).
- A statutory label does not control: the FEC was described as a legislative-branch body and still exercised executive power ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 2 is *Which vesting clause, and which specific clause, carries the power*. What must be shown?
?
- Article I: only the powers "herein granted," listed mostly in § 8.
- Article III: "the judicial Power," followed by the list of cases and controversies.
- Article II: "the executive Power," followed by "a seemingly random assortment" of specific powers ([[Introduction to Article II]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 3 is *Does the text share the power, and was the shared procedure followed*. What must be shown?
?
- Lawmaking: passage by both Houses and presentment (Art. I, § 7); see [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
- Principal officers: presidential nomination plus Senate consent (Art. II, § 2, cl. 2); see [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 4 is *If one House acted alone, does an express exception authorize the solo action*. What must be shown?
?
- Only four one-House actions carry the force of law: House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation, Senate treaty ratification ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).
- Where the Framers meant one House to act alone "they did so in explicit, unambiguous terms."

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 5 is *Did the affected branch consent? Consent does not cure*. What must be shown?
?
- Congress cannot keep a legislative veto that Presidents accepted "as the price for a broad delegation of authority" (White, dissenting in [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).
- Congress cannot give the President a power to cancel enacted provisions, even by a statute he signed ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]).
- Congress cannot appoint officers, even officers who police its own members' elections ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 6 is *Where the text is silent or the power concurrent, where does the act fall in Jackson's scheme*. What must be shown?
?
- Presidential power "fluctuate[s], depending upon [its] disjunction or conjunction with those of Congress" ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]).
- Maximum with authorization; "zone of twilight" in silence; "lowest ebb" against Congress's will, where only a "conclusive and preclusive" power survives.
- Full analysis in [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]].

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 7 is *Does practice settle the allocation*. What must be shown?
?
- Frankfurter's test: practice glosses "executive Power" only if "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned" ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]).
- Widespread practice does not save Congress's inventions: [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] invalidated the legislative veto despite 295 veto-type provisions in 196 statutes.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Step 8 is *Which branch gains, and at whose expense*. What must be shown?
?
- Checks and balances are "a self-executing safeguard against the encroachment or aggrandizement of one branch at the expense of the other" ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]).
- Ask whether the acting branch took another branch's power or defended its own.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Standard of review, and who bears the burden?
?
- **Presumption:** "When the Executive acts, he presumptively acts in an executive or administrative capacity"; when one House acts, it acts in its legislative sphere ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Carve-out 1 of 4?
?
- **The four express one-House powers.** House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation and Senate treaty ratification operate without bicameralism or presentment ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Carve-out 2 of 4?
?
- **Each House's internal governance.** Under Art. I, § 5 each House judges its members' elections, sets its own rules, and punishes or expels members ([[A Map of Article I]]); each may appoint its own officers "to assist in the legislative processes" without offending the Appointments Clause ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]).

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Carve-out 3 of 4?
?
- **Bodies exercising no executive power.** A commission that only investigates and reports to Congress may be staffed by Congress ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]). What survives of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor v. United States]] after [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] is only the "observation" that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the removal rule.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Carve-out 4 of 4?

- **The Federal Reserve.** Reserved in Part III-B of [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. **Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Trap 2 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **"Both branches agreed, so the arrangement is valid."** [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] and [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] struck schemes the President and Congress had accepted. Jackson's category one enlarges the President's executive discretion; it cannot give him a share in the § 7 lawmaking procedure, which is why Breyer's reliance on category one failed in Clinton.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Trap 3 of 4 — the wrong answer that sounds right?
?
- **"Clinton forbids the President to decline to spend."** [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] held only that a statutory power to "cancel" enacted provisions, depriving them of "legal force or effect," violates the Presentment Clause, and did not reach delegation. Scalia's "not a dime's worth of difference" between cancellation and discretionary spending shows the rule turns on drafting.

- **"Buckley bars Congress from creating independent offices."** Congress's power to create offices and define their powers "is broad indeed"; [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]] holds only that Congress may not fill them itself.

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Leading case, and the fact that drove it?
?
[[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]
- **Holding:** 6–3, Black for the Court. The President's power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself," and seizing steel mills to avert a strike is "a job for the Nation's lawmakers, not for its military authorities."

**Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances** — Best counter-case, and how do you argue each side?

[[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]]
- **Distinguishing line:** If the power at stake belongs to the President alone under Article II, a contrary statute is void whatever Congress has said (Slaughter); if Congress also holds power over the subject, the President's authority depends on what Congress has said (Youngstown).
