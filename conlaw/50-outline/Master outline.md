## 2. What Baude rewards — the ten rules

1. **Bottom line in sentence one.** Zero of fourteen circulated Con Law openings restate the question. "Bicameralism and presentment requirements are met (Art.I-7)."
2. **Mirror the prompt's labels as headings,** then convert the prompt's verbs into sub-headings: *Could / Should*, *Current doctrine / Best understanding*, the prompt's locations in the prompt's order (2023 Q2: post office, courthouses, school, train station — the exemplars split courthouses into federal and state).
3. **Cite clauses, not statutes,** in his house shorthand: Art.I-7, Art.II-2.2, Art.I-9.7. Seventeen clause cites in one 3,500-word exemplar. If the Constitution is supplied, use it; if not, the cites you need are the ones in Part X's clause map.
4. **Case plus Justice** is the citation currency: (Youngstown, Jackson) · (Raich, Scalia) · (Gundy, Kagan) · (NFIB, joint dissent). Six to thirteen named Justices per strong script.
5. **Count votes.** "Unclear whether that holding had five votes" is a rewarded move. Know which opinions are pluralities (Gundy), which holdings are one-Justice controlling (NFIB commerce), which decisions were stay orders (Cook).
6. **Rank the arguments.** "California's strongest challenge is…"; "a slightly weaker theory is…"
7. **Attribute and answer every counterargument,** with a weight judgment: "This is a fairly weak argument, however."
8. **Name the missing fact, say how it flips the result, then answer anyway.** Never "we cannot say."
9. **Commit with "should."** Hedge the argument, never the writer's own verb. Arguing the Court is wrong is graded content.
10. **Element-march.** "Show me" means every element of every test appears on the page, even the easy ones, disposed of in a clause.

## 3. The two-track structure

Every fact pattern can be answered twice, and Baude has asked for both explicitly (2021 Q1: 20 points doctrine, 30 points "proceeding as a departmentalist"). Even when the prompt asks only one, a short second track is where the 186 lives.

| Track | Question | Authority currency | Verdict form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Current doctrine** | What would a court bound by these precedents do? | Holdings, votes, controlling opinions | "A district court should hold…" |
| **B. Best understanding** | What does the Constitution actually require, and what should a departmentalist official do? | Text, structure, original meaning, liquidated practice, named Justices' positions, Baude's scholarship | "The President should conclude…, because…" |

**Track B in four moves** (the 2021 Q1 instruction: "tell the President how the Constitution should be interpreted, and how that interpretation applies"):

1. **State the method in one sentence.** Default: original public meaning, with indeterminacies settled by liquidated practice, and precedent given weight according to its quality and reliance. See Part I §§ 1–2.
2. **Defend it in two or three sentences** against the strongest rival (living constitutionalism / common-law constitutionalism; Frankfurter's gloss; the dead-hand objection, Part I § 3).
3. **Apply it gate by gate,** in the same order as Track A, so the parallel structure carries the argument.
4. **Mark where the tracks diverge** and say which is right: "Here the doctrine is off track, and the President should not adopt it."

**The departmentalist's legal duties, in order** (Part IV § 2): a judgment binds and must be enforced unless the court lacked jurisdiction (Baude, *The Judgment Power*); an opinion's reasoning binds other branches only as persuasive authority; the President may sign a bill with an unconstitutional part when other duties justify signing and he stands ready not to enforce that part (Baude, *Signing Unconstitutional Laws*); the veto and the pardon are the President's own constitutional judgments ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).

## 4. The universal attack sequence

Run every fact through these gates before writing. Not every gate fires; every gate gets asked.

**G0 — Role and ask.** Whose lawyer am I (White House, Senate staff, clerk, governor's adviser)? What exactly is asked — could, should, strongest, which parts? The role sets the verdict form: a clerk predicts, an adviser recommends, a President decides.

**G1 — Forum and threshold.**
- Is anyone in court? If so, who has standing — a State (sovereign or proprietary injury; *parens patriae* against the United States barred by [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon]]), a citizen or taxpayer (generalized grievance barred by [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] and *Frothingham*), a House of Congress (institutional injury, [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)|House v. Burwell]])? Part IV § 3.
- Is the question committed elsewhere — impeachment, the Guarantee Clause ([[Luther v. Borden]]), Article V procedure ([[Coleman v. Miller]])?
- If no court will hear it, the question does not disappear: it moves to the political branches, and the answer becomes a Track B answer (2023 Q1B: "imagine instead that nobody has standing").

**G2 — Who is acting?**
- Congress by statute → G3 source of power.
- One House, a committee, or Congress without the President → bicameralism and presentment (Part II § 4).
- The President with Congress, against Congress, or in silence → Youngstown category (Part II § 2).
- An agency or officer → is the actor an officer (Part III § 1), removable by whom (Part III § 2), acting on what delegation (Part II § 3)?
- A court or a court-located body → Article III (Part IV).
- A State → Article IV, dormant commerce, supremacy (Part VII), or the State as the regulated object (Part V § 4).

**G3 — Source of power.** For Congress: name the clause. Commerce (which of the three [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] categories — Class 20 signal), tax (Part VI § 1), spending (Part VI § 2), Necessary and Proper (Part V § 2), war (Part III § 4), Art. IV § 3 property and territory (Part VII § 4). For the President: Article II's text (vesting, Commander in Chief, Take Care, pardon, appointment) plus statutory authorization.

**G4 — Process constraints.** Bicameralism and presentment (Art.I-7); appointments (Art.II-2.2); appropriations (Art.I-9.7); the intelligible principle (Art.I-1).

**G5 — External limits.** Anti-commandeering (Part V § 4); coercion and the [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] conditions (Part VI § 2); tax-or-penalty, direct taxes, uniformity, export taxes (Part VI § 1); bills of attainder (Part II § 7); suspension (Part II § 6); removal limits (Part III § 2); Article III independence (Part IV § 4); privileges and immunities and dormant commerce for States (Part VII §§ 1–2).

**G6 — Political-branch remedies and counter-moves.** Impeachment (Part III § 5); defunding (Part II § 5); jurisdiction stripping and court packing (Part IV § 4); veto, signing statement, non-enforcement (Part III § 3); a successor President's reversal (2023 Q1C); elections.

**G7 — Best understanding.** Section 3 above.

**G8 — Missing facts and remedy.** For each gate, the one fact that would flip it. Then severability: which parts survive ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]] severed the appointment mechanism; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] limited the Medicaid remedy to the new funds).

## 5. Paragraph templates

**Gate opening (25–40 words).** Disposition + authority + the reason in a clause.
> "The $25,000 'tax' is a penalty, not a tax (Art.I-8.1; Bailey; NFIB, Roberts): it is prohibitory in size, applies only to knowing possession, and is collected by the Postal Service and three other agencies rather than the IRS."

**Pre-scored factors.** Name the factors, score each, then analyse.
> "Four things decide whether the exaction is a penalty (Bailey): a prohibitory rate, a scienter requirement, enforcement by a regulating agency rather than the revenue collector, and a prescribed code of conduct, which Taft called indispensable. The first three point toward penalty; the government's best reply is that the fourth is missing."

**The whipsaw.** Trap the government between two forms.
> "Even if the wildfire exaction is a tax, it takes no valid form: as a per-person charge it is a capitation that is not apportioned (Art.I-9.4); as an indirect tax laid on five named States it is not uniform (Art.I-8.1)."

**Ranked arguments.**
> "On the spending condition, California's strongest argument is that 'substantially mitigate the risks' is too ambiguous to be knowingly accepted (Dole; Pennhurst); its next strongest is that cutting off pre-existing programs it relies on is coercive (NFIB); the germaneness argument is weak."

**The named counter, answered and weighed.**
> "Justice Scalia would argue the intelligible-principle test is unmanageable, but he would likely uphold this delegation because it is one of carrying out the law (Mistretta, Scalia dissenting). That argument is weaker here, because…"

**The missing fact.**
> "If the 'fire-prone' designation tracks a neutral criterion, the uniformity objection fails; if Congress chose the five States by name to burden them, the tax is geographically non-uniform. I assume the former, so…"

**The Track B pivot.**
> "That is what current doctrine requires. The best understanding of the Constitution differs at one point: [gate]. A departmentalist President should conclude…, because the text… and the practice from 1789 to 1791 liquidated…"

**The close.** One sentence restating the verdict, plus word count if caps exist: "The President should sign the bill, state in a signing statement that the West Virginia exemption raises a uniformity problem, and ask Congress to cure it. (WC: 1,498)"

## 6. Canned hooks — portable moves that push the ball

Each hook is a sentence Ashwin can deploy in any answer where its trigger appears. Hooks drawn from Baude's own work are marked **[Baude]** — he is the reader.

- **Liquidation [Baude, *Constitutional Liquidation*, 71 Stan. L. Rev. 1 (2019)].** Trigger: an argument from historical practice. "Practice can settle the meaning of an indeterminate provision, but only if three conditions hold: a genuine textual indeterminacy, a course of deliberate practice reflecting constitutional reasoning, and a settlement marked by the losing side's acquiescence and public sanction. Practice can expound the Constitution; it cannot alter it." Part I § 2.
- **Liquidation versus gloss.** Trigger: [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]], removal, war powers, long-standing agency practice. "Frankfurter's 'systematic, unbroken executive practice' asks only how long; liquidation asks whether the practice was deliberate, whether the other side acquiesced, and whether the text was open to begin with. On that test, [[The Decision of 1789]] counts and ninety years of independent agencies do not — 'no branch may rely on adverse possession' ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]], Roberts)."
- **Judgment versus opinion [Baude, *The Judgment Power*, 96 Geo. L.J. 1807 (2008)].** Trigger: defiance, departmentalism, [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]], [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Merryman]], [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]]. "The President must enforce a federal court's judgment unless the court lacked jurisdiction; he need not adopt its reasoning as his own rule for other cases. Lincoln's position on Dred Scott is the model: obey the judgment as to Dred Scott, resist the opinion as a political rule." Paulsen's "Merryman power" is the foil — and Paulsen co-edits the casebook.
- **Signing unconstitutional laws [Baude, 86 Ind. L.J. 303 (2011)].** Trigger: a bill presented to the President (2021 Q1). "There is no categorical duty to veto a bill with an unconstitutional provision. The President may sign it when his other duties justify enacting the rest, provided he stands ready not to enforce the unconstitutional part, because nothing unconstitutional happens until enforcement." Cross-ref [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]].
- **Great powers [Baude, *Rethinking the Federal Eminent Domain Power*, 122 Yale L.J. 1738 (2013)].** Trigger: Necessary and Proper. "Some powers are too great to be implied as incidental — McCulloch's 'great substantive and independent power.' Chief Justice Roberts used that idea against the mandate in NFIB." Baude's own application is federal eminent domain within the States; he calls Roberts's use questionable and suggests commandeering, sovereign immunity and conscription as stronger candidates. Part V § 2.
- **Kind of power, not kind of procedure [Baude, *Adjudication Outside Article III*, 133 Harv. L. Rev. 1511 (2020)].** Trigger: a tribunal or adjudicator outside Article III; Slaughter's reserved category. "The question is what kind of governmental power the body exercises, not whether it adjudicates. An executive adjudicator exercises executive power, bounded by due process — which after Slaughter suggests the President can control it."
- **How to save the Fed [Baude, "How To Save The Federal Reserve," *Divided Argument* blog, May 2025 — his writing, not a class statement].** Trigger: [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]]. Before the decisions, Baude suggested the Court use its stare decisis discretion to preserve [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's]] for the Fed alone. The Court instead rested on First-and-Second-Bank history. Ask which ground is more honest: a precedent-based carve-out admits the line is pragmatic; a history-based carve-out claims it is principled and invites Justice Thomas's reply that the Banks "possessed no sovereign power."
- **"It is a constitution we are expounding."** Trigger: any expressio unius or "the text doesn't mention it" argument against a federal power. [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]] (Marshall): a constitution marks "only its great outlines," so incidental powers need not be listed.
- **The lowest ebb.** Trigger: the President acting against a statute. "Presidential power is at its lowest ebb, and the President can rely only on his own constitutional powers minus any constitutional powers of Congress over the matter" (Youngstown, Jackson). Then ask whether the power is "conclusive and preclusive" ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]).
- **"Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."** Trigger: any multimember independent body ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]], Roberts).
- **Formalism / functionalism pivot.** Trigger: any separation-of-powers question. "Under the formalist approach of Chadha and Slaughter, the question is which branch's power this is; under Morrison's functionalism, whether the arrangement impermissibly undermines a branch's core function. The Court has used both, and the answer here turns on which governs."
- **The dead hand answered.** Trigger: a normative "why follow the 1787 text" argument. "The dead-hand objection is strongest against a Constitution that cannot be changed; Article V, liquidation, and the people's ongoing acquiescence are the three answers, and the hardest of the three is Article V's difficulty." Part I § 3; Part VII § 5.
- **Political safeguards and their limits.** Trigger: [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]], spending, commandeering. "Garcia trusts the political process to protect the States; Printz and NFIB conclude that some structural protections cannot be left to it. The question is which protections the political process actually supplies."

## 7. Writing it — the Transactions method, adapted

- **Write it pissed off; stay calm.** Every fact in a Baude prompt is load-bearing: the $200B budget, the 75% reimbursement, "watch it burn," the five named States, the $25,000 "knowingly," the agency that collects. Before writing, underline each number, actor, and verb and assign it to a gate. A fact with no gate is a gate not yet found.
- **Peel and ram.** For each gate: rule → each element → apply facts → strongest counter, named → weight → verdict. Then the next gate.
- **Hooks that push forward.** Every canned hook must end by applying to the facts. A hook that only shows knowledge is wasted words against a cap.
- **Regurgitate his research — accurately.** Liquidation, judgment power, signing unconstitutional laws, great powers, adjudication outside Article III. One precise use beats three name-drops.
- **Spill every tie-in, then come out on a side.** Weave in the adjacent doctrine (tax ↔ commerce ↔ N&P; removal ↔ immunity ↔ impeachment), but every paragraph ends in a verdict.
- **Structure before writing.** Headings first, mirroring the prompt; gate list with word budgets; then write.
- **Control the tempo.** If a gate runs 50% over budget, cut to its verdict and move on. Return only if words remain.
- **End-of-question checklist:** bottom line in sentence one? every heading mirrors the prompt? every element shown? strongest argument ranked? counter named and weighed? missing fact named? Track B present? verdict restated? word count?

# Part I — How to Argue: Interpretation, Practice, and the Dead Hand

## 1. Modes of constitutional argument

**Clause** — No single clause. The anchors for "who interprets": the oath, "bound by Oath or Affirmation, to support this Constitution" (Art. VI cl. 3 → Art.VI-3), and the President's oath to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution" (Art. II § 1 cl. 8 → Art.II-1.8).

**Bottom line** — Constitutional arguments come in five types: text, historical context, structure, precedent and practice, and consequences ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]). No authority ranks them, so the graded move is to use several and then say which one controls and why. Practice is the one type with an internal test, Madison's liquidation, and it is Baude's own scholarship (see § 2).

**The test**

1. **Text — what did the words mean to informed readers when enacted?** Intratextual comparison is the strongest form: § 10's "absolutely necessary" against § 8's "necessary"; the Tenth Amendment's missing "expressly" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]). Johnson's *Dictionary* serves Jefferson and Hamilton equally ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]), so the interpreter must choose the sense.
2. **Historical context — what problem was the provision solving?** "Declare" replaced "make" war, "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks" ([[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]). Rejected Convention proposals are weak evidence ([[The Bank Debate]]).
3. **Structure — which variant?** Name it: relationship of provisions ([[The Decision of 1789]]), location (the Necessary and Proper Clause sits "among the powers of Congress"), or the arrangement as a whole (Marshall's representation argument in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
4. **Precedent and practice — what kind?** Ordinary precedent, liquidation, or historical gloss are three different claims ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]; [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] (Frankfurter)).
5. **Consequences — argued at the level of whether this kind of power should exist.** Reductio is the usual form: Jefferson's "boundless f[ie]ld of power"; Marshall's states that "may tax the mail; they may tax the mint."
6. **Which type controls here, and why?** An answer that lists modes without ranking them has not made an argument.

**Cases and who said what — the face of each mode**

- **Text:** (Youngstown, Black) — no statute, no clause, no power; (Bank Debate, Hamilton) — intent "is to be sought for in the instrument itself"; (Post Roads, Livermore).
- **Structure:** (McCulloch, Marshall) — the tax holding is structure with no text behind it: "the power to tax involves the power to destroy"; (Post Roads, Madison).
- **Original meaning / history:** (Dred Scott, Curtis) against (Dred Scott, Taney) — same mode, opposite results; (Gundy, Gorsuch) dissenting on the founding understanding of legislative power; (Slaughter, Roberts) reading 1789.
- **Practice:** (Youngstown, Frankfurter) — gloss; (Stuart v. Laird, Paterson) — "practice and acquiescence . . . affords an irresistible answer"; (Lafayette letter, Madison); (Slaughter, Sotomayor dissenting) — 140 years of legislative practice.
- **Precedent:** (Slaughter, Roberts) — the four stare decisis factors; Lincoln's criteria for a settled decision ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]).
- **Consequences:** (Youngstown, Jackson) — all five modes, consequences above all; (Gundy, Kagan) — the plurality's warning that a strict nondelegation rule would condemn much of government; (Post Roads, White).
- **Leading case:** [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] runs all five types on one question, then demotes practice: Marshall would reach the same result "were the question entirely new."
- **Best counter-case:** [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]. Distinguishing line: prefer the interpreter who asks what the words meant in law (Curtis) over the one who asks what the framers expected or tolerated (Taney).

**Baude in class** — He introduced the five modes and immediately ranked them against Youngstown: the majority put text and structure above practice, and "emergency ≠ executive power" (session 02). His frame for the term is the Constitution's three jobs: rights, separation of powers, federalism (session 01). He warned that punctuation and capitalisation may not carry much meaning.

- EXAM SIGNAL: "Make constitutional arguments to a fact set" (session 01).
- EXAM SIGNAL: Keep three argument types separate — a policy argument, a constitutional argument, and a constitutional argument a court will accept. No right answer, but many bad ones; inconsistency is how answers go bad (module gloss).
- Office hours: the order-of-text structural argument is closed — Article I does not outrank the Bill of Rights for coming first, and amendments do not matter less for being amendments. The available flip: a later amendment clarified or shed light on the original meaning.

**Doctrine vs. best understanding — the departmentalist methodology kit** (2021 Q1: "you must both tell the President how the Constitution should be interpreted, and how that interpretation applies")

1. **State the method in one sentence.** "The President should read the Constitution by its original public meaning; where that meaning runs out, he should treat a liquidated course of deliberate practice as settling it, and give judicial precedent the weight its quality and settlement earn."
2. **Defend it in three moves.**
   - Oath: the President swears to the Constitution, not to the Court's reading of it; each officer supports it "as he understands it" ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).
   - Written law: a written constitution is "a real existence" that "can be produced in a visible form" (Tucker, [[Constitutional Themes]]); its meaning does not change with whoever holds the office.
   - Settlement without chaos: liquidation gives departmentalism a stopping rule, so the President is not free to reopen everything (§ 2).
3. **Answer the strongest objection.** The objection is that original meaning is indeterminate on most hard questions. The reply is that indeterminacy is exactly where liquidation and precedent take over, so the method has a rule for its own gaps.
4. **Apply issue by issue, in the same order as the doctrinal half** — parallel structure is itself the argument (2021 exemplars: `1. Current Doctrine` / `2. Ideal Doctrine`).
5. **Commit.** Each issue ends with "The President should sign / veto / sign and decline to enforce § X."

**Fact triggers** — This is a modes problem when the prompt says "best understanding," "as a departmentalist," "should the Court overrule," "what does the Constitution actually require," or asks for advice to a political actor with no court involved.

**Oscillation points**

- Text vs. practice when text looks clear: war initiation (practice is long, text is "declare"). Ranked: (1) text controls initiation of sustained hostilities; (2) practice may have liquidated brief, limited strikes; (3) pure gloss argument is weakest because it never asks whether the text was open.
- Original meaning vs. precedent: the Slaughter four factors (quality, consistency, workability, reliance) against Baude's view that liquidation could improve stare decisis.

**Traps**

- **"List the five types and stop."** Rank them and name the controlling one.
- **"It is a constitution we are expounding means living constitutionalism."** In context it is a rule for construing grants of *means* in a document marking "only its great outlines."
- **"Dred Scott proves originalism fails."** Curtis is the better originalist in the same case; the case is evidence against bad history.

**Canned hook**

- "The text does not settle the question, so the controlling argument is [structure/practice]; and because the practice was deliberate and accepted, it has liquidated the meaning."
- "Even on its own originalist terms, the majority's reading fails, because it asks what the framers expected rather than what the words meant in law (Dred Scott, Curtis)."

## 2. Liquidation and historical practice

**Bottom line** — Where the Constitution's text is indeterminate, a course of deliberate practice by the political branches, ending in a settlement the losing side accepts and the public sanctions, fixes the provision's meaning. Practice may "expound" the Constitution but may not "alter" it. This is Baude's own research (William Baude, *Constitutional Liquidation*, 71 Stan. L. Rev. 1 (2019)), and he is grading the exam: run the three elements by name.

**The test — three elements** (from the article's abstract)

1. **Textual indeterminacy.** Clear provisions cannot be liquidated. Indeterminacy covers both ambiguity and vagueness, and a provision can be clear at its core and open at its edges.
2. **A course of deliberate practice.** "Repeated decisions that reflected constitutional reasoning." Silence and drift do not count; votes mixing expediency and constitutionality are weak ([[The Post Roads Debate]]).
3. **Settlement**, marked by two ideas:
   - **Acquiescence** by the dissenting side — the losers accept the result as law, not just lose the vote.
   - **Public sanction** — "a real or imputed popular ratification."

**Where the course invokes liquidation**

- **The Bank (the paradigm).** Madison opposed the Bank in 1791 on constitutional grounds; his 1815 veto message waived the constitutional question "as being precluded in my judgment by repeated recognitions under varied circumstances"; he signed the 1816 charter, and later explained the reversal as deference to settled practice ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]). The reversal is consistent: the 1791 argument showed only that the text was doubtful.
- **Jackson's counter-tally.** [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]] accepts the criterion and disputes the count: 1791 for, 1811 against, 1815 and 1816 split, so "the precedents drawn from that source" are "equal." The Bank War then undid forty years of practice ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- **Removal.** [[The Decision of 1789]] began "a regular course of practice" that "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the removal power, which [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] adopts. The dissent answers with [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s argument from 140 years of deliberate legislative practice. [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] reads the First and Second Banks as a tradition of insulation that survives 1789.
- **Spending.** Madison relabelled the 1792 cod-fishery "bounty" an "allowance" and characterized Haitian-refugee relief as repayment of a debt to France, each time to avoid "establishing a dangerous precedent" for a general spending power. The [[The Savannah Fire Debate|Savannah fire relief]] (1796) failed 55–34 as a general-versus-local welfare vote. See [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]].
- **Recess appointments.** *NLRB v. Noel Canning* (2014), the Court's first interpretation of the Recess Appointments Clause, reintroduced liquidation by quoting Madison's "regular course of practice" passage. Baude co-authored an amicus brief against the appointments, yet argues that Breyer was right to count practice "even when that practice began after the founding era," over Scalia's objection.

**Liquidation vs. Frankfurter's gloss** ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]])

- **Order of operations:** liquidation finds indeterminacy first; Frankfurter lets practice "give meaning to the words of a text or supply them," text second.
- **Kind of practice:** gloss asks what power has "been made part of the structure of our government," "never before questioned"; liquidation demands constitutional deliberation, more than silence.
- **Foundation:** gloss tracks what officials found workable; liquidation tracks what the people, through representation, found acceptable.
- **The two Jacksons.** Liquidation runs through both. President Jackson shows a settlement can be contested politically — but, per Baude, his veto did not unsettle the Bank's constitutionality, because Jackson's view never itself became settled. Justice Jackson's category two, the "zone of twilight," is where congressional "inertia, indifference or quiescence" can matter; liquidation tells you when quiescence counts (deliberate, accepted) and when it is mere drift.

**Baude in class** — Liquidation is the piece he told you to dig deep on: where text is ambiguous, and almost anything can be argued ambiguous, practice settles meaning if it is reasoned on constitutionality and repeated or settled (module gloss). In Slaughter, he expects the doctrine's own conditions to be pressed against both opinions.

- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 18: "dig deep" on liquidation): any answer invoking practice should establish indeterminacy, deliberation and settlement by name, not assert that something "has long been done."
- Office hours: liquidation is "one step stronger than custom" — it moves a practice from norm to law. For Madison as President, bound by his oath and still doubting the Bank, the practice had to be "almost like law" to count.
- Office hours: precedent is a subset of practice — the courts' practice.
- Office hours: President Jackson's veto did not liquidate the Bank question the other way, because his view never became the settled consensus.

**Doctrine vs. best understanding**

- **The Court:** uses practice opportunistically — founding practice is "weighty" (Slaughter), modern practice is "adverse possession" (Slaughter), and Frankfurter-style gloss is respectable (*Noel Canning*).
- **Baude's corrections:** early practice is not privileged, because liquidation works by analogy to precedent and precedents can be made 50 or 231 years on; liquidation is not necessarily permanent, and a later settled practice controls; and a single tight, partisan vote is not liquidation, though a 5–4 judgment is precedent — the ACA's passage (279–251 across both chambers, on Baude's count) liquidated nothing, while [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] binds.
- **Position to take:** courts should treat a genuinely liquidated question as settled law, and should not credit long practice that never involved constitutional deliberation. Strongest counter (Rakove): liquidation was "at best a secondary element" in Madison's thought and is "highly political." Baude's reply: political acts are given legal structure by the precedent analogy, and modern law uses past law "for reasons of its own," whatever Madison's interest level ([[Constitutional themes and the dead hand]]).

**The liquidation test applied**

| Question | Indeterminate? | Deliberate practice? | Settled? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Removal | Yes: Art. II silent | Yes: 1789 debate on constitutional grounds | Contested: Tenure of Office Act, Humphrey's | Senate role liquidated out; at-will for all officers is the fight |
| War initiation | Doubtful: "declare" is clear for sustained war | Mixed: two centuries of strikes, few deliberated | No: War Powers Resolution, Nixon veto | Liquidated at most for brief, limited strikes |
| Bank | Yes: "necessary" | Yes: 1791, 1816, *McCulloch* | Settled on constitutionality by 1816; Jackson's 1832 veto never became the settled view | Constitutionality liquidated; the institution died politically |
| General welfare spending | Yes: Madison v. Hamilton | Yes: Madison's dodges show members deliberating on constitutional grounds | Arguably, for Hamilton's reading, later adopted by [[United States v. Butler]] | Best candidate for a modern liquidation |
| Judicial supremacy | Yes: no arbiter text | Jefferson, Jackson, Lincoln resisted | Contested until *Cooper* | Judgments settled; opinion supremacy not liquidated |

**Oscillation points** — Did 1789 liquidate at-will removal, or only the rejection of a Senate role? Ranked: (1) only the Senate-role rejection is "clear"; (2) the majority's broader reading needs Madison's theory to have commanded the vote, which the "mixed coalition" record denies; (3) the dissent's 140-year counter-practice is real but faces the "adverse possession" answer.

**Traps**

- **"Liquidation = longstanding practice."** It requires indeterminacy and deliberation first.
- **"Slaughter both says the text is clear and says practice liquidated it."** Liquidation needs indeterminacy, so the majority cannot have both.
- **"Only First Congress practice counts."** Baude rejects the "First Congress canon" as Madison's theory.

**Canned hook**

- "Practice can settle this question only if it liquidated an indeterminate text: here [the text is open], [the branches deliberated on constitutional grounds], and [the losing side acquiesced], so the meaning is fixed."
- "This is gloss, not liquidation: the practice is long, but no one debated its constitutionality, so it deserves weight, not authority."

## 3. Constitutional themes and the dead hand

**Clause** — "We the People" (Preamble); "the supreme Law of the Land" (Art. VI cl. 2 → Art.VI-2); Article V's amendment route (Art.V).

**Bottom line** — The Constitution is supreme written law made by the people, binding on every officer and changeable only through Article V. Six themes supply the premises for structural argument: written constitution, popular sovereignty, separation of powers, federalism, rights, and imperfect congruence with justice ([[Constitutional Themes]]). Beneath them sits the dead-hand problem: why does a 1789 text bind, and each answer implies a method.

**The test — four answers to the dead hand** ([[The Dead-Hand Problem]])

1. **Jeffersonian:** "The earth belongs always to the living generation"; every constitution "naturally expires at the end of 19 years." Points to present judgment.
2. **Acceptance:** each generation could reject the Constitution and has not. Ambiguous between original meaning and current understanding.
3. **Inheritance:** "the children who inherit a house must accept its mortgage and its covenants." Points to the meaning inherited.
4. **Precommitment:** a nation persisting through time can bind its future self. Points to the commitment as made.

**Cases and who said what** — Madison's reply to Jefferson: a government "so often revised" would be "too mutable"; Tucker: the constitution is "a real existence"; Roane: a perfect Constitution becomes "a nose of wax"; Federalist 39's scorecard, "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both." Background on the Articles of Confederation: [[Before the Constitution]].

**Baude in class** — Rights, separation of powers, federalism as the organising split of the term; constitutional law is supreme over statutes and more ambiguous, so its fights run at a higher level of abstraction (session 01).

**Doctrine vs. best understanding** — The doctrine assumes the Constitution's authority; the dead-hand question asks whether that assumption is earned. Position to take: inheritance plus Article V — we use the institutions, so we take their limits, and the Constitution supplies its own lawful route to change ([[The Riddles of Article V]]; [[Amendment process - Art. V]]). Strongest counter: Article V is so hard that present consent is formal, not real.

**Fact triggers** — Q3 prompts on amendments, overruling, or "is the Constitution worth following."

**Traps** — **"Article I § 1 and Article II § 1 are parallel."** Article I vests "All legislative Powers herein granted"; Article II vests "The executive Power" with no limiting phrase.

**Canned hook** — "Whatever one thinks of the dead hand, the President who takes the oath inherits both the office's powers and its limits; he cannot claim the first while disowning the second."

# Part II — Separation of Powers: The Framework and the Legislative Power

## 1. The vesting clauses, formalism and functionalism

Module: [[Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances]]

**Clause.** "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress" (Art. I § 1 → Art.I-1); "The executive Power shall be vested in a President" (Art. II § 1 cl. 1 → Art.II-1.1); "The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court" (Art. III § 1 → Art.III-1). Point to the missing "herein granted" in Article II and to "a President" (one person).

**Bottom line.** Each branch may exercise only the power the Constitution vests in it, and one branch cannot take another's power even with that branch's consent. Where the text shares a power, the shared procedure is the only lawful route ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|INS v. Chadha]]; [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|Clinton v. City of New York]]). Classify the act first; the classification usually decides the case.

### The test

1. **What is the act in character and effect, whatever its label?** Legislative if it "alter[s] the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch" ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]]); a legislative-branch label did not save the FEC ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]); "no ifs, ands, or quasis" ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]]).
2. **Which vesting clause and which specific clause carries the power?** Article I lists powers "herein granted"; Article II follows its vesting clause with "a seemingly random assortment" of specific powers ([[Introduction to Article II]]); an unlisted power such as removal comes from the Vesting Clause or from nowhere.
3. **Does the text share the power, and was the shared procedure followed?** Lawmaking (Art. I § 7), principal-officer appointment (Art. II § 2 cl. 2), impeachment, war, appropriations (Art. I § 9 cl. 7).
4. **Did one House act alone?** Only the four express one-House powers carry legal force ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]]).
5. **Did the affected branch consent?** Consent does not cure ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|Clinton]]; [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]]).
6. **If the text is silent, where does the act fall under Jackson?** See § 2.
7. **Does practice settle the allocation?** Frankfurter's gloss versus "no branch may rely on adverse possession" ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]]).

### Cases and who said what

- [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] (1952, 6–3) → formalism wins the judgment (Youngstown, Black); functionalism wins the framework (Youngstown, Jackson).
- [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|INS v. Chadha]] (7–2) → "the Framers ranked other values higher than efficiency" (Chadha, Burger) versus "an important if not indispensable political invention" (Chadha, White).
- [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] (6–3) → formalism with one box: a power is executive or it is not. Best counter-case: [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] (5–4), where history, not function, preserves the Fed.
- **Distinguishing line:** If the power belongs to the President alone, a contrary statute is void (Slaughter); if Congress shares the subject, the President's authority depends on what Congress said (Youngstown).

### Baude in class

- The Constitution does three things: rights, separation of powers, federalism; constitutional law is supreme and "more ambiguous" than statutes (session 01).
- The energy-and-accountability argument works only where Congress has not spoken, so establish Congress's position before choosing a theory (module gloss).
- The administrative state cannot be a fourth branch; re-characterise the body into an existing branch and test it there, which is what Humphrey's did by calling the FTC quasi-legislative (sessions 07, module gloss).

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Formal classification, then the vesting clause; no balancing once an act is classified as another branch's ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]]); Jackson only for silence.
- **Off track:** The Court is formalist when policing Congress (Chadha, Clinton) and when expanding the President (Slaughter), and functionalist when tolerating agencies that make rules without § 7. White's "veritable fourth branch" charge is never answered on the merits.
- **Position:** The Court should apply formalism evenly: the "herein granted" asymmetry supports a residual executive power over officers, but no branch's "executive" label can carry lawmaking. Strongest counter: Brandeis's line that the separation exists "to preclude the exercise of arbitrary power," which supports functional review of aggrandizement rather than labels.

### Fact triggers

- This is a separation-of-powers problem when a statute places an officer or body outside the branch whose power it exercises.
- When a body is labelled "independent," "quasi-judicial," or "within the judicial branch but outside the supervision of any court" (2018 Q2).

### Oscillation points

1. **Strongest:** classification. The same act (Chadha's deportation) is executive when the Attorney General does it and legislative when the House does it; the difference is the procedure.
2. **Weakest for the government:** practice. Chadha struck 295 veto provisions; Slaughter rejected "practice-makes-perfect."

### Traps

- **"Both branches agreed, so it is valid."** Chadha and Clinton struck agreed schemes; Jackson's category one cannot give the President a share of § 7.
- **"Buckley bars independent offices."** Buckley bars Congress from filling offices, not from creating them; and Buckley, not Humphrey's (the FTC), is the FEC case.

### Canned hook

> "The first question is not whether this arrangement is wise but which branch's power it exercises; once the act is classified, the Constitution supplies the procedure, and consent between the branches cannot substitute for it (Chadha; Clinton)."

## 2. Youngstown and presidential power

Module: [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]]

**Clause.** "The executive Power" (Art.II-1.1); "Commander in Chief" (Art.II-2.1); "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" (Art.II-3); Congress's "legislative Powers" (Art.I-1). The only express emergency clause is the Suspension Clause, and it sits in Article I (Art.I-9.2).

**Bottom line.** The President needs authority from a statute or from the Constitution itself, and when he relies on the Constitution alone, the strength of his claim depends on what Congress has said. Under Jackson's concurrence in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]], authorization puts him at his "maximum," silence leaves him in a "zone of twilight," and defiance puts him at his "lowest ebb," where only a "conclusive and preclusive" power survives.

### The test

1. **What source does the President claim: statute or Constitution?** If a statute authorizes, stop. The 2023 Q1 model opening: "we're not in a Youngstown Category 1 situation because the President has no statutory authority."
2. **Which category?**
   - *Category one:* "all that he possesses in his own right plus all that Congress can delegate" (2001 AUMF in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).
   - *Category two:* silence; turns on "the imperatives of events and contemporary imponderables" ([[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]]; [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]]; [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] before ratification).
   - *Category three:* "express or implied will" against him (Youngstown; [[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]]; § 2262 in [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
3. **Refusal or silence?** What moves a case into category three: a rejected proposal (Taft-Hartley 1947 seizure amendment, read by Frankfurter as "You may not seize"); an occupied field ("three statutory policies inconsistent with this seizure," Jackson); an express prohibition. What keeps it in category two: "congressional inertia, indifference or quiescence."
4. **In category three, is the power exclusive?** Ask whether Congress is disabled from acting on the subject. Exclusive: pardon, removal of executive officers, whom to investigate and prosecute ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]); recognition of foreign sovereigns, the one power upheld at the lowest ebb (*Zivotofsky v. Kerry*).
5. **In category two, does practice supply a gloss?** "Systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned" (Youngstown, Frankfurter). Three 1941 seizures fail; 252 land withdrawals in *Midwest Oil* pass.
6. **Is the act lawmaking in character?** An order that sets policy and authorizes subordinate rules "like a statute" is legislation (Youngstown, Black).
7. **Is force turned outward or inward?** "Widest latitude" against "the outside world," none against "a lawful economic struggle between industry and labor" (Youngstown, Jackson).

### Cases and who said what

- [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] (1952, 6–3) → the seizure of the steel mills was unlawful.
  - (Youngstown, Black): power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself"; the Take Care Clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker."
  - (Youngstown, Jackson): the Vesting Clause is not "a grant in bulk of all conceivable executive power"; the Framers "knew what emergencies were"; emergency powers "would tend to kindle emergencies."
  - (Youngstown, Frankfurter): "the gloss which life has written upon" the Constitution; reserved a "short, explicitly temporary" seizure.
  - (Youngstown, Douglas): a seizure is a taking; only Congress can pay, so only Congress can authorize.
  - (Youngstown, Clark): where Congress has prescribed a procedure, the President must follow it (*Little v. Barreme*).
  - (Youngstown, Vinson dissenting, with Reed and Minton): holding the status quo "until Congress could act"; rejects the "messenger-boy concept of the Office."
- **Best counter-case:** [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]] (6–2, Miller) → "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law,'" so a marshal may guard a Justice without a statute. (Neagle, Lamar dissenting): "law" means enacted law.
  - **Distinguishing line:** Silence plus protection of the government's own officers sustains the President (Neagle); a congressionally occupied field plus regulation of private property condemns him (Youngstown).
- [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] → absolute criminal immunity within "conclusive and preclusive" authority; see [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]].
- [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] → the President must "resist force by force," but "has no power to initiate or declare a war"; see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

### Baude in class

- Black wrote for five Justices and is "official precedent"; Jackson supplies the three steps; the majority puts "text/structure > practice"; "Emergency ≠ Executive power" (session 02).
- The executive must "always need to cite authority when it's acting"; past practice does not help where "practice was wrong" (session 02).
- Frankfurter narrows usable practice to seizures that were emergency, temporary and congressionally permitted; Douglas reaches the result through the Takings Clause (session 02).
- On the Civil War materials: The Prize Cases differ from Youngstown because Congress was out of session (position unknown, not contrary) and the war was domestic; the Emancipation Proclamation goes back inside Youngstown as a seizure of property on land (module gloss).
- Gloss advice: in an emergency hypo, resist the pull toward category three; establish whether Congress has actually spoken, and if not, argue twilight (module gloss).

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Jackson's framework, with category three exits limited to exclusive powers; Trump v. United States converts that exit into an immunity zone.
- **Off track:** Frankfurter's gloss counts any long practice Congress tolerated. Liquidation is narrower and better: practice settles meaning only where the text is indeterminate, the practice reflects deliberate constitutional reasoning, and the dissenting side acquiesced with public sanction (see Part I, liquidation). Executive-only practice, mostly what OLC cites in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]], is assertion, not settlement.
- **Position:** A court should apply Jackson for the category and liquidation rather than gloss for practice; the list of conclusive and preclusive powers should stay short and textual. Strongest counter: Vinson's point that a President unable to hold the status quo in a crisis becomes a messenger boy.

### Fact triggers

- This is a Youngstown problem when the President acts by order on a subject Congress has legislated about.
- When Congress considered and rejected the President's chosen tool.
- When the President cites "the executive power" or the Take Care Clause as his only authority (2023 Q1 student loans).
- When an emergency is offered as the source of power.

### Oscillation points

1. **Strongest:** silence versus refusal. A single rejected amendment flips category two to three.
2. **Middle:** whether funding is authorization. Weakest category-one evidence; War Powers Resolution § 8(a)(1) forbids the inference.
3. **Weakest:** the move from "Congress cannot forbid this act" to "Congress cannot punish this act" (Trump v. United States); argue both sides.

### Traps

- **"Youngstown held the President was at his lowest ebb."** Black never used categories; name the opinion you apply.
- **"No authorization means lowest ebb."** Silence is category two; Neagle, Cox and the Prize Cases are silence cases the executive won.
- **"Category three means automatic defeat."** Defeat unless the power is conclusive and preclusive.

### Canned hook

> "Because the President cites no statute, this is not category one; the question is whether Congress's [silence/refusal] puts him in the twilight or at the lowest ebb, and on these facts [rejected proposal/occupied field] it is the latter, where he wins only if the power is conclusive and preclusive (Youngstown, Jackson) — which [the claimed power] is not."

## 3. Nondelegation

Module: [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]

**Clause.** "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress" (Art.I-1), read with the Necessary and Proper Clause (Art.I-8.18) and the Take Care Clause (Art.II-3).

**Bottom line.** Congress may give the executive broad discretion in carrying out a statute but may not hand over the power to make the law itself. A delegation is valid if Congress lays down "an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized . . . is directed to conform" ([[J. W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States|J.W. Hampton]], restated in [[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]]). The test is "not demanding": only two statutes have ever failed, both in 1935.

### The test

1. **Properly construed, does the statute delegate as much as the challenger says?** Read narrowly first; the inquiry "always begins (and often almost ends) with statutory interpretation" (Gundy, Kagan).
2. **Is the delegated power legislative at all?** Gorsuch: "generally applicable rules of conduct governing future actions by private persons." Government operations ([[The Post Roads Debate]]) and powers overlapping Article II (foreign affairs, command) tolerate more.
3. **Is there an intelligible principle?** "The general policy" plus "the boundaries of [his] authority" (American Power & Light); fails only where Congress "failed to articulate any policy or standard" (Mistretta).
4. **Is the standard as definite as standards already upheld?** "Public interest," "fair and equitable," "just and reasonable," air quality "requisite to protect the public health."
5. **In the alternative (dissent's test), does it fit a Gorsuch category?** Fill up the details (*Wayman v. Southard*); contingent fact-finding (*The Brig Aurora*); non-legislative or already-executive matters.
6. **Is the concern policed under another name?** Major questions doctrine; vagueness; [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]] and [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|Clinton]].
7. **Is any reserved congressional check lawful?** One-House veto void; sunset, report-and-wait, presented joint resolution valid.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy v. United States]] (2019, 5–3, no majority opinion) → SORNA's "specify the applicability" delegation upheld.
  - (Gundy, Kagan, plurality of four): the delegation was "distinctly small-bore"; "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional."
  - (Gundy, Alito, concurring in the judgment): would reconsider with a full Court, but would not single out this statute; endorses neither analysis. He is the fifth vote that keeps the test alive by default.
  - (Gundy, Gorsuch, dissenting with Roberts and Thomas): delegation defeats bicameralism and presentment; Congress "passed the potato to the Attorney General"; the intelligible principle was "plucked" from a narrower case.
  - Kavanaugh did not participate.
- [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] (1935, unanimous, including Brandeis and Cardozo) → code-making by private trade groups with no standards ([[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]]).
- **Best counter-case:** [[The Post Roads Debate]] (1791) → the House rejected Sedgwick's motion to let the President choose routes; Madison warned of leaving "no line of separation whatever."
  - **Distinguishing line:** Did Congress keep the choice that decides the matter (which roads, which offenders) or hand that choice to the executive? Kagan upheld SORNA only by reading it to keep the whether-to-register choice in Congress.
- After the casebook: *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025, 6–3, Kagan) applied and upheld the test; Gorsuch dissented.

### Baude in class

- Nondelegation is a line-drawing problem: Congress cannot delegate "legislative power" in pure form, but Zeno's paradox means it cannot specify every route between post offices; draw the line by policy, historical context, practice (session 03).
- Agreement between the branches is not enough, because the public good is involved (session 03).
- He posed, without answering, whether Gorsuch's categories differ from the intelligible principle; "Alito is saying he agrees with the dissent, but won't join them" (session 03).
- The test survives because of line-drawing problems and because the Major Questions Doctrine does the work by statutory interpretation (session 03).
- Implicit message of Chadha: delegation to the executive is fine; Congress cannot delegate power back to itself; delegation to private groups left open (session 04).
- Office hours: Gorsuch's objection in [[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]] is not that the Attorney General decides whether SORNA applies — ordinary prosecutorial discretion — but that he decides what the law is, a step above it.
- Office hours: a nondelegation holding is constitutional precedent, not statutory. Congress cannot cure it except by making the law less of a delegation; a later Court changes course by applying the intelligible-principle test differently.
- Office hours: *Loper Bright* lets courts fix a statute's meaning over the agency's, but a very ambiguous statute that broadly assigns a question to an agency is hard to take back; administrative law answers nondelegation by finding an intelligible principle in the statute.
- Office hours: nondelegation does not reach delegation to the States — the national-parks example, on Gorsuch's own Gundy logic that such a delegation returns power the recipient started with.

### Applying it: 2021 Q1 EPA climate statute

- **The text:** EPA may "make and enforce any such regulations as are necessary to prevent human activities from changing the global climate."
- **Current doctrine:** The statute should survive. "Necessary to prevent" states a goal at least as definite as "requisite to protect the public health," and Kagan's comparative method sustains goal-plus-necessity standards. The live risk is statutory: the major questions doctrine would narrow the grant rather than void it.
- **Gorsuch's test:** The statute fails. EPA would write generally applicable rules of private conduct across the whole economy, and "any such regulations" covers every human activity, so there is no detail to fill and no fact to find. Count votes: three dissenters plus Alito's stated willingness makes four to five.
- **Best understanding (departmentalist President):** The President should treat the delegation as invalid under the Post Roads principle, because Congress handed over the decisive choice of which activities to regulate. He should still sign if other duties justify it and decline to enforce the excess (see § 5, Baude's signing argument).

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** The intelligible principle, applied by comparison, after narrowing construction; a plurality and a default.
- **Off track:** The Gundy plurality's necessity argument is a "constitutional fudge" (Gundy Note 1); the 1791 House vote and Madison's warning point to a real limit.
- **Position:** The Court should adopt a decisive-choice test: Congress must make the choice on which the regulated party's liability turns, and may delegate details, fact-finding and matters already executive. Strongest counter: Sedgwick's point that execution always involves discretion, and Benson's observation that the 1791 bill designated no post offices at all, so Congress was already delegating.

### Fact triggers

- This is a nondelegation problem when a statute's operative standard is a goal ("prevent," "protect," "as necessary") with no rule of conduct.
- When the delegate is a private body or a commission given power to "establish any policies and rules necessary" (2018 Q2).
- When criminal liability, taxing, war or appropriations depend on the delegate's choice (Gundy; Gundy Note 3).

### Oscillation points

1. **Strongest:** narrowing construction. Most challenges die at step 1.
2. **Middle:** government operations versus private conduct; the Post Roads debate cuts against Gorsuch's definition even though Madison's side won.
3. **Weakest:** reviving Schechter; one good year "and 211 bad ones."

### Traps

- **"Gundy reaffirmed the test."** Four Justices endorsed it; it survived by default.
- **Reciting the test without narrowing the statute.** The plurality's work is statutory.
- **Calling Clinton a nondelegation holding.** Clinton decided presentment and found delegation "unnecessary to consider."

### Canned hook

> "Under current doctrine this delegation should survive: 'necessary to prevent' is no less intelligible than 'requisite to protect the public health' (Gundy, Kagan). But the doctrine stands on four votes and a default (Gundy, Alito), and on the better view — Madison's in the Post Roads Debate — Congress may not hand away the choice that decides who is regulated."

## 4. Bicameralism and presentment

Module: [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]]

**Clause.** "Every Bill which shall have passed the House of Representatives and the Senate, shall, before it become a Law, be presented to the President" (Art.I-7.2); "Every Order, Resolution, or Vote to which the Concurrence of the Senate and House . . . may be necessary" must be presented (Art.I-7.3). The four one-House powers: Art.I-2.5, Art.I-3.6, Art.II-2.2.

**Bottom line.** A law can be made, amended or repealed only by the identical text passing both Houses and going to the President. Any action "legislative in its character and effect" must follow that "single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure" ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|INS v. Chadha]]), and a presidential power to cancel enacted provisions is void even with Congress's consent ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|Clinton v. City of New York]]).

### The test

1. **Which branch acted, and what power is it presumed to exercise?** The Executive presumptively acts executively; one House presumptively acts legislatively ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]]).
2. **Is the act a lawmaking act?** Does it alter "legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch"? If Congress would otherwise have had to pass a statute, the device is legislation.
3. **Does one of the four express one-House powers apply?** No fifth is implied.
4. **Did identical text pass both Houses and go to the President, who acted on the whole bill before enactment?** "There is no provision in the Constitution that authorizes the President to enact, to amend, or to repeal statutes" (Clinton).
5. **Set aside consent, utility and practice.**
6. **Classify any reserved check by form.** One-House, committee and concurrent-resolution vetoes void (War Powers Resolution § 5(c)); presented joint resolution, sunset and report-and-wait valid.
7. **If the executive used discretion a valid statute gave him, ask delegation instead.**

### Cases and who said what

- [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|INS v. Chadha]] (1983, 7–2) → the one-House veto in INA § 244(c)(2) is void, and with it every legislative veto.
  - (Chadha, Burger): Madison feared evasion "by the simple expedient of calling a proposed law a 'resolution' or 'vote.'"
  - (Chadha, Powell, concurring in the judgment): the House "assumed a judicial function" by ruling on one person.
  - (Chadha, White, dissenting): "the death knell for nearly 200 other statutory provisions"; if Congress may delegate, it may reserve a check.
- **Best counter-case:** [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|Clinton v. City of New York]] (1998, 6–3, Stevens) → the Line Item Veto Act's "cancel" power is a partial repeal by the President alone.
  - (Clinton, Kennedy, concurring): "one Congress cannot yield up its own powers, much less those of other Congresses to follow."
  - (Clinton, Scalia, dissenting): "not a dime's worth of difference" between cancellation and discretion to spend; Breyer also dissented.
  - **Distinguishing line:** Chadha condemns a shortcut outside any enactment; Clinton condemns a power, granted by a valid statute, to change which enacted text is law. A discretion to decline to spend survives.

### Baude in class

- The organising question is legislative versus executive power, and the answer is procedural: the Attorney General and the House were doing "functionally the same thing" in Chadha; the difference is "the PROCESS of how that decision was reached" (session 04).
- Two tells that the House overreached: the action amends the immigration law's operation, and it fixates on specific people, which is administration (session 04).
- Clinton: the Item Veto Act let the President "change the text of the bill, which is different from execution"; Youngstown category one cannot cure it, though declining to spend "in practice . . . is still intact" (session 04).
- Severability: the Act survives; only the veto falls (session 04).
- Office hours sorting for Chadha: passing the statute is legislative; the Attorney General's suspension of deportation is executive; the House's veto of that suspension is legislative, so it needed bicameralism and presentment.

### Applying it: one-House actions that are and are not legislative (2021 Q2 hook)

- **Not legislative:** internal governance under Art. I § 5; each House's own officers ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]]); the auxiliary power to investigate in aid of legislation (*McGrain v. Daugherty*; *Trump v. Mazars*, outside the casebook excerpt).
- **Legislative:** any one-House act that changes a private party's legal position as a statute would ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]]).
- **2021 Q2 application:** Chadha should not bar either one-House subpoena, because a subpoena is an incident of each House's own powers, not a change in the law. The House subpoena rides on the express one-House impeachment power (Art.I-2.5); the Senate subpoena needs a valid legislative purpose (court-packing qualifies). The real fight is pretext and the burden on Justices' personal records, not § 7.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Formal and categorical; consent and practice irrelevant.
- **Off track:** Only the presumption keeps agency rulemaking outside § 7 (White's "veritable fourth branch"). Clinton turns on drafting ("cancel" versus "decline to spend"), so the Court was policing delegation under a presentment label.
- **Position:** Chadha should stand as a correct textual reading; Clinton should be defended only as a formal rule about changing enacted text, with Scalia right that the underlying problem is delegation. Strongest counter: White's original-meaning reply that cl. 3 by its terms reaches only votes needing both Houses' concurrence.

### Fact triggers

- This is a presentment problem when one House, a committee, or a concurrent resolution can change an agency's or a private party's position.
- When the President can cancel, suspend or rewrite part of an enacted law.
- When a one-House subpoena or resolution is challenged as "legislation."

### Oscillation points

1. **Strongest:** the lawmaking-act gate; most cases are decided by classification.
2. **Middle:** Art. I § 5 rules devices (deemed passage, separate enrollment); the final product is presented, which thins the Chadha objection.

### Traps

- **"The defect was that only one House acted."** Presentment is independent; a two-House concurrent resolution also fails.
- **"Clinton forbids declining to spend."** Clinton held only that a statutory power to cancel violates presentment.
- **"A signing statement is a line-item veto."** A signing statement changes nothing in the law ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).

### Canned hook

> "The House reached a defensible result by the wrong route. Ask what Congress would have had to do without the device: here, pass a statute. That makes the act legislative in character and effect, and Art. I § 7 supplies the only procedure for it (Chadha)."

## 5. The power of the purse

Module: [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]]

**Clause.** "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law; and a regular Statement and Account of the Receipts and Expenditures of all public Money shall be published from time to time" (Art.I-9.7). Distinguish the spending power, "to pay the Debts and provide for the . . . general Welfare" (Art.I-8.1).

**Bottom line.** No federal money may be spent unless a statute appropriates it. Congress may fund, refuse to fund, or fund on conditions, and a condition the executive thinks unconstitutional does not free the executive to spend, because voiding a restriction creates no appropriation. Congress may not use the purse to do what another clause forbids ([[United States v. Lovett (1946)|United States v. Lovett]]); the hard question is who can enforce the clause.

### The test

1. **Is there an appropriation made by law?** Express, not inferred; permanent or current ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)|House v. Burwell]]).
2. **No appropriation at all, or spending outside a contested one?** The first is constitutional; the second is statutory ("Article I is not a talisman," Burwell).
3. **Is a rider forbidden by another clause?** Bill of Attainder Clause (Lovett); Compensation Clauses; arguably exclusive presidential powers.
4. **Which presidential response?** Veto; sign, object and comply (Roosevelt in Lovett); sign and construe (Obama on § 2262); spend without appropriation (forbidden).
5. **Which Youngstown category?** Spending against an express denial is category three, and a power to spend unappropriated money is the least plausible conclusive and preclusive power.
6. **Who can sue?** The House as an institution on a no-appropriation theory: yes (Burwell; *House v. Mnuchin*). Individual members, a State declining a grant, a taxpayer: no ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]). A party denied a specific payment: yes (Lovett).
7. **For war: defund, repeal, or continue paying?** Only the cutoff is self-executing ([[Terminating War]]).

### Cases and who said what

- [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)|House v. Burwell]] (D.D.C. 2015, Collyer, J.) → the House "as an institution" suffers concrete injury when funds are drawn without an appropriation; statutory claims dismissed. Never reviewed on appeal; the Trump administration stopped the payments and settled.
- **Best counter-case:** [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] → a taxpayer's interest is "comparatively minute and indeterminable"; a State can frustrate a conditional grant "by the simple expedient of not yielding."
  - **Distinguishing line:** An injury that belongs to the plaintiff alone (the House's constitutional role) supports standing; an injury shared with everyone does not.
- [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] → signed to avoid "a lapse in appropriations"; construed § 2262 "not to abrogate these Presidential prerogatives."
- *Train v. City of New York* → impoundment rejected on statutory grounds; (Clinton, Scalia): a discretion to "decline to spend" is valid beyond "the slightest doubt."

### Live problems from the materials

- **[[Executive Order 14242|EO 14242]] (closing the Department of Education):** "To the maximum extent appropriate and permitted by law" concedes the President cannot abolish a statutory department; the question is what remains short of abolition (declining to hire, reorganising, slowing disbursement). Section 2(b) adds an executive-written condition to appropriated money, which asks whether the executive may add a condition at all, not whether it is coercive under *Dole*.
- **[[Trump v. IRS settlement agreement]] (the Anti-Weaponization Fund):** The plaintiffs take no money, so cl. 7 is satisfied as to them. "Shall establish funding" names no appropriation, and a settlement and an Attorney General's order are neither "Appropriations" nor "made by Law"; the sweep of leftovers to accounts the President designates is a transfer between purposes. The five Members may be officers; see [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].
- **Impoundment:** Cl. 7 does not say appropriated money must be drawn, so a refusal to spend belongs under [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] ([[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]]).

### Baude in class

- The purse has an internal limit: Congress cannot use appropriations as a means to an "impermissible" end, such as defunding vetoes or executive orders (session 05).
- With Scalia in Clinton: traditional discretion exists, "but if Congress mandates that all money be used, POTUS must spend it"; impeachment is the remedy (session 05).
- EO 14242: an executive that hires no one cannot spend; an inherent power not to spend is "debated" (session 05).
- Standing asymmetry: unspent money gives the intended recipient a suit; unappropriated spending is "harder," and the House's institutional injury is the theory; Baude agrees that sometimes no one has standing, and congressional standing needs at least a whole chamber (session 05).
- The signing statement is not a line-item veto: it changes no text and binds no successor (session 05).
- Office hours: hold two themes together — the purse is Congress's "ultimate check" on the executive, and Congress cannot use spending to do what the Constitution otherwise forbids ([[United States v. Lovett (1946)|Lovett]]).
- Office hours: on a defunded set of positions, Obama's answer to § 2262 was in effect "I'm going to keep the roles." That is either a [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)|Burwell]] violation (voiding a restriction creates no appropriation) or a Take Care defence, plus the claim that Congress cannot be so specific that it constrains execution. Departmentalism permits Obama's reading; under judicial supremacy he must comply and wait for a court.
- Office hours: Congress has a usable injury only where it did not sign off; when Congress passed the bill, no one has standing ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Mellon]]).
- Office hours: an appropriations rider barring federal funds for prosecuting state-legal marijuana shows the purse doing separation-of-powers work on enforcement.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Cl. 7 is a rule; the fight is over reaching the merits; one unreviewed district court opinion grants House standing.
- **Off track:** A check no one can enforce becomes political only, which cuts against Mellon's breadth; inter-branch suits sit uneasily with [[The Correspondence of the Justices]].
- **Position:** Courts should hear no-appropriation claims by a full House, because the injury belongs to the chamber alone; an executive-created settlement fund with no appropriation should be held to violate cl. 7. A President who signs a bill with an unconstitutional rider acts lawfully if other duties justify signing and he declines to enforce the rider (Baude, *Signing Unconstitutional Laws*, 86 Ind. L.J. 303 (2011)) — but a void funding denial still leaves nothing to spend. Strongest counter: Mellon's warning that every taxpayer would then sue over "every other appropriation act."

### Fact triggers

- This is a purse problem when money moves without a named appropriation (settlement funds, transfers, "establish funding").
- When an appropriations rider targets persons, positions, or a presidential function.
- When the President declines to spend, staff, or disburse what Congress funded.

### Oscillation points

1. **Strongest:** no appropriation versus contested scope; the label decides whether there is a constitutional claim at all.
2. **Middle:** duty to spend; strong where the mandate is absolute, weak where the appropriation is "not exceeding."
3. **Weakest:** a purse-based end-run on exclusive executive powers; the line between defunding a war and defunding the Commander in Chief.

### Traps

- **"Congress's purse is unlimited."** Lovett.
- **"An unconstitutional rider frees the President to spend."** Voiding a restriction creates no appropriation.
- **Confusing cl. 7 with the spending power.** Whether Congress may spend on an object is Art. I § 8 cl. 1; see [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].

### Canned hook

> "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law (Art.I-9.7). A settlement and an executive order are neither, so the first question is which statute appropriated this money; if none did, the scheme fails whatever its merits, and the only question left is who can sue (Burwell; Mellon)."

## 6. Suspension of habeas corpus

Module: [[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]]

**Clause.** "The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it" (Art.I-9.2). The Clause does not say who suspends; the argument is from its placement in Article I.

**Bottom line.** Only Congress may suspend the privilege, and only during rebellion or invasion endangering the public safety ([[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]]). Suspension withdraws the remedy, not the underlying rights. The live contest is who decides, and what follows when the executive refuses the writ.

### The test

1. **Is there a "Rebellion or Invasion"?** Nothing less triggers the Clause.
2. **Who judged public safety required it?** Bates relies on [[Luther v. Borden]] for deference to the President.
3. **Did Congress act?** Lincoln's April 1861 order to General Scott was private; Congress authorized suspension by statute in 1863.
4. **Is the suspension confined in place and time?** Lincoln's best version: temporary, until Congress convenes.
5. **Does the detention rest on anything beyond suspension?** Due process, warrants and speedy trial survive ([[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Merryman]], Taney).
6. **If the writ is refused, did the court have jurisdiction?** Routes to [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

### Cases and who said what

- [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] (1861, Taney on circuit, sitting alone) → the privilege "could not be suspended, except by act of congress"; Taney filed the record and sent it to the President because his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome." No release order issued.
- **Best counter-case:** [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates opinion]] (1861) → the departments are "co-ordinate and coequal"; if one branch determines the extent of all three branches' powers, "that one can control the whole government"; "a judge, at chambers" cannot review the President.
  - **Distinguishing line:** Bates justifies refusal on lack of jurisdiction over a political subject, not on a general power to ignore judgments.
- [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] → Congress reached a similar result lawfully by withdrawing appellate habeas jurisdiction.

### Baude in class and the scholarly fight

- Baude ran Merryman and Bates as the departmentalism problem in its sharpest form: the Clause sits in Article I, and Lincoln suspended anyway (module gloss).
- Paulsen's "Merryman power": no defensible middle ground between judicial supremacy and executive non-execution of decrees.
- Baude's judgment power: the executive "must obey judicial judgments, as long as the judiciary properly possessed jurisdiction" (Baude, *The Judgment Power*, 96 Geo. L.J. 1807 (2008)), which is the best explanation of Bates. Paulsen's reply: jurisdiction "gives away the game."
- Youngstown overlay: Lincoln acted against the Clause's placement in Article I, at the lowest ebb (module gloss); the 1863 statute moved later suspensions to category one.

### Position

- The executive should be held to lack a suspending power except, arguably, a temporary one pending Congress's return; and it should obey any habeas judgment of a court with jurisdiction. Strongest counter: Lincoln's necessity argument.

### Fact triggers

- This is a suspension problem when the executive detains civilians and refuses or ignores habeas, or when Congress strips habeas routes instead.

### Traps

- **"Lincoln defied an order to release Merryman."** No release order issued.
- **"Merryman is a Supreme Court decision."** One Justice on circuit.
- **"The Clause names Congress."** Taney's argument is placement and history.
- **"Cooper v. Aaron settles Merryman."** [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper]] concerns state officials under the Supremacy Clause, not coordinate federal departments.

### Canned hook

> "The Suspension Clause sits in Article I among the limits on Congress, so a President who suspends the writ alone acts at his lowest ebb unless Congress is unable to convene (Merryman; Youngstown, Jackson)."

## 7. Bills of attainder

Module: [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]]

**Clause.** "No Bill of Attainder or ex post facto Law shall be passed" (Art.I-9.3); the state counterpart is Art.I-10.1.

**Bottom line.** Congress may not single out people and punish them by statute instead of leaving guilt to a court. The Clause reaches "legislative acts, no matter what their form, that apply either to named individuals or to easily ascertainable members of a group in such a way as to inflict punishment on them without a judicial trial" ([[United States v. Lovett (1946)|United States v. Lovett]]).

### The test

1. **Is it a legislative act?**
2. **Does it single out named people or an easily ascertainable group?**
3. **Does it inflict punishment?** Permanent exclusion from a vocation is "punishment, and of a most severe type"; disqualification for non-retributive reasons is not.
4. **Without a judicial trial?** In Lovett, a secret subcommittee "inquest of office."
5. **Does form disguise punishment?** Purpose and effect control; a pay cutoff can be an attainder.

### Cases and who said what

- [[United States v. Lovett (1946)|United States v. Lovett]] (1946, Black; no dissent; Jackson took no part) → § 304's pay cutoff for Lovett, Watson and Dodd was an attainder. (Lovett, Frankfurter, with Reed, concurring in the judgment): the Clause is "defined by history"; a true attainder specified an offense and declared guilt.
- **Best counter-case:** [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] → § 2262 defunded positions, not people, with no finding of guilt, so it raises separation of powers rather than attainder.
  - **Distinguishing line:** Positions with no legislative finding of guilt: no attainder. Named people on a record of findings of disloyalty: attainder.

### Baude in class

- The line is valence, not specificity: a negative, criminal-type judgment on an individual is forbidden; "positive, targeted, civil actions aimed at certain people" are valid (session 04, module gloss).
- Gloss advice: watch for a burden dressed as a civil measure; argue whether the act is punitive in character, not whether it names someone (module gloss).

### Fact triggers

- This is an attainder problem when a statute or rider names people or an identifiable group and attaches a burden, especially after legislative findings of misconduct or hearings attacking an officeholder.

### Traps

- **"An attainder must impose criminal punishment."** Section 304 designated no crime.
- **"Any statute naming a person is an attainder."** Specificity is necessary, not sufficient.
- **"Lovett is a removal case."** The removal claim was not reached.

### Canned hook

> "Naming the officer is not enough; the question is whether Congress has substituted its own finding of guilt for a court's (Lovett). Here [findings/permanent bar] supply that punitive element, so the rider is an attainder whatever its form as an appropriation."

# Part III — The Executive Power

## 1. Appointments and the officer line

**Clause.** "[B]y and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, shall appoint . . . all other Officers of the United States . . . but the Congress may by Law vest the Appointment of such inferior Officers, as they think proper, in the President alone, in the Courts of Law, or in the Heads of Departments" — Art. II § 2 cl. 2 (Art.II-2.2). Each House's own officers: Art. I § 2 cl. 5, § 3 cl. 5. Recess: Art. II § 2 cl. 3. Module: [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].

**Bottom line.** Congress creates offices but never fills them. Anyone "exercising significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States" is an officer and must be appointed through Art.II-2.2 ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]); Congress may prescribe who is eligible, but it may not choose, or hand the choice to someone outside the Clause's list.

### The test

1. **Officer or employee?** Continuing position + significant (not ministerial) authority + under federal law. Rulemaking, civil enforcement and litigating "for vindicating public rights" make an officer; investigating and reporting to Congress does not ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]]). The statutory label (the FEC "in the legislative branch") does not control.
2. **Principal or inferior?** The answer decides only whether Senate consent may be dropped. An officer with no superior below the President is principal. [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] upheld a court-appointed independent counsel as inferior; *Edmond* (1997) asks whether the officer's work is directed and supervised by a principal officer.
3. **For a principal officer: the President nominates and the Senate alone consents.** Confirmation by both Houses is its own defect ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]]).
4. **For an inferior officer: did Congress vest appointment in a listed appointer** — "the President alone," "the Courts of Law," or "the Heads of Departments"? The Speaker and President pro tempore are none of these. Interbranch appointment is allowed (a court appointing a prosecutor in [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]]) unless "incongruous."
5. **Do statutory qualifications narrow the field or transfer the choice?** Baude's line from class: prescribing who may serve ("learned in the law," partisan-balance caps) is valid; naming the appointee transfers the choice and is not. Extension (Ashwin's, not Baude's): confining the President to someone else's list transfers the choice the same way.
6. **New office or germane added duty?** Germane duties need no second appointment ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]; *Shoemaker*; *Weiss*).
7. **Remedy: sever.** [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]] struck the appointment mechanism, kept the agency's informational functions and its past acts, and stayed the judgment.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]] (1976, unanimous on appointments) → FEC commissioners chosen by the Speaker and President pro tempore cannot exercise enforcement power; the Necessary and Proper Clause cannot authorize what Art.II-2.2 "by clear implication prohibits." (Buckley, White) — the Court's leading functionalist refused to bend here.
- [[The Federalist No. 76]] → one appointer begets "a livelier sense of duty"; an appointing assembly trades "Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that."
- [[The Federalist No. 70]] → unity and energy; the premise for single-head presidential choice.
- [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] (1988, 7–1) → a court may appoint an inferior officer with limited jurisdiction and tenure. (Morrison, Scalia dissenting) — the counsel is not "inferior" to anyone.
- [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] → best counter-case. Distinguishing line: unbroken founding practice can settle that existing officers take added duties without new commissions; a scheme invented in 1974 gets the text applied strictly.
- [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] → appointment complete on signature; delivery "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law."
- [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] (2010) → the SEC as a whole is a "Head[] of Department" able to appoint PCAOB members.

### Baude in class

- Officer status is a threshold of power, not a title — "significant authority," tied back to [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]].
- Structural puzzle: [[The Federalist No. 76]] presents appointment as a presidential choice checked by the Senate, yet inferior officers can sit outside the President's appointment entirely.
- Founders did not anticipate parties, producing "separation of parties, not powers"; states elect executive officers separately while the national government elects only the President — is the federal executive too unitary or the states too fragmented?
- On Buckley's dual-House confirmation: ask whether the defect is severable, and why it differs from an expert qualification ("learned in the law"), which is allowed.
- For odd officers, ask who appointed the officer's superior.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Buckley's "significant authority" test, formal and categorical.
- **Off track?** Jennifer Mascott's founding-era meaning — "any government official with responsibility for an ongoing governmental duty" — would make thousands of current employees officers. *Lucia* (2018) moved toward that reading by making SEC ALJs officers.
- **Should position:** the Buckley line should hold and should be read with Mascott's breadth at the margin, because the Clause's purpose is accountability for whoever exercises the sovereign's continuing authority. Strongest counter: Stuart v. Laird practice and the cost of re-papering the civil service.

### Fact triggers

- Congress, a House, or a congressional officer picks or confirms members.
- A statute confines the President to a slate or a list prepared by others.
- A body "within the judicial branch" exercises rulemaking or spending power ([[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]]).
- Judges sit on a non-adjudicative commission (*Mistretta* territory).

### Oscillation points — the 2018 Q2 template (Independent Defender Commission)

- **Commissioners are officers** (rulemaking, appointing heads of defender offices, compensating counsel) and principal (no superior), so presidential nomination and Senate consent are required — and provided.
- **Slate from the Chief Justice and defense attorneys** — strongest objection: the slate transfers the choice to people outside the Clause (an extension of Baude's "choosing who serves" line). Weaker counter: the Senate already constrains the field, and recommendation lists have long practice.
- **Partisan cap (≤4 of 7) and judge cap (≤3)** — weaker objections: qualifications narrow the field and carry deep practice (FTC, FEC). After [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]] the partisan cap's rationale (independence) has lost its doctrinal home.
- **No removal except impeachment** — a removal problem; see § 2.
- **The dissent's alternative (district judges in each circuit elect one of their number)** — commissioners would be principal officers chosen by something other than nomination and consent, and a circuit's district judges voting as an electorate are not a "Court[] of Law"; the scheme fails even if commissioners were inferior.
- **Best fix (Part B):** make commissioners inferior officers, supervised by and appointed by a listed appointer (the President, or the Supreme Court as a "Court of Law," subject to *Morrison*'s incongruity check), and keep removal with that appointer.

### Traps

- **"Congress may appoint inferior officers."** Congress appears nowhere in the list.
- **"The label decides the branch."** Buckley looked at the power exercised.
- **"Appointment and removal rise and fall together."** Separate questions, separate answers.
- **"Any citizen can challenge a defective appointment."** [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] — no generalized-grievance standing.

### Canned hook

> "The commissioners exercise significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States, so they are officers who must be appointed under Art.II-2.2 (Buckley). Congress may tell the President what kind of person to choose, but not whom; the slate requirement does the second."

## 2. Removal after Slaughter and Cook

**Clause.** "The executive Power shall be vested in a President" — Art. II § 1 cl. 1 (Art.II-1.1); "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" — Art. II § 3 (Art.II-3); Congress's side: Necessary and Proper, Art. I § 8 cl. 18 (Art.I-8.18). No removal clause except impeachment (Art.II-4). Module: [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

**Bottom line.** The President may fire at will any officer who exercises executive power, whether the agency has one head or five ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]]). Protection survives only for a body exercising "no part of the executive power," for the categories Slaughter reserved, and for the Federal Reserve on a First-and-Second-Bank historical ground, where "cause" is judicially reviewable and sets "a substantial threshold" ([[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]]).

### The test

1. **Does Congress or the Senate take a role in removal?** Always invalid — rejected in [[The Decision of 1789]], the ground of [[Myers v. United States|Myers]], and *Bowsher*.
2. **Does the office exercise executive power?** Rulemaking, investigation and in-house adjudication, or suing for penalties — any one suffices; "no ifs, ands, or quasis about it" ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]]).
3. **If yes, at-will removal is required.** Multimember structure, partisan balance and fixed terms do not save the protection.
4. **If no executive power, Congress may protect the office** — the Humphrey's residue (a body that only investigates and reports).
5. **Reserved category?** The Fed (decided in [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]]); non-Article III judges and purely adjudicatory bodies (*Wiener*); inferior officers with limited duties ([[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]]). [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] still bars two layers of for-cause protection.
6. **Where protection survives, run Cook's inquiry:** reviewability; who decides cause (courts, unless Congress commits it to the President); threshold ("substantial, reasonable and just"); factors (seriousness, nexus to duties); pretext; notice and a chance to respond; interim relief.

### Cases and who said what

- [[The Decision of 1789]] → the First Congress rejected Senate consent and impeachment-only removal; whether it endorsed Madison's constitutional theory "is not so clear."
- [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] (1868) → Johnson impeached for firing Stanton under the Tenure of Office Act; acquitted 35–19.
- [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] (1926, 6–3, Taft) → Senate-consent requirement for removing a postmaster invalid.
- [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] (1935, unanimous) → FTC "quasi-legislative / quasi-judicial," so for-cause protection valid.
- [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] (1988, 7–1) → for-cause protection for an inferior officer with limited duties upheld. (Morrison, Scalia dissenting) — "this wolf comes as a wolf."
- [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] (2010, 5–4, Roberts) → two layers of for-cause protection invalid.
- [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] (2020, 5–4, Roberts) → a single-head agency must be at will. (Seila Law, Thomas) — would overrule Humphrey's; Slaughter did.
- [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026, 6–3, Roberts) → leading case. "[I]f anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it"; "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession"; "three branches, but only one President." (Slaughter, Gorsuch) — the President now effectively exercises the agencies' delegated legislative and judicial power too. (Slaughter, Sotomayor dissenting) — 1789 chose a default Congress may vary; reliance.
- [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] (2026, 5–4, Roberts with Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Jackson) → best counter-case. Distinguishing line: protection survives where the office sits in a founding-era tradition of insulation — the First and Second Banks — not because independence is valuable. (Cook, Thomas dissenting) — the Banks "possessed no sovereign power"; the majority's real ground is policy.

### Baude in class

- Removal is a spectrum: Unitary Executive ←→ Congressional Control, with "Congress says POTUS can remove people" in the middle.
- **History is "a wash"**: 1789 → Tenure of Office Act → Myers → Humphrey's → Morrison → Free Enterprise Fund → Seila Law → Slaughter and Cook.
- In 1789, option four — Congress controls removal through the Necessary and Proper Clause — "could've been a valid position"; "[h]ard to tell if this side could've also won."
- Unitary arguments: Federalist 70 energy and accountability; Take Care; the Vesting Clause lacks Article I's "herein granted"; "a President" is one person. Counter: firing someone ≠ executing policy, since the replacement still needs Senate confirmation.
- Congressional-control arguments: the energy argument works "only when Congress doesn't speak"; Article I lets Congress "establish" offices; Necessary and Proper fills constitutional silence.
- Humphrey's does not adopt congressional control; it recharacterizes the FTC as not executive.
- Open after Slaughter: civil servants five or six layers below an agency head; non-Article III judges; the Registrar of Copyrights (appointed by the Librarian of Congress). Working answer: whoever appointed the officer's boss may be able to remove the officer.
- Cook: the Fed is "maybe like Georgetown" — not exercising sovereign power ("but it is"); the Court instead distinguishes by history.
- **EXAM SIGNAL:** can the unitary-executive and congressional-control arguments reach a middle ground?
- Office hours: the President may in theory fire down the chain until someone complies — the Saturday Night Massacre path; the law is unclear, but in theory he can. If the Attorney General is fired, the President can pick another Senate-confirmed officer to act.
- Office hours: the unitary-executive theory is more partisan-driven than congressional control — Congress is many people and its laws bind every administration, while unitary control runs under one ship and does not carry forward.

### The middle ground (built for the exam signal)

1. **Congress controls the office; the President controls the officer.** Art.I-8.18 lets Congress create, fund, shape and abolish the office; Art.II-1.1 means whoever executes the law answers to the President.
2. **The Appointments Clause draws the line.** Principal officers exercising executive power: at will. Inferior officers whose appointment Congress has vested in a head of department or a court: Congress may condition their tenure, because the Clause itself lets Congress place inferior officers outside the President's direct choice ([[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]]; Baude's "boss's appointer" answer).
3. **Function decides, not form.** A body that only investigates and reports, or a pure adjudicator, exercises no executive power and may be protected (Humphrey's residue; *Wiener*).
4. **Process, not substance.** Where protection survives, Congress may require notice, reasons and a real cause showing ([[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]]), without dictating whom the President keeps for executive work.
5. **History-anchored exceptions only.** The Fed survives because 1791 must be read with 1789 — the same Congress that made the Decision of 1789 chartered an uncontrolled Bank.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Slaughter's power-based test with Cook's historical carve-out.
- **Off track?** If 1789 is liquidation (Madison's settlement by deliberate practice), Slaughter needs acquiescence and public sanction — and the Tenure of Office Act, Humphrey's and ninety years of independent agencies look like dissent, not acquiescence. If history is a wash, as Baude says, liquidation never occurred, and the text (silence + Art.I-8.18) arguably leaves the question to Congress.
- **Should position:** the President should have at-will control over principal officers who execute the law, because unity in Art.II-1.1 is the clearest textual signal and accountability is its point; Congress's room should run through the inferior-officer route and through process. Strongest counter: Sotomayor's default-rule reading of 1789 plus Hamilton's "displace" in Federalist 77.

### Fact triggers

- Fixed terms, "for cause," or "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance" language.
- Multimember, partisan-balanced commissions.
- An office protected by a protected appointer (two layers).
- Officers in odd homes: the Library of Congress, a body "within the judicial branch," a congressionally chartered corporation.
- A firing announced on social media with no stated reasons (Cook process).

### Oscillation points

- **The Fed carve-out: the Court's route vs. Baude's.** Before the decisions, Baude ("How To Save The Federal Reserve," *Divided Argument*, May 2025) proposed using stare decisis discretion to preserve Humphrey's for the Fed alone, on the view that its non-executive function is very important. The Court in [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]] instead rested on First and Second Bank history.
  - Stronger for the Court's route: history is formalist on Slaughter's own method and needs no concession that independence is a policy good.
  - Stronger for Baude's route: candid about being precedent-based, avoids Thomas's charge that the Banks were private corporations with no sovereign power, and keeps the scope question (monetary policy vs. bank supervision) tied to function.
- **Non-Article III judges.** Baude, "Adjudication Outside Article III," 133 Harv. L. Rev. 1511 (2020): ask what kind of governmental power a tribunal holds. Executive adjudicators exercise executive power bound by due process — which on Slaughter's logic makes them removable, with due process rather than tenure as the protection. The Court reserved the question.
- **Inferior officers below at-will heads.** An ALJ protected only against now-at-will commissioners is one layer, so a [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] question that may survive.

### Traps

- **"Humphrey's was flatly overruled."** The no-executive-power residue survives; Morrison, *Wiener* and non-Article III courts are untouched.
- **"Multimember is safe."** The number of heads is irrelevant after Slaughter.
- **"Cook holds Cook cannot be removed."** Statutory process on a stay application; cause left open.
- **"The Fed survives because independence is valuable."** The Article II ground is history.
- **"[[United States v. Lovett (1946)|Lovett]] is a removal case."** Bill of attainder; removal undecided.

### Canned hook

> "After Slaughter the question is not how the agency is built but what power it wields: if the office executes the law against private parties, the President must be able to remove its head at will, and Humphrey's survives only for bodies that exercise 'no part of the executive power.' The only live question is whether this office sits in the Fed's historical tradition or a category Slaughter reserved."

## 3. Enforcement discretion, suspending and dispensing

**Clause.** "[H]e shall take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" — Art. II § 3 (Art.II-3); Oath, Art. II § 1 cl. 8 (Art.II-1.8); purse, Art. I § 9 cl. 7 (Art.I-9.7); pardon, Art. II § 2 cl. 1 (Art.II-2.1); federal property, Art. IV § 3 cl. 2 (Art.IV-3.2). Module: [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].

**Bottom line.** The executive decides whom to prosecute and how to spend scarce resources, and courts do not review those choices ([[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]]). The executive may not suspend a statute or license violations in advance: a consciously adopted general policy of not enforcing a mandatory statute is abdication ([[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]]).

### The test

1. **What law is being executed?** Usually a statute; [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]] adds obligations "fairly and properly inferrible" from the Constitution.
2. **What did Congress say?** Silence or authorization = Youngstown category two (Cox); "authorized and directed," with means specified = category three (Adams). See [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]].
3. **Form: individual decision, priority framework, or categorical policy?** Frameworks are lawful if case-by-case discretion is real ([[The Thompson Memo]]).
4. **Retrospective or prospective?** Declining to prosecute past conduct excuses no one in advance; assurance about future conduct resembles a dispensation.
5. **Do benefits ride along?** Work authorization, tolling or payments need independent statutory authority.
6. **Ground: resources, policy disagreement, or constitutional objection?** Resources is strongest; policy disagreement fails; a constitutional objection is a distinct, higher-order ground ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
7. **Abdication?** Reserved in [[Heckler v. Chaney]] n.4; never applied by the Supreme Court.
8. **Paying the violators?** Then an appropriations question ([[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]]).
9. **Who can sue?** Usually the decisive barrier (*United States v. Texas* (2023); [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]]).

### Cases and who said what

- [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]] (5th Cir. en banc 1965) → leading case. Two different 4–3 majorities: signing cannot be compelled; drafting can. (Cox, Jones) — formalist: prosecution is executive. (Cox, Brown) — swing vote on both questions: "The power not to initiate is indeed awesome. But it has to reside somewhere." (Cox, Wisdom) — strong discretion with a federalism overlay. (Cox, Rives dissenting in part) — the grand-jury guarantee is itself a law to be faithfully executed.
- [[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]] (D.C. Cir. en banc 1973) → best counter-case. Distinguishing line: mandatory command + specified means + general refusal (+ payments to violators) = reviewable abdication; a choice inside a working program stays under Cox.
- [[Heckler v. Chaney]] (1985) → APA presumption that refusals to act are unreviewable; n.4 reserves abdication.
- [[The Thompson Memo]] (OLC 2014) → priorities lawful; deferred action must leave real case-by-case discretion. Its three concessions about deferred action (toleration rather than declination, attached benefits, an invitation to apply) are the best brief against it.
- [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] → declining to treat a rider as binding on constitutional grounds.

### Baude in class

- Cox's four opinions as a spectrum: Jones (cannot compel the signature) → Brown (must prepare, need not sign; aids the "public shaming effort") → the dissent (must bring the case so the declination happens "in public") → Wisdom (strong discretion; national AG against a Mississippi grand jury and judge).
- If someone else signs: "No if unitary executive"; "[y]es, maybe if there are independent special prosecutors."
- Adams limits: (1) statutory mandate; (2) general policy; (3) resources provided and misused under the guise of discretion.
- Marijuana hypo: stop all prosecution but leave a case-by-case backdoor → "typically allowed, but shows major rule of law concerns."
- Thompson Memo: prioritization is a clean use of discretion; deferred action attaches benefits to a general policy. "[M]ust Congress have to add 'this law must be enforced' after every law it passes?"
- **Hypo he pressed (Class 8):** would the answer differ if the President cites unconstitutionality rather than policy disagreement?
- The real obstacle is standing: "Who is being harmed by these 'non-enforcements'? Who has standing?" (TikTok ban, National Firearms Act).
- Office hours: the baseline expectation is that a law will not be fully enforced — a baseline separate from the presumption of constitutionality — so a non-enforcement hypo starts from under-enforcement as normal and argues what makes this instance different.
- Office hours: writing "you must enforce this law" into a statute is not much of a limiting principle; it is the same line-drawing problem as administrative law.
- Office hours: Cox's prepared-but-unsigned indictment is symbolic; a future administration can simply restart the prosecution.
- Office hours: categorical marijuana non-enforcement could be challenged under [[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams]] as a clear general policy; the obstacle is standing.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Take Care as the source of discretion (Cox; Heckler; *Texas* (2023)).
- **Off track?** "[F]aithfully" imposes a duty; the Convention unanimously rejected a suspending power; category-wide prospective forbearance is the suspending power renamed.
- **Should position:** individual and resource-driven priorities should be lawful; prospective, categorical non-enforcement grounded in policy disagreement should be treated as suspension. Strongest counter: resource scarcity exists for every statute, so the line is one of degree.

### Application — 2023 Q1 (student-loan non-enforcement)

- **A. Court.** Arguments against the President, ranked:
  1. Cox is a criminal-prosecution case; collecting civil debts owed to the United States is administering federal property, and forgiving the debts disposes of property, which Art.IV-3.2 gives to Congress.
  2. The policy is categorical, prospective and benefit-conferring — Adams's general policy, the Thompson Memo's "special risks," and *Regents*' forbearance/benefits split.
  3. Youngstown category three if the Higher Education Act directs collection (missing fact: what the statute says about collection and compromise), and there is no "conclusive and preclusive" power over loans.
  - Ruling: the court should hold the policy unlawful, assuming standing.
- **C. Successor reversal.** President Scott should be able to resume enforcement: non-enforcement changes no law, discretion belongs to each President, and deferred action is revocable by design. Limits to name: any statute of limitations (missing fact); fair-notice and due-process objections to retroactive interest and penalties for the forbearance period, although estoppel against the government rarely succeeds (*OPM v. Richmond*). The borrowers' strongest argument is against penalties, not principal.

### Fact triggers

- A memo announcing who will not be prosecuted, with no case-by-case exception.
- Non-enforcement plus a benefit (work permits, cancellations, payments).
- A presidential claim that a statute is unconstitutional, in a signing statement or memo.
- A grand jury indicts and the U.S. Attorney refuses to sign.

### Traps

- **"Cox has one holding."** Two 4–3 majorities.
- **"Heckler holds Article II confers absolute non-enforcement power."** APA presumption only.
- **"The Supreme Court has held wholesale non-enforcement unconstitutional."** Adams is a court of appeals funding case.
- **"A signing statement is a line-item veto."** It changes no law; [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] is the cancellation case.
- **"[[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] blesses non-enforcement."** It blocks criminal punishment, not legal review.

### Canned hook

> "The Take Care Clause gives the President discretion over whom to pursue, but 'faithfully' makes it a duty to execute, not a license to suspend. A case-by-case judgment about past conduct is Cox; a categorical, prospective promise not to enforce a statute that directs enforcement is Adams — and this policy is the second."

## 4. War powers

**Clause.** Congress may "declare War, grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal, and make Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water" — Art. I § 8 cl. 11 (Art.I-8.11); armies, navy, rules for the forces, militia — cl. 12–16; purse — Art.I-9.7. "The President shall be Commander in Chief" — Art. II § 2 cl. 1 (Art.II-2.1). Module: [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

**Bottom line.** Congress decides whether the nation goes to war; the President conducts it and may repel attacks without prior authorization. The Convention changed "make" to "declare," "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks" ([[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]), and the President "has no power to initiate or declare a war" ([[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]]).

### The test

1. **Which stage — initiation, conduct, or termination?** Most errors apply the initiation rule to a conduct question.
2. **Has Congress decided?** Declaration, or specific statutory authorization (2001 AUMF). Not appropriations (WPR § 8(a)(1)), not a treaty, not the U.N. ([[Declarations of War]]).
3. **If not, is the President repelling an attack?** On U.S. territory: yes. On forces abroad: by logic, and WPR § 2(c)(3). An imminent attack: open. A punitive strike after the attack ends: no.
4. **If neither, does Article II alone suffice?** OLC: important national interests + "anticipated nature, scope and duration" short of "war" ([[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).
5. **The War Powers Resolution.** § 5(c) concurrent resolution: invalid after [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]]. § 5(b) sixty-day clock: valid unless the President has power to continue hostilities indefinitely without Congress. § 8(c): no new authority. [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] objects to both.
6. **Youngstown placement.** Authorization = 1; silence = 2; prohibition (clock run, funding cutoff) = 3 ([[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]]).
7. **Conduct: has Congress prescribed the method?** The Captures Clause and rules for the forces are congressional; the President's order yields (*Little v. Barreme*). [[The Emancipation Proclamation]] rests on the war power "as a fit and necessary war measure."
8. **Law-of-war limits.** [[Lincoln, Order of Retaliation]] rests on "[t]he law of nations and the usages and customs of war."
9. **Termination.** By treaty, legislation or proclamation (*Ludecke*; [[Terminating War]]); in practice, the funding cutoff ended Vietnam.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]] → "make" became "declare." (Madison's Notes, King) — "make" might be understood to mean "conduct"; (Madison's Notes, Sherman) — "declare" narrowed Congress's power "too much."
- [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] (1863, 5–4, Grier) → leading case. A civil war "becomes such by its accidents"; the President "was bound to meet it in the shape it presented itself, without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name." (Prize Cases, Nelson dissenting) — war is a legal status only Congress can create; captures before July 13, 1861 are void.
- [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] → best counter-case. Distinguishing line: a brief, limited strike with no ground troops is where executive practice is strongest; sustained or escalating force is initiation.
- [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] → Congress should act by "positive action," not "handcuff every future President merely by doing nothing and sitting still."
- [[A Note on Executive Agreements]] → the parallel foreign-affairs question: what the President may do without two-thirds of the Senate.

### Baude in class

- The Commander in Chief Clause had to be written down, which suggests there is no inherent military executive — otherwise there would be no need to name it.
- "Declare" leaves the President an inherent power to "repel attacks," read out of [[Madison's Notes on the War Power|Madison's notes]].
- Prize Cases: Lincoln acted to "repel," within Madison's reasoning — "[s]till, isn't martial law a step above 'repelling' attacks?" A third option (Congress can never declare war on part of the country): "no one takes this view."
- Contrast [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]: Congress's position was unclear (out of session), and the war was at home, not in Korea (Black's "theater of war").
- The Emancipation Proclamation freed only slaves in rebel territory; seizure on land puts it "[f]ully in Youngstown territory."
- WPR: § 5(c) — "[e]veryone agrees" it fails presentment; § 5(b) — "[d]isagreement," and not unconstitutional if the President lacks authority to continue hostilities indefinitely.
- Purse: the executive's response that Congress "can't use the power of the purse as an end run."

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** almost none from the Supreme Court; lower courts split on justiciability (*Dellums v. Bush*; *Ange v. Bush*; *Orlando v. Laird*). Practice is the operative law.
- **Off track?** OLC's "not war" definitional move lets the President's prediction of duration fix the constitutional line. Liquidation needs acquiescence; Congress passed the WPR over a veto, so the practice is contested, not settled.
- **Should position:** practice should be read to settle only a narrow category of brief strikes and rescues; initiating sustained hostilities should require Congress, and § 5(b) should bind. Strongest counter: two centuries of unilateral force (Frankfurter's gloss) and Yoo's formal-declaration reading.

### Fact triggers

- An air campaign or "limited" strike running past sixty days.
- Funding bills cited as authorization.
- A concurrent resolution ordering withdrawal.
- A strike justified as retaliation after an attack has ended.
- Seizure of property or regulation of civilians "as a war measure" at home.

### Oscillation points (ranked)

1. **Strongest for Congress:** text + Convention + a WPR passed over a veto = no acquiescence.
2. **Strongest for the President:** the repel-attacks carve-out extended to forces and citizens abroad, plus long practice of brief strikes.
3. **Weaker for the President:** "declare" means only formal recognition (Yoo).
4. **Weakest for the President:** appropriations as authorization — foreclosed by WPR § 8(a)(1).

### Traps

- **"The Prize Cases approve unilateral presidential war."** Grier says the opposite.
- **"Only a formal declaration authorizes war."** Specific authorization is Congress's decision too.
- **"Continued appropriations authorize the war."** § 8(a)(1).
- **"Congress can end a war by concurrent resolution."** Not after Chadha.
- **"The Commander in Chief's control of conduct is exclusive."** The Captures Clause says otherwise.

### Canned hook

> "The question is not whether this is called a war but which side of Madison's line it sits on: the President may repel, but he may not initiate. Absent an attack to repel or a specific authorization, the President is in Youngstown category two at best, and once the War Powers Resolution's clock runs he is in category three."

## 5. Impeachment

**Clause.** "The President, Vice President and all civil Officers . . . shall be removed from Office on Impeachment for, and Conviction of, Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors" — Art. II § 4 (Art.II-4). House "sole Power of Impeachment," Art.I-2.5; Senate "sole Power to try," two thirds to convict, Art.I-3.6; judgment limited to removal and disqualification, party "nevertheless" liable to indictment, Art.I-3.7. Module: [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].

**Bottom line.** "High Crimes and Misdemeanors" need not be indictable crimes — the phrase is an English term of art for abuse of office or violation of public trust — but it must be more than maladministration or policy disagreement, a line the Chase and Johnson acquittals fixed in practice. Impeachment is a political question, so the real constraint is the Senate's two thirds.

### The test

1. **Covered officer?** President, Vice President, civil officers; judges hold "during good Behaviour."
2. **Which reading of "high Crimes and Misdemeanors"?** Indictable-offense (the 1867 committee dissent; the Impeachment Judgment and Pardon Clauses); political-offense (Hamilton, Federalist 65, "abuse or violation of some public trust"; Story); official-abuse ([[Law Professors' Letter on the Clinton Impeachment]] — "grossly derelict exercise of official power").
3. **More than maladministration?** Madison struck the word because "[s]o vague a term . . . will be equivalent to a tenure during pleasure of the Senate."
4. **Good-faith legal disagreement, or knowing or corrupt use of power?** The first is outside the standard (Brooks and Trumbull in 1868); the second, with bribery as the paradigm, is inside.
5. **A judicial ruling?** Outside, by the Chase settlement ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]).
6. **Procedure:** House majority; Senate two thirds; the Chief Justice presides at a presidential trial.
7. **Could vs. should** — always answer separately (2023 Q1B).

### Cases and who said what

- [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] (1868) → leading case. Impeached 126–47 for firing Stanton under the Tenure of Office Act; acquitted 35–19. (Johnson, Trumbull) — impeaching for "insufficient causes" means "no future President will be safe." Currie: "no one should be impeached because he disagrees with the congressional will."
- [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (1805) → best counter-case. Distinguishing line: rulings and good-faith legal positions are out; misconduct in office (a partisan harangue, drunkenness on the bench, corruption) can be in, crime or not. Article VIII, charging no crime, drew the most guilty votes (19–15).
- [[Law Professors' Letter on the Clinton Impeachment]] (1998) → private perjury is not impeachable; Hyde's reply that perjury by the chief law-enforcement officer "is a public act." Acquitted 55–45 and 50–50.
- *Nixon v. United States* (1993) → Senate trial procedures nonjusticiable. Not [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]].
- [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] → impeachment is not a precondition of prosecution, but remains the principal check on core powers.

### Baude in class

- A term of art from English usage → abuse of office / violation of public trust; not limited to indictable crimes; more than unsatisfactory executive performance.
- Chase shows "mere disagreement of policy/partisanship ≠ High crime/misdemeanor," established ex post by the acquittal; two thirds "is a high bar to overcome."
- Berger's view: repeated bad decisions may be impeachable, but the bar stays high enough to avoid impeachment for every unfavorable decision.
- The norm against impeaching for Supreme Court decisions — "difficult to understand if this is the norm based on practice or reading of Constitution."
- Johnson's removal of the Secretary of War was the most concrete ground — and [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]] now shows that the removal was lawful.
- Ranking of responses to judicial disagreement: impeachment (textual, highest bar) → jurisdiction stripping (narrow, surgical) → court packing (no text). See [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** none judicial; the settlement is Senate practice.
- **Best understanding:** the acquittals liquidate the clause — real interpretive weight, enforceable only by Congress.
- **Should position:** the House should impeach for knowing usurpation of another branch's power or corrupt use of a real power, and should not impeach for good-faith legal positions, however wrong. Strongest counter: the 1867 dissent's indictable-offense reading.

### Application — 2023 Q1B (non-enforcement of all student debt)

- **Could the House impeach and the Senate convict?** Yes. Currie lists "a president's deliberate usurpation of congressional power" as conduct the political reading reaches, and disposing of $1.5 trillion of federal property with no statutory authority is a candidate. The judgment is nonjusticiable, so no court checks it.
- **Should they?** The answer turns on good faith. A President acting on a colorable Take Care theory resembles Johnson (a legal disagreement later vindicated); a President whose own lawyers found no authority resembles knowing usurpation. Missing fact: the advice he received and how he described it. Even if the conduct is impeachable, Congress's better first tools are the purse, legislation forcing collection, and the election.

### Fact triggers

- A President defies a statute he claims is unconstitutional.
- A judge is targeted for rulings.
- Corrupt use of a core power (pardons for money).
- A House subpoena issued "so that it can consider impeaching" (2021 Q2) — the impeachment purpose supplies the legislative purpose.

### Traps

- **"Johnson shows an indictable crime is required."** Article X charged only speeches; the principle was never resolved.
- **"Striking 'maladministration' proves a statutory crime is required."** It proves only a floor above bad government.
- **"A President must be convicted before prosecution."** Rejected in [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]].
- **"The Chase norm is judicially enforceable."** Only the Senate enforces it.

### Canned hook

> "'High Crimes and Misdemeanors' is a term of art for abuse of public trust, not a cross-reference to the criminal code, so the House could impeach here. Whether it should turns on the line Chase and Johnson drew: good-faith disagreement about the law is not impeachable, and knowing usurpation is."

## 6. Presidential immunity and privilege

**Clause.** No text. Inferred from Art. II § 1 cl. 1 (Art.II-1.1) and Art. II § 3 (Art.II-3). Counter-text: the Impeachment Judgment Clause, party "nevertheless" liable (Art.I-3.7); Speech or Debate immunity for members of Congress only (Art.I-6.1); due process and compulsory process (Amends. V, VI). Module: [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]].

**Bottom line.** Presidential communications are "presumptively privileged," but a generalized claim "must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial" ([[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]]). A former President has absolute immunity for acts within his "conclusive and preclusive" authority, at least presumptive immunity for other official acts, and none for unofficial acts ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]).

### The test

1. **Evidence or liability?** A subpoena for someone else's trial runs through Nixon; a prosecution of the President runs through Trump. The burdens run in opposite directions.
2. **Subpoena:** threshold (an intra-branch dispute is justiciable while the regulation stands; Rule 17(c) relevancy, admissibility, specificity) → balance confidentiality against "the fair administration of criminal justice" → protect what survives by in camera review. National-security secrets draw far more deference.
3. **Prosecution — sort the act into a tier.**
   - *Absolute:* investigation and prosecution decisions, removal of executive officers, the pardon.
   - *Presumptive:* official acts touching powers shared with others (pressing the Vice President on certification); the Government must show "no 'dangers of intrusion on the authority and functions of the Executive Branch.'"
   - *None:* unofficial acts.
4. **No forbidden shortcuts.** Courts "may not inquire into the President's motives," and may not call an act unofficial "merely because it allegedly violates a generally applicable law."
5. **Evidentiary bar (Part III-C, five Justices).** Evidence of immune conduct is inadmissible, even to prove intent for unofficial acts. (Trump, Barrett dissenting from III-C) — the Constitution "does not require blinding juries."
6. **What check remains?** Impeachment — available, not a precondition.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] (2024, 6–3, Roberts) → leading case. "The President is not above the law. But . . . the President may not be prosecuted for exercising his core constitutional powers." (Trump, Sotomayor dissenting) — "the President is now a king above the law"; Seal Team Six; a bribe for a pardon. (Trump, Barrett) — frames the issue as an as-applied challenge to the statute. (Trump, Thomas) — questions the Special Counsel's appointment.
- [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]] (1974, 8–0, Burger; Rehnquist not participating) → best counter-case. Distinguishing line: producing evidence for another's trial yields to demonstrated need and the courts decide; prosecuting the President for a core act cannot proceed. Rests on [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]]'s "say what the law is."
- [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] → (Youngstown, Jackson) supplies "conclusive and preclusive," which Trump converts from a category of power into a zone of immunity.
- [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] → the President answers "before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal" — the ancestor of the absolute tier.

### Baude in class

- The map: core (directing the AG) is absolutely immune; talking with the VP is presumptively immune; the "[r]est of acts are not clarified." Hypos will sit in the unclarified zone.
- The question pressed: "Just because POTUS has power to direct cabinet members, does that mean he can use the power however he wants?"
- The Court's two defenses, each with Baude's counter: (1) immunity runs only to the President — "Contra. President can offer anyone a pardon"; (2) it stops a weaponized DOJ against predecessors — "Contra. Didn't this decision stop Biden from [prosecuting] Trump?"
- The dissent's Seal Team Six hypothetical — "No response from majority." Why are Article II powers greater than other constitutional rights?
- Nixon: how could the United States sue Nixon? The special prosecutor's authority ran through the AG; Nixon's firing attempt; was the Court a better resolver than impeachment, or only "a step zero" with Congress still playing a dormant role?
- Approach: argue from the purpose-versus-power gap and the unanswered hypothetical, not from the conceded core.
- Office hours: Roberts's "energy" runs to the current office and is forward-looking — the worry about successive prosecutions protects the sitting President's decisions, which answers the objection that the decision arms the President to weaponize the Attorney General.

### Removal feedback loop

- Removal of executive officers is a paradigm absolute-tier act.
- [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] already treats removal as core, conclusive and preclusive power. [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] enlarges the set of officers that core power reaches — now including FTC, NLRB or SEC heads — subject to the exceptions: the Fed ([[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]]), inferior officers with limited duties ([[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]]), and the reserved non-Article III and purely adjudicatory bodies (*Wiener*).
- The Nixon-era regulation protecting a special prosecutor is now doubly fragile: revocable by the AG, and any firing under it falls within the core.
- See [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Trump's three tiers and evidence rule.
- **Off track?** The Constitution gives members of Congress Speech or Debate immunity and gives the President nothing; the Impeachment Judgment Clause presupposes liability ("nevertheless"); bribery "implicates official acts almost by definition" (Trump dissent).
- **Should position:** the absolute tier should protect the President's power to act (removal, pardon) and not the purpose behind the act, so that selling a pardon or firing to obstruct is prosecutable under statutes that clearly reach the President; the evidentiary bar should go, as Barrett argues. Strongest counter: chilling "bold and unhesitating action" ([[The Federalist No. 70]]) and *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*.

### Fact triggers

- Prosecution of a former President for conversations with DOJ, firings or pardons.
- Grand-jury or trial subpoenas to the White House.
- Congressional subpoenas (reserved in Nixon; *Mazars* governs).
- Mixed acts: public statements, dealings with state officials, the Vice President.

### Oscillation points (ranked)

1. **Strongest for the prosecution:** text (no immunity clause; "nevertheless" liable) plus bribery.
2. **Strongest for the President:** structure and energy, and the risk of successor retaliation.
3. **Hardest classification line:** pressing the Vice President (presumptive) vs. pressing state officials on electors (Barrett: unofficial).

### Traps

- **"Nixon holds the President can be prosecuted."** It was a subpoena for others' trial.
- **"Trump gives absolute immunity for all official acts."** Only for conclusive and preclusive powers.
- **"The act was unofficial because it was illegal or corrupt."** Both shortcuts are foreclosed.
- **"Nixon and Trump apply the same presumption."** Opposite burdens.

### Canned hook

> "Trump v. United States immunizes the power, not the purpose: firing the Attorney General is conclusive and preclusive, but nothing in Article II makes the price of a pardon or the motive for a firing part of the power. A bribery statute that punishes the sale, not the exercise, intrudes on no executive function — which is the question the Seal Team Six hypothetical left the majority unable to answer."

# Part IV — The Judicial Power and Its Limits

## 1. Judicial review

**Clause** — "The judicial Power of the United States" (Art. III § 1 → Art.III-1); "all Cases . . . arising under this Constitution" (Art. III § 2 cl. 1 → Art.III-2.1); original jurisdiction limited to cases "affecting Ambassadors, other public Ministers and Consuls, and those in which a State shall be Party" (Art. III § 2 cl. 2 → Art.III-2.2); laws "made in Pursuance thereof" (Art. VI cl. 2 → Art.VI-2); the oath (Art.VI-3).

**Bottom line** — A federal court deciding a case must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute and refuse to give the statute effect ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]). The power is an incident of deciding a case, "little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]). The live contest is reach, not existence: whether the Court's exposition binds anyone beyond the parties (§ 2).

**The test**

1. **Is there a case — adverse parties, something turning on the decision?** No advisory opinions ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]); rights "must be contested" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).
2. **Is the party directly injured?** "A general interest common to all members of the public" fails ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]); see § 3.
3. **Does the court have jurisdiction from a valid source?** Congress cannot add to original jurisdiction, because "[a]ffirmative words are often, in their operation, negative of other objects than those affirmed" ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]).
4. **Is there a genuine conflict, or does a saving reading avoid it?** Section 13's mandamus language can be read to supply a remedy only where jurisdiction already exists, which dissolves the conflict.
5. **Is the remedy confined to the parties?** The statute is "censured" but "not abolished" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]); an injunction runs against "the acts of the official, the statute notwithstanding."

**Cases and who said what**

- [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] (1803, unanimous, Marshall) → § 13 cannot add to original jurisdiction; courts must decline to apply an unconstitutional statute. Marbury wins on right (commission complete at signing) and remedy, then loses on forum.
  - "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
  - For the departmentalist: "courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument."
- [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] (1803, Paterson) → one week later, sustains the Repeal Act on "practice and acquiescence" and never mentions judicial tenure. Read with Marbury, the Court asserts review in principle and avoids every order the political branches could resist.
- [[The Federalist No. 78]] (Hamilton) → review is ordinary choice of law; the judiciary is the "least dangerous" branch; review presupposes "that the power of the people is superior to both."
- [[Brutus No. 11]] → judges "will not confine themselves to any fixed or established rules"; "there is no power provided in the constitution, that can correct their errors."
- [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]] (1796) → review on the merits before Marbury, unquestioned ([[A Map of Article III]]).
- [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]] → quotes "province and duty" to hold that the courts, not the President, decide an executive privilege claim.
- **Best counter-case:** [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]] (7–2, Taney; Curtis dissenting). Distinguishing line: Marbury refuses power in a case it could not otherwise decide; Dred Scott reaches past a finding of no jurisdiction to void another branch's settlement of a national question. (Dred Scott, Curtis): nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" a dismissal.

**Baude in class** — Marbury is a strategic opinion, not a syllogism. Marshall did not want to find out whether Jefferson would obey, so he put the power to read the Constitution in dicta, told Jefferson he was wrong, and denied relief on original jurisdiction so nothing had to be enforced (session 11). Work the holding in order: commission complete on signing → Judiciary Act read broadly → conflict with Art. III § 2 cl. 2 → supremacy. Delivery of the commission does not matter. Press "in Pursuance thereof" as the strongest textual argument against the holding: if it means "in pursuit of" rather than "in accordance with," the conflict dissolves. Dred Scott is the first real test of Marbury, and he leaves the dilemma open: the Court that can act heroically is the Court that can strike the Missouri Compromise (session 12).

- Office hours: the Marbury–Dred Scott arrow runs in opposite directions. In Marbury the want of jurisdiction was the holding (unconstitutional statute → no jurisdiction), and the rest was dicta; in [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] there was no jurisdiction to begin with, and the Court reached past that to strike a statute anyway.
- Office hours: severability is open. The test asks what Congress would have wanted (the legislative-veto cases kept the statute), which is the question textualism says cannot be answered; Congress can write a package-deal clause; Marbury quietly kept the rest of the Judiciary Act.

**Doctrine vs. best understanding**

- **The Court:** review is settled and, after [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]], expository supremacy is claimed.
- **Best understanding:** review is well grounded in the duty to decide cases under a written, supreme law, and in the oath; it is weakest as a claim to bind non-parties. Position to take: judicial review should be defended as a duty to decide the case, which is all Marbury needed, and no more.
- **Strongest counter:** the oath binds every officer, so the oath argument proves departmentalism, not judicial review in particular.

**Fact triggers** — This is a judicial review problem when Congress reroutes the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction or an executive official asks a court for advice.

**Oscillation points** — Did Marshall have to reach the constitutional question? Ranked: (1) no — the narrow reading of § 13 disposes of the case on the statute; (2) yes, if § 13 plainly granted mandamus as an original remedy; (3) prudentially, reaching it was the price of announcing review without an order to defy.

**Traps**

- **"Marbury established judicial supremacy."** The sentence "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" is Cooper's (1958).
- **"The Court annulled the statute."** It disregarded the statute in the case; the vocabulary matters for who else is bound.
- **"The Supremacy Clause is the best text for review of federal statutes."** The Clause names state judges; against a federal statute the work is done by "in Pursuance thereof."

**Canned hook** — "A court that finds a statute unconstitutional does not repeal it; it declines to apply it in the case before it (Marbury; Mellon). Whether anyone else must follow that reasoning is a separate question."

## 2. Judicial supremacy and departmentalism

**Clause** — "bound by Oath or Affirmation, to support this Constitution" (Art. VI cl. 3 → Art.VI-3); "the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby" (Art.VI-2); the President's oath (Art.II-1.8); "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" (Art.II-3); the veto (Art.I-7.2).

**Bottom line** — Judicial review is not judicial supremacy: a court's duty to prefer the Constitution in a case does not by itself bind non-parties to its reasoning. There are three positions, not two — opinion supremacy ([[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]]; Douglas), judgment supremacy (Lincoln in 1858; Baude), and executive coordinacy reaching judgments (Paulsen's "Merryman power"). The President should act on his own constitutional reading where no judgment binds him, and should execute judgments of a court with jurisdiction.

**The test**

1. **Who is acting, and in what capacity?** Departmentalism is strongest for powers given to the officer alone: vetoing, voting, pardoning, declining to prosecute. Jackson claims independence only "when acting in their legislative capacities" ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).
2. **Is obedience demanded to the judgment or to the opinion?** Decisions "absolutely determine the case decided" and "indicate to the public how other similar cases will be decided" ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]).
3. **Does the judgment bind the parties?** On every view but Paulsen's, yes. Lincoln (1858): "We do not propose that when Dred Scott has been decided to be a slave by the court, we, as a mob, will decide him to be free."
4. **Does the opinion bind non-parties?** Cooper says the interpretation "is the supreme law of the land." Lincoln (1861): if policy is "irrevocably fixed by decisions of the Supreme Court . . . the people will have ceased to be their own rulers" ([[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]]).
5. **Did the court have jurisdiction?** Test this before calling non-compliance defiance.
6. **Is the question settled?** Madison's cumulative conditions ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]); Lincoln's criteria: unanimity, no partisan bias, accord with legal expectation and departmental practice, true historical premises, or reaffirmation "through a course of years."
7. **Is the axis nation against State, or branch against branch?** Article VI makes a hierarchy only between federal and state law.

**Baude's own research — two articles to deploy**

- ***The Judgment Power*, 96 Geo. L.J. 1807 (2008).** The President is bound by Article III judgments and must enforce them even when he disagrees, except where the issuing court lacked jurisdiction. The foil is Paulsen (the casebook co-author), whose "Merryman power" lets the President refuse to execute an erroneous judgment. Baude reads [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]] as jurisdictional: habeas "is in the nature of an appeal," and no judge "at chambers, can entertain an appeal . . . from a decision of the President." Paulsen's reply: jurisdiction "gives away the game," because whoever decides jurisdiction decides everything.
- **Opinion vs. judgment, run as a precedent theory.** Judgment supremacy is res judicata for the parties; opinion supremacy is stare decisis imposed on coordinate branches. Baude's class statement: if the Court has jurisdiction, its judgment binds those parties, and everything else, including whether other courts must follow the opinion, is not binding (session 14). Liquidation is the departmentalist's substitute for stare decisis: practice across the branches settles meaning, and a judgment settles the case (Part I § 2).
- ***Signing Unconstitutional Laws*, 86 Ind. L.J. 303 (2011).** No categorical duty to veto a bill containing an unconstitutional provision. The President may sign if his other duties justify enacting the rest and he stands ready to use his other powers not to enforce the unconstitutional part; nothing unconstitutional happens until enforcement. A signing statement records the objection. Compare [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]: comply and seek repeal on §§ 1112–1113; "construe . . . not to abrogate" on § 2262, which is nonenforcement under another name.

**Cases and who said what**

- [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] (1958, signed by all nine) → the leading case: "No state legislator or executive or judicial officer can war against the Constitution without violating his undertaking to support it." The supremacy passage is dictum by its own account: "we should answer the premise."
- [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] (1832) → best counter: the officer supports the Constitution "as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others"; "[t]he opinion of the judges has no more authority over Congress than the opinion of Congress has over the judges." Distinguishing line: Arkansas resisted a decree binding parties; Jackson declined to make a new law.
- [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] → (Lincoln) judgment binds, opinion does not bind "the voter," "Congress or the President"; (Douglas) a citizen is bound to a decision contrary to his opinions as much as to one he likes.
- [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] → Taney's writ and attachment were resisted; his final order was only to file the record and send it to the President, conceding his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome." No ruling on whether the President must obey.
- [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]] → the departments "are co-ordinate and coequal," and the framers "did not even try . . . to create an arbiter among them"; the President answers only "before the high court of impeachment."
- [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]] → the occasion. Congress banned slavery in the territories in 1862 against it; no court passed on that statute.

**Baude in class** — The oath is the engine on both sides: it is Marbury's reason for review and the reason a President with no ruling against him may decline to enforce a statute he thinks unconstitutional. His sequence (session 13):

1. No ruling yet → the executive may act on its own reading.
2. A ruling exists → judicial review binds.
3. Both political branches think a statute constitutional → it can still fall ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|the Line Item Veto Act]]).

- Most departmentalists obey the judgment and reject the opinion as law; the hardcore version (Paulsen) would disobey the judgment too, and the practical check is that other executive officers may refuse the President.
- Departmentalism does not travel beyond the three federal branches: Brown is a Supremacy Clause case, which is why Cooper answers Arkansas with flat judicial supremacy.
- A general policy of not enforcing a statute is unconstitutional, but the President may distinguish a statute he believes unconstitutional (session 14).
- Office hours: judicial supremacy wins in practice because lower courts can enjoin executive action even when the President rejects a Supreme Court opinion.
- Office hours: the President takes an extra oath (Art. II § 1 cl. 8) on top of the Article VI oath.

**Doctrine vs. best understanding**

- **The Court:** opinion supremacy (Cooper), at least against States.
- **Best understanding:** judgment supremacy among the federal branches, opinion supremacy only through the Supremacy Clause over state officers. Position to take: the President should execute every judgment of a court with jurisdiction, and should be free to act on his own reading in signing, vetoing, pardoning and prosecuting.
- **Strongest counter:** Douglas's consistency point and the chaos objection — every State relitigates every right. Answer: liquidation supplies settlement without opinion supremacy, and a position on the Court's authority must "last you through good decisions and bad ones."

**Fact triggers** — This is a departmentalism problem when the President must decide whether to sign, veto, enforce or defend a statute; when a new administration repudiates a predecessor's legal position (2023 Q1(C)); when a prompt says "proceeding as a departmentalist"; or when a state officer resists a federal decree.

**Oscillation points** — May the President decline to enforce a statute the Court has upheld? Ranked: (1) as to the parties to the judgment, no; (2) as to future prosecutions he controls, the departmentalist says yes, because prosecution is his own power; (3) the cost is that a policy of nonenforcement runs into the Take Care Clause (see [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]]).

**Traps**

- **"Lincoln was Faubus."** Lincoln disclaimed resisting the judgment; Faubus used troops against a decree.
- **"Jackson's veto licenses disobeying a court."** It claims independence only in legislative capacity.
- **"Taney ordered Merryman released and Lincoln refused."** Only the writ and attachment were resisted.

**Canned hook** — "The President is bound by the Court's judgment, not its opinion (Baude, The Judgment Power): he must execute the decree, but in signing, vetoing and prosecuting he should act on his own oath (Jackson, Bank Veto)."

## 3. Cases and controversies: standing and justiciability

**Clause** — "to all Cases . . . to Controversies" (Art. III § 2 cl. 1 → Art.III-2.1); the Opinion Clause, opinions "in writing, of the principal Officer in each of the executive Departments" (Art.II-2.1).

**Bottom line** — A federal court may decide a constitutional question only while resolving an actual dispute brought by a plaintiff with a concrete injury, fairly traceable to the defendant and redressable by a favorable ruling ([[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]]). Standing is jurisdictional: no standing, no merits. Where the honest answer is that nobody can sue, say so and say the constraint is political.

**The threshold gate — run first on any fact pattern**

1. **Who is the would-be plaintiff?**
   - Private person regulated, taxed, prosecuted or sued under the challenged power → direct injury; standing is easy ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]).
   - Citizen or federal taxpayer complaining of an expenditure → no standing ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Frothingham]]; [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]).
   - Taxpayer challenging a tax he pays → standing, the orthodox exception Baude noted.
   - State → needs its own theory: proprietary, quasi-sovereign rights "actually invaded or threatened," or a command to act; never the "naked contention" that Congress usurped reserved powers, and never parens patriae against the United States.
   - Chamber of Congress → institutional injury from spending "without a valid appropriation" ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]], a district-court ruling only).
2. **Injury, traceability, redressability** — run all three.
3. **Timing** — ripe (hardship and fitness) and not moot.
4. **Political question** — a separate bar even with standing ([[Luther v. Borden]]; see [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]]).

**Applied (2018 Q1, "could our state or our citizens challenge the law?")** — California has standing to challenge the spending condition, because it is threatened with losing existing grants and services it receives (the [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] posture, not [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Mellon]]'s option it could decline). Its citizens have standing to challenge the wildfire tax they must pay. Name the missing fact: whether the Secretary's determination has yet issued, since the condition may not be ripe until a major wildfire occurs.

**Cases and who said what**

- [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] (1923, Sutherland) → leading case; neither State nor taxpayer may sue merely because Congress exceeded its powers. The Maternity Act "imposes no obligation but simply extends an option"; a taxpayer's interest is "shared with millions of others."
- [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] (1937, per curiam) → a citizen and member of the bar cannot challenge Justice Black's appointment under the Ineligibility Clause (Art. I § 6 cl. 2). The Court decided nothing about the merits.
- [[The Correspondence of the Justices]] (1793) → refused twenty-nine questions on three grounds: separation of powers; judges "of a court in the last resort"; the Opinion Clause "purposely as well as expressly united to the executive departments."
- **Best counter-case:** [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]] (Collyer, J.). Distinguishing line: injury to a constitutional role belonging to the plaintiff alone is particularized; an interest shared with every taxpayer is not.

**Baude in class** — Standing is jurisdictional, so no standing means no ruling on the merits, and the order is particular injury, then relief. Levitt failed because nobody could show the appointment operated on him; [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]] shows the fix, a pending case in which the challenged power is exercised over you. Mellon closes the shortcuts: no taxpayer standing, and a State suing the federal government needs a specific theory. The consequence he accepts: unconstitutional spending may be unreviewable unless Congress or one House sues the executive, even though Congress passed the bill. Advisory opinions are barred because the Court would become an ally of the branch it later reviews (session 14).

- EXAM SIGNAL: A Baude exemplar opens a question in under twenty words — "Assuming all the plaintiffs have suffered an injury in fact, they have standing to sue (Levitt)." Dispose of standing first, briefly, then move to the merits.
- Office hours: where standing rests on a complicated chain of causation, the Court is more skeptical if the plaintiff is not the party directly regulated (*Diamond Alternative Energy*; *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*); a weak traceability theory can be recast as no particularized injury.
- Office hours: on an illegal tax, everyone from whom it is collected has standing.
- Office hours: an unlawful war gives no citizen standing, even though widely shared monetary harm can still be injury in fact.

**Doctrine vs. best understanding** — The three-part test appears nowhere in the text. The originalist question is whether "Cases" and "Controversies," read with the 1793 letter, require an injured plaintiff or only a real, adverse dispute; qui tam and relator suits at the founding are the evidence for the looser reading. Position to take: the case requirement is solid; the modern injury elaborations are contestable, but the result — that some clauses are left to the political branches and their oaths — is the system working, not failing.

**Fact triggers** — This is a standing problem when the prompt asks "could X challenge," names a State or a taxpayer as plaintiff, involves spending or an appointment that operates on no one in particular, or says "imagine nobody has standing" (2023 Q1(B)), which is an instruction to argue the political remedies.

**Oscillation points** — May a House of Congress sue the executive over spending? Ranked: (1) yes for spending without any appropriation, an institutional injury (Burwell; *Mnuchin* in the D.C. Circuit); (2) no for misreading a statute, because "Article I is not a talisman"; (3) never decided by the Supreme Court.

**Traps**

- **"Levitt held Black's appointment lawful."** It held only that the petitioner could not ask.
- **"Standing and political question are the same bar."** They are separate.
- **"A State has standing whenever it pleads the Tenth Amendment."** Only if commanded to act or deprived of something it has.

**Canned hook** — "The State has standing not because it objects to the scope of federal power, which Mellon forbids, but because the condition threatens funds it already receives."

## 4. Congress versus the courts: jurisdiction stripping, court packing, impeaching judges

**Clause** — "such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish" (Art. III § 1 → Art.III-1); appellate jurisdiction "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make" (Art.III-2.2); "during good Behaviour" (Art.III-1); impeachment for "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors" (Art.II-4), conviction by two-thirds (Art.I-3.6).

**Bottom line** — Article III fixes little and leaves the rest to Congress: whether inferior courts exist, what they hear, how many Justices sit, and which cases reach the Supreme Court on appeal. Congress may make exceptions to appellate jurisdiction, even reaching an argued case, and courts will not inquire into its motives ([[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]]). The open question is the statute McCardle did not face: one closing every federal route to a constitutional claim.

**The test**

1. **Does the statute create, abolish or reorganize inferior courts, or move causes among them?** Valid ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]; the Madisonian Compromise, [[A Map of Article III]]).
2. **Does it except a class of cases from appellate jurisdiction?** Valid; then ask whether it reaches pending cases and whether another route survives. McCardle closed the 1867 habeas route and said the 1789 route remained.
3. **Does it add to or subtract from original jurisdiction?** A Marbury problem, not a McCardle one.
4. **Does it change the Court's size?** Within the power; the objection must be to purpose ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]).
5. **Is Congress removing an individual judge?** Only by impeachment ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]).
6. **Does it cross an outer limit?** In order: does it dictate the outcome of a pending cause rather than remove a class (*United States v. Klein*)? Does it leave any forum, federal or state? Does it reach "transactions past and closed"? Does it leave a detainee without a forum ([[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]])?

**Cases and who said what**

- [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] (1869, 8–0, Chase) → leading case: "It is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception"; "[w]e are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature"; "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law." The repeal passed over Johnson's veto during his impeachment trial ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]).
- [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] (1803, Paterson) → Congress may "transfer a cause from one such tribunal to another"; circuit riding upheld on practice alone; silent on the displaced judges' tenure.
- [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] (1937) → best counter: a Democratic committee voted 10–8 against a Democratic President's bill as "a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle." Distinguishing line: where Congress uses an undoubted power to change the Court's answers rather than its workload, no court will stop it, so the objection must be made and enforced in Congress. "If we yield to temptation now to lay the lash upon the Court, we are only teaching others how to apply it to ourselves."
- [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (1804–05) → majorities voted guilty on three of eight articles, none reached two-thirds; the highest vote (19–15) was on the political grand-jury charge, not on any ruling. A precedent of restraint, not a holding that only indictable crimes are impeachable.
- [[Martin v. Hunter's Lessee]] → Supreme Court review of state courts, the reason stripping lower-court jurisdiction still leaves a federal last word.

**Baude in class** — He marked this as an exam question in terms (session 15).

- EXAM SIGNAL: "What are Congressional limits to jurisdiction stripping?"
- His answer starts from text: the Exceptions power is real, and exceptions are not a total ban (the core theory).
- Timing limit: Congress stripped jurisdiction after oral argument in McCardle; he thinks it cannot strip after the Court rules, though the Constitution does not say so. Where the line falls between argument and judgment is left to you — argue from what the limit protects.
- Lower-court jurisdiction is freely strippable because Congress creates those courts under § 1; the Supreme Court is the only hard case.
- Prudential reading available: the Court may have acquiesced, as in Marbury, to preserve its authority for later.
- Ranking of responses to judicial disagreement: (1) impeachment is the most constitutionally sound, because it has text, and the hardest, because it needs two-thirds; (2) jurisdiction stripping is narrower and allows surgical strikes; (3) court packing has no text. Stripping and packing need only simple majorities.
- The norm against impeaching Justices for wrong decisions is hard to classify: practice or a reading of the Constitution.
- Office hours: Roosevelt's plan needed a statute, and it failed because the Senate majority was not on board ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]).

**Doctrine vs. best understanding**

- **The Court:** Exceptions Clause read on its face; motive unreviewable; Klein forbids dictating outcomes.
- **Best understanding:** one original-meaning reading takes "Exceptions . . . and . . . Regulations" to concern fact review and housekeeping, not removal of constitutional questions; Federalist 78 and Brutus both assumed the Court would review laws. Position to take: Congress should be held to the "not both" position — it may limit the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction or the lower courts' existence, but not both, so some court keeps the last word on federal constitutional claims. Strongest counter: the text says "Exceptions" without limit, and state courts can be the last word (the "menu" view).

**Fact triggers** — This is a court-control problem when Congress responds to a decision by withdrawing jurisdiction, adding seats, restructuring circuits, impeaching or investigating Justices (2021 Q2 — court packing and impeachment subpoenas), or repealing jurisdiction mid-case.

**Oscillation points** — Is court packing unconstitutional? Ranked: (1) not legally — the size is statutory and has ranged from six to ten; (2) arguably, if a Court enlarged to change its answers is not "independent" in the sense Article III tenure and salary presuppose; (3) the Senate Report's own remedy, a constitutional amendment, concedes that ordinary legislation could otherwise do it.

**Traps**

- **"McCardle holds Congress may cut off all Supreme Court review."** It repealed one statutory route and said another survived.
- **"Stuart v. Laird upheld abolishing the 1801 judgeships."** It never mentions tenure.
- **"Nine Justices is constitutionally fixed."** It is statutory.
- **"The Exceptions Clause reaches original jurisdiction."** It attaches only to appellate jurisdiction.

**Canned hook** — "Congress may make exceptions to the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction (McCardle), but an exception is not an abolition: so long as the 1789 route or a state court remains open, the statute regulates the Court's docket rather than the Constitution's meaning."

# Part V — Federalism I: The Union, the Bank, Commerce, and State Sovereignty

## 1. The nature of the Union: compact, nation, secession

**Clause** — Preamble, "We the People of the United States . . . in Order to form a more perfect Union"; admission of new States, Art. IV § 3 cl. 1 (Art.IV-3.1); the Guarantee Clause, Art. IV § 4 (Art.IV-4); Supremacy, Art. VI cl. 2 (Art.VI-2). There is no exit clause — point to its absence.

**Bottom line** — No State may leave the Union by its own act, because the Constitution was ordained by the people, not entered by sovereign States as a revocable compact; the ordinance and the acts giving it effect are "absolutely null" ([[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]]). The only lawful exits are "revolution, or . . . consent of the States." The text cannot settle the question on its own, because "[u]nderstanding the nature of the Union was a predicate of—not a product of—constitutional interpretation" ([[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell]]).

### The test

1. **Who is sovereign — which of Campbell's four views?** Jefferson (compact: only the peoples of the States are sovereign); Madison (quasi-national: "a mixture of both," made by the people of each State); Marshall (1787 nationalism: the people of the States "forged themselves together into a unitary whole"); Wilson (1776 nationalism: the nation preceded the States) ([[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell]]).
2. **What interpretive rule follows?** Compact theory imports the law-of-nations rule that sovereign rights are alienated only "in clear and express terms" — strict construction in [[The Bank Debate]]; nationalist premises give the States no presumption, which is how [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] reaches "Let the end be legitimate."
3. **Does perpetuity apply?** Articles "perpetual" → Constitution "more perfect Union" → "[w]hat can be indissoluble if a perpetual Union, made more perfect, is not?" ([[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]]; [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln]]; [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]]).
4. **Was admission a revocable compact?** No: admission "was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final" (Chase).
5. **Is federal force coercion of a State or execution of law against individuals?** Lincoln: void ordinances leave only individuals resisting federal law, reached by the militia power "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections" (Art.I-8.15); see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
6. **Who restores a defeated State?** Congress, under the Guarantee Clause — "primarily a legislative power" — so executive governments are "provisional" ([[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]]).

### Cases and who said what

- [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] (1869, 5–3, Chase) → secession void; Texas "continued to be a State"; "an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States." Posture drove it: whether Texas could sue in original jurisdiction. (Texas v. White, Grier dissenting): statehood is "a political fact, not . . . a legal fiction," and Texas "now sets up the plea of insanity." (Texas v. White, Swayne): joins Grier on jurisdiction only.
- [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln, First Inaugural]] → perpetuity, anarchy ("the central idea of secession, is the essence of anarchy"), and contract — "does it not require all to lawfully rescind it?" The contract argument concedes the compact premise and still wins.
- [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] → secession unlawful *and* coercion of a State unlawful; "Secession is neither more nor less than revolution."
- [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]] (best counter) → secession is "an essential attribute of State sovereignty"; "each State is a sovereign, and thus may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever." No clause cited.
- **Distinguishing line** — A claim resting on each State's power to revoke for any reason fails under Texas v. White and Lincoln's contract argument; a claim resting on the other States' material breach of an express right survives those answers and meets only perpetuity.

### Nullification vs. secession

- **Nullification:** one federal *statute* exceeds the delegated powers, so the State treats it as void inside its borders. The compact is intact; the agent overstepped; the State stays in and appeals to the other States.
- **Secession:** the *compact itself* has been materially breached, so the State is discharged and leaves.
- **Davis's use of the distinction:** "Nullification and secession, so often confounded, are indeed antagonistic principles" — nullification is a remedy "within the Union, and against the agent of the States," so Jackson's Force Bill is no precedent for coercing a seceded State.
- **The better answer to Davis:** both doctrines rest on each State's authority to judge the compact for itself, a premise with no stopping point.

Marshall's is the usable formulation — States plus people, the federal government "supreme . . . within its sphere" — because it builds the limit into the supremacy claim.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] — void, and Congress restores.
- **Off track?** Chase reasons from "the nature of the Union," not text, and decides as a jurisdictional predicate what arms had already decided. Grier's political-question objection ([[Luther v. Borden]]) is the strongest doctrinal counter.
- **Should:** Ashwin should defend the result on the contract argument plus the Marshall/Madison account of ratification, and concede that perpetuity alone proves too much (it would forbid dissolution even with every State's consent).

### Fact triggers

- A State legislature declares a federal statute void within the State → nullification; answer with Supremacy (Art.VI-2), [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s "action of a part on the whole," and Marshall's ratification account.
- A referendum or ordinance to leave → secession; consent-of-the-States route and the open *Quebec Reference* negotiation question.
- Federal troops or marshals enforcing law in a resisting State → coercion of a State vs. execution against individuals.

### Oscillation points (ranked)

1. **Strongest for the Union:** the contract argument — even a compact needs all parties to rescind.
2. **Next:** ratification by the people in conventions, not by state governments ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]).
3. **Weakest:** bare perpetuity.
4. **Strongest for the States:** material breach of an express right by the other States — Lincoln's arguments do not answer it cleanly.

### Traps

- **"Texas v. White is purely nationalist."** The "indestructible States" half underwrites [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]].
- **"'Absolutely null' voids every act of the rebel government."** Only acts in furtherance of the rebellion (*Horn v. Lockhart*).
- **"Lincoln answered the coercion question."** He denied it arose — which works only because the seceding States seized federal property.

### Canned hook

> "Whether Congress's power here is broad or narrow depends on a prior question the text does not answer: who made the Union. On Marshall's account — the people, not the States as parties to a compact — the federal government is 'supreme within its sphere,' and that premise, not any clause, drives the construction that follows."

## 2. Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause

**Clause** — "To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution," Art. I § 8 cl. 18 (Art.I-8.18). Contrast "absolutely necessary," Art. I § 10 cl. 2 (Art.I-10.2). Tenth Amendment: "not delegated" — no "expressly."

**Bottom line** — Congress may choose any reasonable means to execute an enumerated power, but a means must serve that power rather than become a new power of its own. "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]). Courts defer on "necessary" and police only pretext and "proper" ([[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]).

### The test — a deployable five-gate N&P screen

1. **Legitimate end within the scope of the Constitution?** The end must itself be an enumerated power — Hamilton's own example of an invalid end is a federal corporation to police Philadelphia ([[The Bank Debate]]).
2. **Means or end?** Incorporation "is never the end for which other powers are exercised, but a means" (Marshall), against Madison's "a distinct, an independent and substantive prerogative."
3. **Plainly adapted?** Rational relation is enough; Congress judges degree of necessity — inquiry into it would "tread on legislative ground." The chain may run several links: crime → prison → civil commitment ([[United States v. Comstock]], 2010, 7–2, Breyer).
4. **Prohibited?** Art. I § 9 and the Bill of Rights defeat the means regardless of fit.
5. **"Proper" — consistent with letter and spirit?** Three independent screens:
   - Not a "great substantive and independent power" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]], Roberts).
   - Not a breach of state sovereignty — commandeering is not "proper" ([[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]], Scalia).
   - Not a law that lets Congress "create the necessary predicate" for its own power ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]]).
6. **Pretext check (reserved, dormant):** a law passed "under the pretext of executing its powers" for "objects not entrusted to the government" is void (Marshall) — dormant since [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]], alive in the taxing line ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]]).

**The Comstock five considerations** (when the chain is long): (1) breadth of the Clause; (2) long history of federal involvement; (3) sound reasons for the statute given the government's custodial interest; (4) accommodation of state interests; (5) the link to an enumerated power is not too attenuated or sweeping. (Comstock, Alito): not "carte blanche." (Comstock, Thomas, dissenting): the end must itself be enumerated, and the five factors are a "novel" test.

**Answer to "are there bounds beyond the other enumerated powers?"** Yes, three: the end must trace to an enumerated power (gate 1); "proper" carries independent force (gate 5); pretext remains formally reserved (gate 6). The Tenth Amendment adds nothing — "a truism" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]]).

### Cases and who said what

- [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] (1819, unanimous, Marshall) → Congress may charter the Bank; Maryland may not tax it, because "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" — a State taxing the Bank is "the action of a part on the whole." Leaves room for nondiscriminatory taxes on real property.
- [[The Bank Debate]] (1791) → (Bank, Hamilton): necessary = "needful, requisite, incidental, useful, or conducive to"; (Bank, Jefferson): "indispensable," without which the grant would be "nugatory"; (Bank, Madison): necessary = incidental, not merely important, and a charter is a substantive power; the chain-of-implications objection.
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] (best counter) → mandate perhaps "necessary" but not "proper." (NFIB, Roberts) plus the joint dissent make five, with no opinion of the Court. (NFIB, Ginsburg): "proper" means only no commandeering of States; Roberts's test is "You will know it when you see it."
- **Distinguishing line** — Comstock and Raich operated on persons or activity already within federal power; the mandate drew people into regulation to create its own predicate.
- [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] → departmentalist: degree of necessity is legislative, so the President, acting legislatively on a veto, judges it himself. [[The Death of the Second Bank]]: the Bank won in court in 1819 and died politically in 1832–36.

### "A constitution we are expounding" — the answer to Ashwin's note

- **Source:** Marshall in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]: "we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding." A constitution cannot "partake of the prolixity of a legal code"; its nature "requires, that only its great outlines should be marked, its important objects designated."
- **Use 1 — against *expressio unius*:** the omission of "bank" or "corporation" from the enumeration proves nothing, because a document of great outlines does not list means. Pair with the Art. I § 10 "absolutely necessary" contrast.
- **Use 2 — enlarging incidental powers:** oaths of office, the federal criminal code and punishment of mail robbery all rest on unenumerated means; a code-reading would forbid them.
- **Limit:** the phrase is an argument about the kind of document, paired with "intended to endure for ages to come," not a licence to update meaning.

### Baude's scholarship — the "great powers" theory

- **Baude, *Rethinking the Federal Eminent Domain Power*, 122 Yale L.J. 1738 (2013):** some powers are too important to be implied through the Clause even when they would help carry out an enumerated power — they must be enumerated. Source: McCulloch's "great substantive and independent power, which cannot be implied as incidental to other powers."
- **NFIB connection:** Roberts's mandate holding is the great-powers theory in operation, and the casebook cites the article for that reading. Baude himself calls Roberts's application questionable and, per the article's summary, suggests commandeering, sovereign immunity, conscription and freedom of the press as better applications.
- **Exam use:** run the great-powers test explicitly, credit the source, then argue whether the power at issue looks more like eminent domain (great) or a bank charter (incidental).

### Baude in class

- EXAM SIGNAL: "Enumerate each person's view and how Marshall responded." The responses: necessary ≠ indispensable (where there are two ways, neither is indispensable yet both are necessary — answering Jefferson); the Bank is incidental (concedes Madison's framing, disputes the application; the post office is the classic response); necessary ≠ everything convenient, because the end must be legitimate.
- The operative limit is pretext — "the hook to press against [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]]."
- NFIB move to memorise: the mandate fails not because it is unnecessary but because it is too great to be incidental; Ginsburg's counter is that there is no workable line.
- Two wrinkles from the Bank debate: the Confederation had a bank (class note: helps Madison); an express incorporation power was dropped at the Convention (helps Madison and Jefferson, though Madison did not lean on it).
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 18): liquidation — practice over time settles meaning; "Baude's liquidation theory / readings → dig deep." Jackson's veto did not unsettle the Bank's constitutionality; Madison was right by his own mechanism.
- Office hours: after Jackson, "[i]t wasn't general consensus/settled" that a bank was unconstitutional — one point against saying the Constitution's meaning was liquidated against a national bank. What died in 1832–36 was the institution, not the settlement on constitutionality.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** McCulloch deference plus Comstock rational basis, limited only by NFIB's "proper."
- **The Bank as the paradigm liquidation (see Part I § 2):** Madison opposed the Bank in 1791, then in 1815–16 waived the question as "precluded . . . by repeated recognitions under varied circumstances." "My construction of the Constitution on this point is not changed" ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]) — what changed was settlement by indeterminacy, deliberate practice, acquiescence and public sanction.
- **Off track?** A means serving many powers at once starts to look freestanding; the great-powers screen is the principled limit the deference cases lack.
- **Should:** Ashwin should keep McCulloch (2018 Q3: never overrule it — every limit in the modern doctrine is quarried from it) and keep Comstock as consistent with McCulloch; defend NFIB's "proper" as McCulloch's own size principle; concede Ginsburg's line-drawing point and answer that "great power" is anchored in history (what the founding generation would expect to see enumerated), not intuition.

### Fact triggers

- A statute rests on no single clause, or on a chain of means (crime → prison → something more) → N&P gates 1–3, Comstock factors.
- A mandate or purchase requirement on individuals → "proper" / great power / predicate (NFIB).
- A federal instrumentality and a state tax or burden → McCulloch Part II (discrimination; "part on the whole").

### Oscillation points (ranked)

1. **Strongest limit:** predicate-creation — the challenged means drags new persons into federal power.
2. **Next:** great power — the means is the kind of power one expects to see enumerated.
3. **Weakest:** degree of necessity — fails in court under McCulloch.
4. **Strongest for Congress:** the means operates within a concededly valid scheme and the scheme fails without it (Ginsburg; Scalia's Raich concurrence).

### Traps

- **"McCulloch means deference, full stop."** It also contains "letter and spirit" and pretext — the sources of Printz and NFIB.
- **"Necessary and proper is one idea."** Separate the words, or the holdings of Printz and NFIB are unreachable.
- **"Madison changed his mind about the Bank."** He changed his mind about the weight of settled practice.

### Canned hook

> "The question is not whether this means is necessary — McCulloch leaves that to Congress — but whether it is proper: whether it executes a granted power or is itself 'a great substantive and independent power' of the kind the Framers would have enumerated."

## 3. The commerce power

**Clause** — "To regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes," Art. I § 8 cl. 3 (Art.I-8.3), working with Art.I-8.18 for the substantial-effects category.

**Bottom line** — Congress may regulate the channels of interstate commerce, its instrumentalities and the persons and things in it, and activities that substantially affect it ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]]). In the third category the activity must be economic and may then be aggregated ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]]; [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]]); noneconomic activity is reached only as "an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity," and inactivity is not reached at all ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]], Roberts).

### The test

1. **Channels?** A shipment ban qualifies whatever its motive ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]]). *Heart of Atlanta Motel* is filed here.
2. **Instrumentalities, or persons or things in commerce?** Intrastate threats to interstate carriers (*Shreveport Rate Case*). No economic nexus required — the train-robbery statute survives here.
3. **Substantial effects — is the activity economic?** "[T]he production, distribution, and consumption of commodities" ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]], Stevens). Gun possession "is in no sense an economic activity" ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]]).
4. **The Lopez factors:** jurisdictional element; findings (helpful, not sufficient — *Morrison*); attenuation ("pile inference upon inference"); area of traditional state concern (Lopez, Kennedy).
5. **If economic, does the class in aggregate substantially affect commerce?** Rational basis; the de minimis character of individual instances is "of no consequence" ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]]; [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]]).
6. **If noneconomic, is it essential to a larger regulation of an interstate market** — would exemption leave "a gaping hole"? ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]])
7. **Activity, not inactivity?** The power "presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]], Roberts).
8. **Proper means?** No predicate-creation, no commandeering — route to § 2 and § 4.
9. **Backup power?** Taxing "reaches every subject" — see [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].

### The lineage — expansion, then reclamation

- **Before Hammer — the prohibition line:** *Champion v. Ames* (Lottery Case, 1903) held that the power to regulate includes the power to prohibit interstate shipment; the *Houston* (Shreveport) and *Caminetti* line followed — Congress reaching intrastate rates and noncommercial interstate travel. These are the deep pulls for "regulate includes prohibit."
- **Formalist trough:** [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] (1824, Marshall) defines commerce as "intercourse" and gives the plenary-power language; [[United States v. E.C. Knight Co|E.C. Knight]] (1895, 8–1, Fuller; Harlan dissenting): "Commerce succeeds to manufacture and is not a part of it"; [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer]] (1918, 5–4, Day): child-labor goods "of themselves harmless," production local, with a pretext inquiry; [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] (1935): "indirect effects."
- **Holmes's dissent that won** (Hammer, Holmes): "Regulation means the prohibition of something" — reachable on the text alone, with no incidental-powers argument needed.
- **Modern origin:** *Shreveport* / *Swift* → [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]] (1937, 5–4, Hughes) → [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]] (1941, unanimous, Stone: generalizes J&L, overrules Hammer, makes the Tenth Amendment "a truism") → [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]] (1942, unanimous, Jackson: adds aggregation).
- **One-line arc:** Holmes = goods must cross the border → Darby = reach production that *affects* commerce → Wickard = *aggregate* effects, no crossing required.
- **The pretext arc:** Marshall reserves the pretext check → Wickard/Jackson releases it to politics ("effective restraints . . . must proceed from political rather than from judicial processes") → Lopez/*Morrison* (commerce side) and NFIB (N&P side) reclaim a judicial limit.

### Darby's two halves

- **The shipment ban** rests on the Commerce Clause directly — regulate includes prohibit (*Champion*). No McCulloch needed. Stone takes from [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] that the power is "complete in itself" and adds motive-blindness: motive and purpose "are matters for the legislative judgment . . . over which the courts are given no control."
- **The wage-and-hour rule for production** is the hard half, because production is not itself "commerce" (the premise of E.C. Knight and Hammer). The bridge is McCulloch: Congress may regulate intrastate activities that so affect commerce "as to make regulation of them appropriate means to the attainment of a legitimate end" — Marshall's formula almost verbatim.
- **So:** Darby = Commerce Clause for the border crossing + McCulloch means-end logic for production. J&L and Darby spoke in commerce terms, using McCulloch's reasoning as support; the explicit re-grounding of substantial effects in the N&P *clause* is a modern gloss (Lopez's categories, Scalia in Raich, NFIB).

### Framing A vs. Framing B

- **Framing A — Commerce Clause alone:** the local activity *is* within the power because it substantially affects commerce. The only question is magnitude, and after aggregation that is barely a limit. One porous screen.
- **Framing B — Commerce + N&P (means-end):** the local activity is not commerce; Congress reaches it only as a *means* to regulating interstate commerce (Raich, Scalia). The derivation imports two limits Framing A lacks:
  - **A genuine commerce-regulating end.** The local reach must effectuate a real regulation of an interstate market. Lopez and *Morrison* fail because no market scheme exists for the reach to serve; Raich succeeds because the home-grown ban stops leakage into the regulated interstate drug market.
  - **"Proper."** The means cannot be a great independent power or subvert structure — NFIB: the mandate may be necessary to the insurance reforms but is not proper.
- **Why the Court re-routed:** after Wickard the magnitude test had dissolved; Lopez's "economic" requirement rebuilt a limit clumsily inside Framing A; Raich and NFIB moved to Framing B to get the "proper" screen, a structural limit magnitude cannot supply. The limit comes from the grammar of derivation, not from N&P as a "limiting clause."

### Economic character vs. jurisdictional hook

- **Economic character works at the class level, categorically:** if the regulated activity is economic, aggregation applies and no hook is needed (Wickard, Raich). This is category three proper.
- **A jurisdictional element works at the case level, individually:** a statute reaching only a gun "that has moved in interstate commerce" relies on category one/two (the thing crossed a line), proved prosecution by prosecution. The hook is the fallback for *non-economic* statutes. Open: whether past movement suffices forever (Thomas, dissenting from denial in *Alderman*).

### Lopez vs. Raich — the line he will exploit

- **Activity vs. object (the thin distinction):** Lopez regulated *where you may carry* a gun, not the gun market; Raich regulated *cultivation and consumption* of the commodity itself. Stevens's definition — production, distribution and consumption of commodities — fits Raich, but a gun is also a commodity, so the line blurs.
- **Comprehensive scheme vs. freestanding statute (the strong distinction):** the GFSZA was standalone; the CSA is an integrated "closed regulatory system," and the home-grown ban is one gear in it. Section 922(q) is severable from the economic parts of § 922; home-grown marijuana is load-bearing within the CSA.
- **Two-step scheme test:**
  - **Umbrella test:** is the provision embedded in a broader statute regulating a commercial market (production, distribution, sale of a commodity), not a standalone police statute?
  - **Leakage/essentiality test (the workhorse):** would exempting the local activity *undercut* the market regulation — is the local activity fungible with, or a substitute for, the regulated interstate supply?
- **The counter (Raich, O'Connor):** packaging local regulation inside broad schemes makes Lopez "nothing more than a drafting guide." (Raich, Thomas): personal medical use is "a distinct and separable subclass."

### Cases and who said what

- [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] (1995, 5–4, Rehnquist) → GFSZA invalid; three categories; overrules nothing. (Lopez, Kennedy with O'Connor): New Deal cases "not called in question." (Lopez, Thomas): commerce = "selling, buying, and bartering"; substantial effects "has swallowed Art. I, § 8." (Lopez, Breyer, dissenting): rational basis on cumulative effects; the economic line gives "controlling force to nomenclature."
- [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]] (2005, 6–3, Stevens) → CSA reaches home-grown medical marijuana. (Raich, Scalia, concurring): substantial effects rests on N&P; reach extends "only to those measures necessary to make the interstate regulation effective."
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] (2012) → no power to compel entry into commerce; Filburn "was at least actively engaged in the production of wheat" (Roberts). Joint dissent: Wickard is "the ne plus ultra."
- **Distinguishing line** — Local activity is reachable when it is production or consumption of a fungible commodity that a comprehensive federal scheme regulates in an interstate market, and not when it is freestanding noneconomic conduct.

- Raich makes the case a framing contest: what is the "class of activities," and how large is the scheme?
- Federalism cases do not overrule each other the way the separation-of-powers line does.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** three categories, economic activity, aggregation, scheme exception, activity limit.
- **Off track?** Thomas's original meaning (commerce = trade) would make the bankruptcy, coinage and patent powers non-surplusage and would reject Wickard; Holmes's middle ground (prohibit crossings, not regulate production) is the coherent stopping point Darby abandoned unanimously.

Strongest counter: reliance — the regulatory state rests on Wickard, and Darby's means-end reasoning already supplies Framing B without overruling anything.

### Fact triggers — the 2023 Q2 location set (gun-free "sensitive places")

### Oscillation points (ranked)

1. **Strongest for federal power:** a fungible commodity inside a comprehensive scheme (leakage).
2. **Next:** a jurisdictional hook.
3. **Strongest against:** freestanding noneconomic conduct in an area of traditional state concern.

### Traps

- **"It substantially affects commerce, so it's valid."** Ask first whether the activity is economic.
- **"Wickard reaches any economic ripple."** Wickard rests on substitution plus aggregation within a commodity scheme.
- **"Darby is only a channels case."** The production half is McCulloch in commerce clothing.

### Canned hook

> "The threshold question is which Lopez category we are in. If the law reaches a channel or instrumentality, no economic nexus is required; if it rests on substantial effects, the activity must be economic — and the real fight, after Raich, is how broadly to draw the class and the scheme."

## 4. State sovereignty and anti-commandeering

**Clause** — Tenth Amendment, "The powers not delegated . . . are reserved" (Amend. X) — no "expressly"; Supremacy, "the Judges in every State shall be bound," Art. VI cl. 2 (Art.VI-2); the oath binding state "executive and judicial Officers," Art. VI cl. 3 (Art.VI-3); N&P "proper," Art.I-8.18.

**Bottom line** — Congress may regulate a State on the same terms as private parties ([[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]]), but may not command a State's legislature to legislate or its officers to administer a federal program ([[New York v. United States]]; [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]). The Tenth Amendment is "a truism" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]]); the operative limit is structural and runs through "proper." Congress may still buy cooperation short of coercion ([[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]]).

### The test

1. **Within an enumerated power?** If not, the law fails for that reason alone.
2. **Whom does the law regulate?** Private actors only → preemption, no problem. The State alongside private actors → [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]], no immunity. The State as a government, told how to govern → gate 3.
3. **Does it command a state organ to govern?** Legislature ([[New York v. United States]], 1992, 6–3, O'Connor: take-title); executive officers ([[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]); prohibition on state legislation (*Murphy v. NCAA*, 7–2: the do/don't distinction "is empty"). State courts are outside the rule (*Testa v. Katt*).
4. **Is it "proper"?** A law violating state sovereignty is not "proper for carrying into Execution the Commerce Clause" (Printz, Scalia).
5. **Does it shift execution away from the President?** Article II (Printz).
6. **Did Congress use money instead?** Dole conditions and NFIB coercion — see [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
7. **Reserved categories:** information-only duties; consent; emergencies.

### The key distinction

- **Does the federal rule bind private actors on the same terms as state actors?** If yes, the law regulates, and anti-commandeering is not triggered — Garcia (FLSA), *Reno v. Condon* (data sales), *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023, ICWA duties apply to private and state actors alike). If the law's object is to direct how the State governs, Printz and New York apply.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] (1997, 5–4, Scalia) → Congress may not "command the States' officers . . . to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program"; categorical, so the modest, temporary Brady duty did not matter. History (early statutes bound only judges), structure ("two political capacities"; voters blame the sheriff), Article II ("as effectively without the President as with him"). (Printz, O'Connor): voluntary, paid and ministerial reporting left open. (Printz, Thomas): doubts the commerce power reaches intrastate handgun sales. (Printz, Stevens, dissenting): text, Federalist 27, the oath, emergencies; the judge/executive line is "empty formalistic reasoning of the highest order." (Printz, Breyer): European federal systems.
- [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] (1985, 5–4, Blackmun; best counter) → overrules *National League of Cities*; "traditional governmental functions" is unworkable; protection "is one of process rather than one of result." (Garcia, Powell, dissenting): the States' role is "a matter of constitutional law, not of legislative grace." (Garcia, O'Connor, dissenting): McCulloch's "letter and spirit" protects state integrity.
- [[New York v. United States]] (1992) → no commanding legislatures; take-title invalid.
- **Distinguishing line** — Same terms as private actors → Garcia and the political process; object is to make the State legislate or administer → Printz and the statute falls.

### Garcia as a political-question doctrine without the jurisdictional consequence

- Garcia borrows the political-question factors — no manageable standards ("licensing drivers" vs. "regulating traffic" is "elusive at best") and something like a commitment to Congress — but decides the merits and reserves "what affirmative limits the constitutional structure might impose."
- The political safeguards: state control of electoral qualifications, the Electoral College, equal Senate representation. The Seventeenth Amendment wedge cuts both ways — either the People abandoned the Senate as the States' agent, or losing that safeguard makes judicial enforcement more necessary ([[A Map of the Federalism Provisions]]).

### Baude in class

- The line sits at *means*, not subject matter: using state machinery as the means fails "proper" — the defect in the Brady Act.
- Scalia's Article II argument (Congress cannot bypass federal enforcement by conscripting state officers) comes first in the opinion, but office hours demoted it: accountability is doing more work than Article II. Supporting arguments: resource drain and implicit buy-in.
- Office hours: cooperative federalism — States voluntarily executing federal law — "is completely fine and is not challenged. Do not try to challenge it." There is no accountability problem, because voters can still point to the state officials who chose to act; that is why accountability, not Article II, carries Printz.
- A State enacting the same law itself is fine, because the objection to the means disappears.
- Office hours: Printz reserved information-reporting duties as not "the precise issue before us."
- Office hours (Garcia): States are better represented in the national political process than individuals, which is why political safeguards are trusted for States but not for rights; he called the trend — substantive due process down, federalism up — a fair critique of the political-safeguards argument.
- Office hours: the sympathetic case for political safeguards is that Congress could do far more than it does, so political pressure does the limiting (the Lopez government's "there are lots of things we don't regulate" is less bad than it sounds); the case against is minority States and pre-Union state sovereignty; prudential localism is the better version.
- Class note (uncertain transcription, "none [?]"): he may see no tangible difference between Printz and Garcia on this axis — a possible opening to argue the line is unprincipled; do not lean on it.
- Printz is the first opinion to approach the commerce power bottom-up, from the States; its effect is to let States decline to enforce federal immigration and gun law.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine:** Garcia for generally applicable laws; Printz/New York/*Murphy* categorical for commands.
- **Off track? — the history:** Campbell (2013, Printz Note 3) argues commandeering was, at the Founding, the *States'-rights* alternative to a large federal bureaucracy — States preferred administering federal law to being cut out. Stevens's Printz dissent makes the policy version: forbidding commandeering breeds the national bureaucracy federalism fears. Bellia and Clark (2020) push back.
- **Off track? — the Article II argument:** Scalia's unity-of-execution argument proves too much. Scalia accepts voluntary cooperative federalism and paid participation, and those arrangements equally put execution in the hands of state officers beyond meaningful presidential control. If voluntary state enforcement is consistent with Article II, compelled enforcement cannot violate Article II for that reason; the real ground must be state sovereignty and "proper."
- **Should (2018 Q3 feed):** keep both Garcia and Printz, because they answer different questions (regulating States vs. commanding them) and the private-actor-parity line reconciles them; ground Printz in "proper" and political accountability, not Article II. Strongest counter: if the spending power can buy what commandeering forbids, Printz is a drafting rule.

### Fact triggers

- A federal statute orders state officials to run checks, collect data or enforce → Printz; check the information-only reservation (8 U.S.C. § 1373).
- A federal statute forbids States to authorize or repeal something → *Murphy*.
- A federal wage, data or safety rule that binds States and private parties alike → Garcia.
- Federal money conditioned on a state law → Dole/NFIB.
- A federal statute requiring state courts to hear federal claims → *Testa*, permitted.

### Oscillation points (ranked)

1. **Strongest for the State:** a direct order to legislate or administer, with no private analogue.
2. **Next:** an information-reporting duty — reserved, with Stevens likening Brady to one.
3. **Weakest for the State:** a generally applicable regulation (Garcia).
4. **Wildcard:** emergencies — Stevens's air-raid wardens, draft and inoculation get no direct answer.

### Traps

- **"Garcia was overruled."** It never was; New York and Printz route around it.
- **"The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not *expressly* delegated."** Day inserted "expressly" in Hammer; the text omits it.
- **"Commandeering ends the analysis."** Always run the spending route next.
- **"Printz rests on Article VI's judges-only text."** Printz concedes no text on point.

### Canned hook

> "The question is not whether this law burdens the State — Garcia leaves ordinary burdens to the political process — but whether it regulates the State as it regulates everyone else, or instead conscripts the State's machinery as the means of federal governance, which is not a 'proper' means under Printz."

# Part VI — Federalism II: Taxing, Spending, and NFIB

## 1. The taxing power

**Clause.** "The Congress shall have Power To lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises . . . but all Duties, Imposts and Excises shall be uniform throughout the United States" — Art. I § 8 cl. 1 (Art.I-8.1). Point to four more places on the page:

- **Art. I § 2 cl. 3 and § 9 cl. 4 (Art.I-2.3, Art.I-9.4)** — the apportionment rule, stated twice, for "Capitation, or other direct, Tax."
- **Art. I § 9 cl. 5 (Art.I-9.5)** — "No Tax or Duty shall be laid on Articles exported from any State."
- **Sixteenth Amendment** — income taxes "from whatever source derived, without apportionment."
- **Grammar of cl. 1** — "Taxes" is the general word and includes direct taxes; the uniformity proviso reaches only "Duties, Imposts and Excises."

**Bottom line.** The exaction should be upheld as a tax if it functions as one — revenue-raising, modest, scienter-free, IRS-collected, and leaving payment a lawful choice ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]], Roberts). If it functions as a penalty, it needs another enumerated power ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture]], Taft). If it is a tax, it must still take the right form: apportioned if direct, geographically uniform if a duty, impost or excise.

### The test

1. **Tax or penalty?** Label is irrelevant to constitutional power (NFIB). Run the [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]] factors, which NFIB turned over:
   - **Prohibitory?** Bailey's 10 percent of a year's net profits for one child for one day was; a payment "far less than the price of insurance" is not.
   - **Scienter?** "[T]ypical of punitive statutes" (NFIB, Roberts describing Bailey).
   - **Which agency enforces?** IRS points to tax; the Labor Department points to penalty.
   - **Prescribed code of conduct?** A "heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business" — the factor Taft treats as indispensable.
   - **Revenue?** "[T]he essential feature of any tax" ([[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]]).
   - **Unlawfulness?** Congress "did not think it was creating four million outlaws" (NFIB).
2. **If the natural reading is a command, is a tax reading "fairly possible"?** Constitutional avoidance, after the most natural reading fails (NFIB, Part III-C).
3. **If a penalty, what other power supports it?** Route to [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]] or [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
4. **If a tax, is it direct?** Settled direct taxes are capitations and land taxes ([[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton]]); *Pollock* added taxes on personal property and its income. Iredell's test: "the Constitution contemplated none as direct but such as could be apportioned."
5. **Does it satisfy the form rule for its class?** Direct → apportioned. Duty, impost or excise → uniform. Income → Sixteenth Amendment. Export → barred outright.
6. **Is it a lever on the States?** A tax-and-credit scheme inducing state legislation routes to spending ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]).

### Uniformity versus apportionment — the arithmetic

- **Uniform** = same rate in every State. **Apportioned** = same amount per person in every State, so the rate must differ wherever wealth differs. Apportionment protects against uniform taxes with disparate impact; uniformity protects against disparately targeted statutes (Baude, Class 21).
- **Why apportionment is fatal rather than annoying.** Two States, one million people each. State A's per-capita income is $100k; State B's is $25k. Congress wants $200M, so each State owes $100M. State A's rate: $100M ÷ $100B = 0.1%. State B's rate: $100M ÷ $25B = 0.4%. The poorer State pays four times the rate. "Must be apportioned" effectively means "cannot exist."
- **Iredell's carriage point.** "If any state had no carriages, there could be no apportionment at all." Apportionment assumed heads and wealth tracked each other, which held only for land; *Hylton*, *Pollock*, the Sixteenth Amendment and *Moore v. United States* are the law coping with that assumption failing.
- **Capitation** is a per-head tax ($10 per person), paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance." The NFIB payment escapes because it is "triggered by specific circumstances" and avoidable: buy insurance and you owe nothing. The feature that makes the mandate feel coercive is the same feature that makes it indirect.
- **Geographic uniformity.** A duty, impost or excise laid only in some States, or exempting one, fails uniformity unless the classification is genuinely non-geographic. *United States v. Ptasynski* (1983) allowed a geographically defined exemption absent actual regional favoritism; an exemption written to buy one Senator's vote is the strongest case against it.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]] (1796, seriatim; Chase, Paterson, Iredell) → a carriage tax is not direct because apportionment cannot "reasonably apply"; Paterson's third class, "[a]ll other classes of an indirect kind." Collusive record (stipulated 125 carriages; Hamilton for the government).
- *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.* (1895) → income from property is direct; reversed by the Sixteenth Amendment. Baude: hard to reconcile with Hylton.
- *License Tax Cases*; *Veazie Bank v. Fenno*; [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] (1904) → Congress may tax what it cannot regulate; courts review neither size ("not to the courts, but to the people") nor motive.
- [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture]] (1922, 8–1, Taft) → the Child Labor Tax is a penalty; "a court must be blind not to see" its object. The leading case holding a tax went too far (see also *Hill v. Wallace* (1922) and *United States v. Constantine* (1935)).
- [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] (1953) → a flat occupational tax on bookmakers survives though revenue is negligible.
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] (2012, 5–4 on tax, Roberts with Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan) → the shared responsibility payment is a tax and not direct (NFIB, joint dissent: "we have never held—never—that a penalty . . . was so trivial as to be in effect a tax").
- **Distinguishing line (NFIB leads; Bailey counters).** A statute writing its own code of conduct and punishing departures heavily, with scienter and non-revenue enforcement, imposes a penalty; a modest, IRS-collected, revenue-raising payment leaving a lawful choice is a tax.

### Baude in class

- He ran Hylton, Pollock, License Tax Cases, Veazie, McCray and Bailey in a row: inconsistent case law with no real limits but also a technical limit.
- Taxpayer standing to challenge a tax is "very orthodox" (Hylton). See [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- "Bailey is very ambiguous" (Class 23) — the factors are arguable in both directions, and they work as factors, not a checklist.
- His hypo (Class 21): a ban on brewing whiskey at home to help collect taxes works as the tax power plus Necessary and Proper.
- His hypo (Class 23): a tax to create a gun-free school zone probably works, because a tax is not punitive the way jail is.
- His aside: Congress could have called the mandate an income tax and avoided the direct-tax question entirely.
- Office hours: Hylton and Pollock start from the same premise — apportionment is "hard and weird" — and reach opposite conclusions: Hylton, so do not call things direct taxes and take the indirect route; Pollock, so the tax cannot be laid.
- Past-exam pattern (not a class statement): a tax question appeared in 2018 Q1, 2021 Q1 and 2023 Q2. Tax-versus-penalty drives 2021 and 2023; 2018 Q1's wildfire tax is really a direct-tax and uniformity question.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine.** The only enforced limit is Bailey's penalty line, rarely applied since (*Constantine*, 1935); once [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]] overruled [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer]], Bailey's second step lost its premise.
- **Should.** The best understanding should keep Bailey's line but anchor it in text: "Taxes" are exactions for revenue, so a measure whose face (scienter, code, non-revenue enforcer, prohibitory rate) shows revenue is not its object is not a tax — Taft's workaround of McCray, reading the statute rather than motives.
- **Strongest counter.** Kahriger and McCray: there is no administrable line, and judicial weighing of purpose corrects one department's abuse "by the abuse of its powers by another department."

### Fact triggers

- Tax/penalty: "tax" plus "knowingly," a large flat sum, or a non-IRS enforcer.
- Direct tax: an exaction per person or on ownership (wealth, head, land).
- Uniformity: the statute names States or exempts one.

### Oscillation points

- **The 2023 Q2 Gun Free Sensitive Places Tax.** $25,000, "knowingly," enforced by four non-revenue agencies: all three Bailey signals, so a penalty. Each location then needs its own power — federal property and postal power for post offices and federal courthouses; channels or instrumentalities for train stations ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]]), with [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]] weaker; schools hardest, because Lopez struck the school-zone statute.
- **The 2021 Q1 carbon tax.** $51 per ton is revenue-raising and non-prohibitory; EPA collection is the Bailey factor against; the West Virginia exemption is the uniformity problem, since an emissions levy is an excise.
- **The 2018 Q1 wildfire tax.** Per person, only in five named States: a capitation would be unapportioned; an excise non-uniform. Best defense: triggered by a circumstance and income-exempt, so not a capitation (NFIB) — and if neither direct nor a duty, impost or excise, the uniformity proviso arguably does not reach it.
- **Drafting cures.** Make it a payable credit or modest surcharge so there is no "heavy exaction for a departure" ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]); move collection to the IRS; drop scienter. Pushing the code into agency rules invites a nondelegation objection ([[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]).

### Traps

- **"The label decides."** It decides the Anti-Injunction Act, not constitutional power: Bailey turns a "tax" into a penalty; NFIB turns a "penalty" into a tax.
- **"Apportionment and uniformity are the same rule."** Same rate everywhere versus same amount per head everywhere; only a capitation satisfies both.
- **"The Sixteenth Amendment ended direct-tax questions."** It reaches only taxes "on incomes"; a wealth tax still faces Hylton and *Pollock*, and *Moore* left realization open.

### Canned hook

- "The exaction should be upheld as a tax: it raises revenue, is collected by the IRS, carries no scienter requirement, and leaves paying 'a reasonable financial decision' (NFIB, Roberts). Bailey's penalty line survives, but none of its signals is present."
- "Even if this is a tax, it must take the right form. Because it is laid only in [named States], it fails uniformity if it is an excise and fails apportionment if it is a capitation (Art.I-8.1; Art.I-9.4)."

## 2. The spending power

**Clause.** "To . . . pay the Debts and provide for the common Defence and general Welfare of the United States" — Art. I § 8 cl. 1 (Art.I-8.1). Read with Art. I § 9 cl. 7 (Art.I-9.7, appropriations) and the Necessary and Proper Clause (Art.I-8.18).

**Bottom line.** Congress may spend for the general welfare beyond its other enumerated powers ([[United States v. Butler|Butler]]) and may buy from the States what it cannot command, so long as the condition is unambiguous, germane, does not induce unconstitutional state action, and does not cross from "pressure" into "compulsion" ([[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]]). The live arguments are ambiguity and coercion; general welfare is nominal.

### The test — Dole's four limits plus the fifth

1. **General welfare.** "[C]ourts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress"; the object must be "General and not local" ([[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]]), a limit never enforced ([[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering]]: discretion belongs to Congress "unless the choice is clearly wrong").
2. **Unambiguous.** The State must "exercise [its] choice knowingly," because spending legislation is "much in the nature of a contract."
3. **Germane.** Related "to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs" — shared purpose (Dole, Rehnquist) against marginal effect (Dole, O'Connor).
4. **No independent constitutional bar.** The condition may not induce the State to act unconstitutionally; the limit is "not . . . a prohibition on the indirect achievement of objectives which Congress is not empowered to achieve directly."
5. **No coercion.** Pressure may not turn into compulsion ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]; Dole; first enforced in NFIB). NFIB's two facts: sheer size (over 10 percent of a State's budget) and old funds threatened to force a new program.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]] (1817) → "general Welfare" is limited by the enumeration; [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]] (1791) → its own end, but "General and not local"; [[The Savannah Fire Debate]] (1796, defeated 55–34) → relief to one city fails even Hamilton's limit.
- [[United States v. Butler|Butler]] (1936) → adopts Hamilton's view while striking the AAA; its "purchase compliance" half is what Dole repudiates.
- [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]] (1937, 5–4) → "temptation" is not coercion; [[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering]] (1937) → welfare is "shaped by Congress."
- [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] (1987, 7–2, Rehnquist) → 5 percent of highway funds for a 21 drinking age is "relatively mild encouragement" (Dole, O'Connor: a "regulation determining who shall be able to drink"; Dole, Brennan: the Twenty-first Amendment is an independent bar).
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] (2012, 7–2 on coercion) → "a gun to the head" (NFIB, Roberts); Medicaid is "a single program with a constant aim" (NFIB, Ginsburg).
- [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon]] → taxpayer and declining-State suits over spending lack standing.
- **Distinguishing line (Dole leads; NFIB counters).** Threatening a State's large, existing, independent grants to force a new program is coercion; conditions on new money or modest amendments are not.

### Baude in class

- He teaches spending as the Hamiltonian view the Court came around to, against Madison's reading that "general welfare" limits only the laying of taxes and is fixed by the enumeration.
- Run Dole as four prongs plus an overall fifth limit: general welfare, unambiguous, germaneness, not unconstitutional, no coercion.
- The fourth prong is close to unusable.
- Between Dole and NFIB the unambiguous prong became the leading prong, operating almost as a nondelegation doctrine ([[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]).
- O'Connor supplies the two arguments against a condition: not germane, and her categorical limit — Congress may say where money goes but may not attach separate regulations as a quid pro quo.
- His hypo: could Congress set a national drinking age by commerce? Only via substantial effects plus a comprehensive scheme ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]], [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]]) — and even then it is only a federal crime, since under [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] the State may keep the conduct legal. Spending reaches what commerce cannot: state law itself.
- Office hours: Blackmun's [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] political-process view against O'Connor's categorical limit.
- Office hours: the Dole factors decide whether an exercise of the spending power is valid or invalid — not whether it is anti-commandeering; do not use commandeering vocabulary. After NFIB the four factors remain current, with coercion as a fifth.
- Office hours: O'Connor has two separate objections — she does not buy the safety connection, and the test has no categorical limit. Baude's answer to the categorical limit is drafting: spend only on highways where teenage accidents occur, which bypasses the limit and forces her back to the weaker relatedness objection.
- Office hours: Garcia's political-process logic can justify conditional spending as a solution to a collective-action problem — States agree a rule is good but none will adopt it alone, so a national conditional program is the political process working.

### The 2018 Q1 wildfire conditions, run through the test

- **General welfare.** Mitigation conditions apply in all States; passes.
- **Unambiguous — strongest challenge.** "[P]asses regulation to substantially mitigate the risks of wildfires" gives no content in advance; whether a State complied is decided after a fire by the Secretary of the Interior. A State cannot accept "knowingly" a condition it cannot read, and judicial review of the Secretary does not cure the ex ante problem. This is the nondelegation-flavored prong Baude flagged.
- **Germane.** Fire-mitigation regulation conditioning fire-suppression money passes even O'Connor's marginal-effect test: mitigation makes suppression cheaper.
- **Independent bar.** None; the condition asks for legislation the State may lawfully enact.
- **Coercion.** The FMAG grant (about $600M against a $200B budget, 0.3 percent) is Dole-sized. But both programs are pre-existing (NFIB's reliance factor), and Forest Service "watch it burn" withholds protection of life and property, not just dollars — coercion "not merely in theory but in fact." Counter: Forest Service operations are federal services, not a grant to the State, so they may not be a "condition" at all, and the question becomes whether Congress may direct executive non-protection ([[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]]).
- **Anti-commandeering overlay.** The condition buys state legislation, which Dole permits and [[New York v. United States|New York]] forbids by command; NFIB roots the coercion limit in that same principle.
- **Ranking.** Ambiguity first, coercion via "watch it burn" second, germaneness last.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Off track.** Madison's reading has the better structural argument — the broad reading "would render nugatory the enumeration of particular powers" ([[The Bank Debate]]) — and conditional grants are the hole in Hamilton's concession that appropriation carries no power "to do any other thing."
- **Should.** A departmentalist should adopt O'Connor's categorical line: Congress may specify how its money is spent, but a requirement beyond that is "not a condition, but a regulation, which is valid only if it falls within one of Congress' delegated regulatory powers." It is administrable, tracks the text, and preserves the Hamiltonian spending power itself.
- **Strongest counter.** The First Congress funded lighthouses "from Maine to Georgia," and practice since has arguably liquidated the broad reading; O'Connor's line also invites endless fights over what counts as the funded activity.

### Fact triggers

- This is a coercion problem when the threatened money is old, large relative to the State's budget, and from a separate program.
- This is an ambiguity problem when compliance is determined after the fact by an officer.

### Oscillation point — repeal and re-enact

- **The hypo.** Congress repeals Medicaid outright and re-enacts it tomorrow with the expansion built in — one program, take it or leave it, no pre-existing funds to threaten. On Roberts's reasoning that should be fine — the hypo Baude posed in Class 23. Is the holding a structural protection or a drafting rule?
- **Ranked responses:**
  1. **Reliance, read functionally.** Coercion asks whether the State accepted "voluntarily and knowingly," and Roberts measures choice "not merely in theory but in fact." A State with fifty years of Medicaid infrastructure is not choosing on a blank slate; "existing funds" is a proxy for reliance, not a formal category.
  2. **Look through the sequencing.** Repeal-then-re-offer is substantively identical to threaten-to-withdraw; the gun is reloaded, not holstered. The burden is explaining why two lawful steps combine into an unlawful one — which is what unconstitutional-conditions doctrine does.
  3. **Limit the bundling.** Expansion is germane to Medicaid, so this bundle survives, but re-enact Medicaid conditioned on something unrelated and the bundle itself is the violation. Concedes the case, saves the doctrine.
  4. **Political impossibility.** Repeal would defund every State, including willing ones. True, and why the holding bites in practice — but an admission that the constraint is formal, not legal.

### Traps

- **"Dole's fourth limit means Congress may not do indirectly what it cannot do directly."** Dole rejects exactly that; Congress may buy what it may not command.
- **"There are four limits."** Coercion is the fifth, and the only one that has ever invalidated a spending statute.
- **"NFIB set a percentage."** Roberts declines to "fix the outermost line"; size and old-versus-new money work together.

### Canned hook

- "The condition should fail Dole's clear-notice requirement before it ever reaches coercion: a State cannot 'exercise [its] choice knowingly' when compliance is defined only after the fact by the Secretary."
- "Degree of success is not coercion (Dole); threatening money a State already relies on, at a scale that leaves 'no real option,' is (NFIB, Roberts)."

## 3. NFIB v. Sebelius as the synthesis case

**Clause.** Art. I § 8 cl. 1, cl. 3 and cl. 18 (Art.I-8.1, Art.I-8.3, Art.I-8.18), with the Tenth Amendment as the coercion backdrop.

**Bottom line.** [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] runs every federalism power on one statute and answers each differently, so it is the template for any multi-power question: commerce first, then Necessary and Proper, then tax as the avoidance backstop, then spending. Baude "REALLY" likes the opinion, so its sequence is the sequence to write in.

### The holdings, the votes, the authors

| Issue | Result | Vote | Opinion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-Injunction Act | No bar — the label controls this statute | Unanimous | Roberts |
| Commerce Clause | Mandate invalid | 5 Justices, two opinions, no opinion of the Court | Roberts (Part III-A, alone) + joint dissent |
| Necessary and Proper | Mandate not "proper" | Same five | Roberts alone + joint dissent |
| Taxing power | Mandate valid as a tax; not direct | 5–4, opinion of the Court (Part III-C) | Roberts + Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan |
| Medicaid coercion | Threat to existing funds invalid | 7–2 | Roberts + Breyer, Kagan; joint dissent agrees |
| Remedy | Bar the threat only; expansion stands as an offer | 5–4 | Roberts plurality + Ginsburg, Sotomayor in the judgment |

- Only Roberts agreed with the whole of the reasoning and the judgment.
- **Vote-counting move.** It is unclear whether the commerce holding had five votes to make it binding: the joint dissenters agreed with Roberts's reasoning but joined no part of his opinion. Ginsburg calls it dictum because the tax holding carried the judgment; Roberts answers that the saving construction becomes available only after the straightforward commerce reading fails, so the commerce step was necessary. Class note (marked uncertain, "[?]"): the Marks rule — the narrowest ground becomes the holding.

### Cases and who said what

- **Commerce.** "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" (NFIB, Roberts); "If all inactivity affecting commerce is commerce, commerce is everything" (NFIB, joint dissent); forgoing insurance "is hardly inconsequential or equivalent to 'doing nothing'" (NFIB, Ginsburg). [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] limits by the kind of activity; NFIB by its existence. See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].
- **Necessary and Proper.** The mandate is a "great substantive and independent power," not an incident ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]), and creating "the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not proper (NFIB, Roberts); "You will know it when you see it" (NFIB, Ginsburg). Baude's own scholarship argues some powers are too great to be implied — the theory Roberts used. See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
- **Tax.** Functional, "fairly possible" reading; [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]] inverted on the same factors. See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].
- **Spending.** Coercion descends from anti-commandeering ([[New York v. United States|New York]]; [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]). See [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]] and [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].

- N&P: the mandate is too affirmative to be incidental (see Printz) — people cannot be commandeered; Ginsburg attacks the test as unworkable.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Off track.** Avoidance deployed after an adverse constitutional ruling lets the Court announce new limits costlessly; the tax holding "save[s] a statute Congress did not write" (NFIB, joint dissent).
- **Should.** The best understanding should keep Roberts's framework: Article I "enumerates not federally soluble problems, but federally available powers" (NFIB, joint dissent), and the "great power" screen is the only limit on the Necessary and Proper Clause that tracks McCulloch's own text. The tax holding is right too, because a power Congress has does not disappear for being mislabeled.
- **Strongest counter.** Ginsburg: the activity line is formalism with no economic content, and "[t]his Court's former endeavors to impose categorical limits on the commerce power have not fared well."

### Fact triggers

- This is an activity/inactivity problem when the statute compels a purchase or a status.
- This is a "great power" problem when the means is an independent, freestanding power dressed as an incident (compelled purchase, eminent domain, conscription).

### Traps

- **"NFIB held the mandate invalid under the Commerce Clause, for the Court."** No opinion of the Court on commerce; count the votes.
- **"The Medicaid expansion was struck down."** Only the threat to existing funds fell; the expansion survives as an offer.
- **"The tax question is closed."** Congress zeroed the payment in 2017; *California v. Texas* (2021) dismissed on standing, leaving open whether a $0 tax is a tax.

### Canned hook

- "Following NFIB's sequence, the provision should first be tested under the commerce power, then the Necessary and Proper Clause, and only then saved, if it can be, as a tax — which requires only a 'fairly possible' reading (NFIB, Roberts)."
- "The commerce holding had no opinion of the Court: five Justices agreed in two opinions, so whether it binds is itself contestable (NFIB, Ginsburg)."

# Part VII — The Rest of the Original Constitution: Articles IV, V, and VI

Classes 25–27 (Article IV, Article V, Article VI) have no typed session notes yet. Sections 1–3, 5 and 6 below are built from the doctrine modules and readings; revise each "Baude in class" block after those sessions are reconciled.

## 1. Privileges and immunities

**Clause.** "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States" — Art. IV § 2 cl. 1 (Art.IV-2.1).

**Bottom line.** A State may not treat citizens of other States worse than its own in the basic civil privileges of citizenship, but it may reserve political rights and its common property, and it may restrict any activity for everyone alike ([[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]]). If a protected privilege is burdened by a citizenship or residence line, the State must show a substantial reason and a close fit.

### The test

1. **Is the claimant a citizen of another State?** Corporations and trade associations are not (*Paul v. Virginia*), so they must sue under the dormant Commerce Clause.
2. **Does the law draw its line by citizenship or residence?** An evenhanded ban raises no claim; a city residency preference counts (*United Building & Construction Trades Council v. Camden*).
3. **Is the interest a protected civil privilege?** Corfield's heads: protection by government, property, passage "for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits," suing in state courts, equal taxes. Commercial livelihoods are in (*Hicklin v. Orbeck*; *Supreme Court of New Hampshire v. Piper*); recreational elk hunting is out (*Baldwin*).
4. **Is it a political right or a share in common property instead?** Voting, office and jury service; a resource held by citizens "as tenants in common" — though *Toomer v. Witsell* rejected ownership as a ground for hoarding commercial fisheries.
5. **If protected, a substantial reason closely related to the discrimination?** The burden is on the State.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]] (Washington, Circuit Justice) → privileges "which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments"; New Jersey may reserve its oyster beds. The catalogue is dictum; the holding is the common-property exception. One Justice on circuit, binding no one.
- [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] → the counter-case: trade associations, no citizenship line, a sales restriction. Distinguishing line: a citizenship line against a natural person in a protected privilege goes to Article IV; a burden on firms or in practical effect goes to the dormant Commerce Clause.
- Taney's citizenship holding in [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] is read as driven by refusing free Black citizens Article IV privileges (Section 4).

### Fact triggers and traps

- **Trap: "The visitor carries home-state rights."** The clause equalizes; it does not transport.

- **Trap: "A corporate client can sue under Article IV."** It cannot; recast under commerce.
- **Canned hook:** "Because the claimant is a natural person facing a residency line in a commercial livelihood, Article IV § 2 should be pleaded first: it puts the burden of a substantial, closely related justification on the State, which the dormant Commerce Clause after Pork Producers does not."

## 2. The dormant commerce clause

**Clause.** Implied from "To regulate Commerce . . . among the several States" — Art. I § 8 cl. 3 (Art.I-8.3); siblings in Art. IV § 2 and Art. I § 10 cl. 2 (Import-Export).

**Bottom line.** A State may not benefit in-state economic interests by burdening out-of-state competitors — the "very core" of the doctrine — but outside that core the dormant Commerce Clause now does very little ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]). There is no "almost per se" rule against extraterritorial effects, and a nondiscriminatory law survives unless a plaintiff plausibly pleads a substantial burden on the interstate market.

### The test

1. **Has Congress acted?** A displacing or authorizing statute ends the inquiry (*Willson v. Black-Bird Creek*; *Cooley*).
2. **Facial discrimination by origin?** Operates "like 'a tariff or customs duty'" and fails without balancing.
3. **Discriminatory purpose or practical operation?** The Pike line "serves as an important reminder that a law's practical effects may also disclose the presence of a discriminatory purpose."
4. **Price-control or price-affirmation statute?** *Baldwin*, *Brown-Forman*, *Healy* — now filed under discrimination.
5. **If nondiscriminatory, a substantial burden on the interstate market?** Not on "particular . . . firms" or "methods of operation" (*Exxon*): "That goes for pigs no less than gas stations."
6. **Only then Pike: burden "clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits"?** No majority on how.
7. **Does another clause do the work?** Article IV § 2 for citizens; Due Process and Full Faith and Credit for wholly out-of-state regulation; Congress for national disruption.

### Cases and who said what

- [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|Pork Producers]] (2023, 5–4 judgment; Gorsuch for the Court in Parts I, II, III, IV-A, V) → California may condition in-state pork sales on how pigs were confined elsewhere. "While the Constitution addresses many weighty issues, the type of pork chops California merchants may sell is not on that list." (Pork Producers, Gorsuch, three Justices: balancing noneconomic benefits is "like being asked to decide 'whether a particular line is longer than a particular rock is heavy'"); (Pork Producers, Roberts, partial dissent with Alito, Kavanaugh, Jackson: vacate and remand under Pike); (Pork Producers, Sotomayor with Kagan: keep Pike balancing, but no burden pleaded); (Pork Producers, Barrett: incommensurable, but burden pleaded).
- [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]] → the counter-case; also rejected a dormant commerce claim, citing [[Gibbons v. Ogden]].
- [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] → inspection, quarantine and health laws are reserved state powers despite "remote and considerable influence on commerce."

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine.** Antidiscrimination core plus a thin Pike threshold.
- **Off track.** The doctrine has no text: the Commerce Clause grants Congress a power and says nothing about state laws when Congress is silent. Several Justices think the principle "may be more appropriately housed elsewhere."
- **Should.** The best understanding should keep only the antidiscrimination core and house it in the Import-Export Clause or Article IV, leaving everything else to the "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause — Congress — as Pork Producers points.
- **Strongest counter.** Two centuries of practice, from *Cooley* on, and the founding-era tariffs (Rhode Island's tax on "New-England Rum") show the Constitution was adopted to stop exactly this.

### Fact triggers, oscillation and traps

- This is a dormant commerce problem when a State law burdens out-of-state firms and Congress is silent.
- **Oscillation.** Rewrite Proposition 12 to bar pork raised in Iowa and it is facial discrimination; have California produce its own pork and ban only imported crated pork, and practical effects expose protectionist purpose.
- **Trap: "Part IV is the opinion of the Court."** Only Part IV-A is; IV-B and IV-D have three votes, IV-C four.
- **Trap: "The Court killed Pike."** It did not; four Justices would have let this claim proceed.
- **Canned hook:** "Absent facial or purposeful discrimination, the law should be upheld: after Pork Producers there is no freestanding extraterritoriality rule, and compliance costs falling on out-of-state producers are not, without more, a burden on the interstate market."

## 3. Republican government

**Clause.** "The United States shall guarantee to every State in this Union a Republican Form of Government, and shall protect each of them against Invasion; and on Application of the Legislature, or of the Executive (when the Legislature cannot be convened) against domestic Violence" — Art. IV § 4 (Art.IV-4).

**Bottom line.** Enforcement of the guarantee belongs to Congress and the President, not the courts ([[Luther v. Borden]]). [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] turns the guarantee into an affirmative power: restoring a rebel State "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," and a President's wartime governments are provisional.

### The test

1. **Is the entity a State "in this Union"?** The guarantee runs only to States in the Union — which is why Lincoln's July 4, 1861 Guarantee Clause argument assumes its conclusion.
2. **Which obligation?** Republican form; invasion; domestic violence (only on the State's application).
3. **Which branch acts?** Congress recognizes a government by seating its members (Luther); the President under the militia act decides which body to answer; the wartime President may install provisional governments.
4. **Are the means necessary and proper?** Chase applies [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]], with no act "prohibited or unsanctioned by the Constitution."

### Cases and who said what

- [[Luther v. Borden]] (Taney) → "it rests with Congress to decide what government is the established one in a State"; otherwise the guarantee is "a guarantee of anarchy, and not of order." Later Guarantee Clause attacks on initiatives (*Pacific States*) and malapportionment were nonjusticiable; *Baker v. Carr* moved malapportionment to Equal Protection.
- [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] (1869, 5–3, Chase) → "an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States"; (Texas v. White, Grier, dissenting) Texas's status is "a political fact, not a legal fiction."
- [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]] → the counter-position: secession is unconstitutional, but no clause authorizes coercing a State. Distinguishing line: force against individuals resisting federal law, or restoration of a State that never left, has a source; war on a State as such is what Buchanan said none authorizes. See [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].

### Fact triggers and traps

- This is a Guarantee Clause problem when a federal actor claims power to restructure, recognize or replace a State government, or a litigant asks a court to declare a State government unrepublican.
- **Trap: "The clause gives individuals an enforceable right."** Luther routes it to the political branches.
- **Trap: "Texas v. White gives the President a Reconstruction power."** Only a provisional, commander-in-chief power; the guarantee power is Congress's.
- **Canned hook:** "Whether this government is republican is for Congress, not a court (Luther); the court should dismiss rather than decide."

## 4. Territories, citizenship, and Dred Scott

**Clause.** "The Congress shall have Power to dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States" — Art. IV § 3 cl. 2 (Art.IV-3.2). Also Art. IV § 3 cl. 1 (new States), Art. III § 2 (diversity), the Fifth Amendment, and the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.

**Bottom line.** The Territories Clause is, on its face, a grant of legislative power over federal territory, including the power to exclude slavery. [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]] held that no descendant of imported slaves could be a "citizen" and that the Missouri Compromise was void under the Fifth Amendment; Article V reversed both holdings. The case is now taught for method — originalism failing on its own terms — and for what a wrong decision binds.

### The test

1. **Who is a citizen — what did the law provide, rather than who was expected to hold it?** Curtis: "natural-born citizen" assumes birthright citizenship, and free Black men voted in five States at the ratification of the Articles, so Taney's own concession (founding-era state citizens became national citizens) answers him.
2. **Where does power to acquire territory come from?** Jefferson doubted any enumerated power covered Louisiana: "let us not make it a blank paper by construction." Candidates: the treaty power plus appropriations, or a "great substantive and independent power." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
3. **Which clause governs the territory?** Curtis and McLean: the Territories Clause is "necessarily a grant of power to legislate." Taney: the clause covers only 1787 territory; power over later acquisitions is implied from the New States Clause.
4. **Which individual-rights limits bind Congress there?** Taney's list (establishment, speech, arms, quartering) is the residue worth keeping; the contested step is substantive due process.
5. **Does the power run both ways?** Nelson: a power to abolish is a power to establish.
6. **What happens on return to a State?** *Strader v. Graham*: forum law governs; Curtis separates what the territory's law did from whether the forum recognizes it.
7. **What does the decision bind?** Lincoln: the decision binds the parties; its force "as a political rule" is another matter. See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

### Cases and who said what

- [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]] (1857, 7–2, Taney) → no citizenship; Missouri Compromise "not warranted by the Constitution, and . . . therefore void." (Dred Scott, Taney): the Constitution speaks "with the same meaning and intent with which it spoke when it came from the hands of its framers." (Dred Scott, Curtis): "Political reasons have not the requisite certainty to afford rules of juridical interpretation." (Dred Scott, Curtis — the Cuba hypothetical): if Congress may free slaves brought from Cuba under the commerce power, "how can a similar regulation respecting a Territory violate the fifth amendment?"
- [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] → the counter-case on what the decision binds. Distinguishing line: a political branch may refuse a decision's reasoning as a rule for its own votes but may not block the judgment between the parties. (Douglas): the decision "becomes the law of the land, binding on you, on me, and on every other good citizen."
- **Aftermath.** Congress banned territorial slavery in 1862; the Senate seated Hiram Revels in 1870; the Thirteenth Amendment removed the property premise; the Fourteenth's Citizenship Clause adopted Curtis's birthright rule. See [[Amendment process - Art. V]].

### Baude in class

- Taney rests citizenship on what the founders perceived, and the history cuts the other way: contemporaries, including preachers, read "all men" to include Black men. Originalism failing on its own terms.
- The Territories Clause move is a "ridiculous argument" from a textualist perspective — the clause grants Congress power over territories.
- The real hook is the Fifth Amendment due process claim; the Territories Clause discussion only bolsters it.
- Taney disclaims jurisdiction and rules anyway, passing straight through the gate Marshall built in [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]]; everything after the jurisdictional holding is advisory. Only the second federal statute the Court invalidated — the first real test of Marbury.
- Taney was pragmatic, "NOT Originalism"; Buchanan wrote to Taney to take the issue off the table.
- The dilemma: it is hard to let the Court act heroically without also letting it make bad decisions.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine.** Both holdings reversed by amendment; the territorial power is plenary, qualified by the Insular Cases.
- **Should.** The best understanding should read the Territories Clause as Curtis did — a grant to a legislature is a grant to legislate — and treat the Dred Scott merits as advisory once jurisdiction failed.
- **Strongest counter.** Taney's best reply: the territorial ruling was itself jurisdictional, since if the Compromise did not free Scott he remained a slave and could not be a citizen.

### Fact triggers, oscillation and traps

- This is a Dred Scott move when a court finds no jurisdiction and then reaches the merits, or when a political actor treats a decision as a rule binding beyond the parties.
- **Oscillation.** Lincoln's departmentalism (reasoning not binding on Congress) versus Douglas's supremacy; move from opposing the rule to blocking the decree and Lincoln's own principle condemns the actor — which is how a departmentalist distinguishes Faubus in [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]].
- **Trap: "Taney held Congress has no territorial power."** He implied the power from the New States Clause; the statute fell on due process.
- **Trap: "Originalism produced the decision."** Both opinions claim 1787; Curtis wins on legal meaning and on Taney's own concession. See [[Modes of constitutional argument]].
- **Trap: "'No rights which the white man was bound to respect' is an aside."** It is the premise of the citizenship holding, offered as history, and false as history.
- **Canned hook:** "A court that holds it lacks jurisdiction should stop; as in Dred Scott, everything after that holding is advisory (Curtis, dissenting), and Marbury's merits discussion at least invalidated nothing."

## 5. Amending the Constitution: Article V

**Clause.** "The Congress, whenever two thirds of both Houses shall deem it necessary, shall propose Amendments . . . which . . . shall be valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of this Constitution, when ratified by the Legislatures of three fourths of the several States, or by Conventions in three fourths thereof, as the one or the other Mode of Ratification may be proposed by the Congress" — Art. V (Art.V). Two entrenched provisos: the 1808 slave-trade and capitation clauses, and equal Senate suffrage.

**Bottom line.** An amendment is valid when properly proposed and ratified by three-fourths of the States by the mode Congress chose; the President plays no part. On time, [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] implies a "reasonable time" and lets Congress fix one, but the executive branch certified the Twenty-seventh Amendment 203 years after proposal on the view that Article V "contains no time limits" ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]).

### The test

1. **Validly proposed?** Two-thirds of both houses, or a convention called on two-thirds of state legislatures — never used, designed "to circumvent Congress" ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
2. **Mode designated?** Legislatures or conventions, Congress's choice ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
3. **Three-fourths ratified?** Count numerator and denominator separately (seceded States; coerced Fourteenth Amendment ratifications, which route to [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]]).
4. **Timely?** Three questions: any implied limit (Dillon yes, OLC no)? Where was any deadline — in the text or a separate resolution ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]])? May Congress set, extend or recall after proposal?
5. **Valid rescission?** After the threshold, no — "the scale-tipping ratification locks the amendment in place." Before, open (*Idaho v. Freeman*, vacated).
6. **Who certifies?** The Archivist under 1 U.S.C. § 106b; on the OLC's view, a duty that does not wait for Congress.
7. **Entrenched proviso touched?** 1808 clauses; equal Senate suffrage.
8. **Presentment?** Not required (*Hollingsworth v. Virginia*), though President Carter signed the 1978 ERA extension.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] (Van Devanter) → ratification must be "sufficiently contemporaneous . . . to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period"; Congress may set seven years. Holding narrow; the contemporaneity language is reasoning.
- [[Coleman v. Miller]] (no controlling majority; Hughes for three) → lapse of time is for Congress or a political question; called Dillon's reasoning dictum.
- [[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]] (1992) → the counter-case: "The alternative to procedural formalism is uncertainty and litigation"; giving one branch control over the Constitution's content would be "a return to parliamentary supremacy." The casebook: "This is departmentalism in action." Distinguishing line: where Congress fixed a deadline, Dillon upholds it; where it fixed none, Dillon's language is dictum.
- [[The Congressional Pay Amendment]] → crossed three-fourths May 7, 1992; Congress then voted 414–3 and 99–0 to accept it, after the Archivist had decided to certify.
- [[The Equal Rights Amendment]] → 38 States only if the deadline and five rescissions are ignored; President Biden's January 2025 statement changed nothing, because the Archivist did not certify.
- [[The Riddles of Article V]] → limited conventions, self-entrenchment (Corwin), amendment outside Article V (Ackerman, Amar): "Why go to the bother of listing the prescribed methods of amendment if they are not the prescribed methods of amendment?"

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine.** Dillon's narrow holding, Coleman's non-majority, and an executive certification no court has tested.
- **Should.** A departmentalist should side with the OLC on text — Article V names no time limit, and the 1808 proviso shows the framers wrote time rules when they meant them — while conceding Dillon's point that a consensus spread over two centuries is no identifiable people's consensus.
- **Strongest counter.** "Isn't Dillon right that there is something fishy about adding an amendment without a real consensus . . . ? But isn't the O.L.C. opinion right that Article V does not say that?"

### Fact triggers and traps

- This is an Article V problem when the facts involve a late ratification, a rescission, a deadline in a resolution rather than the text, a convention call, or a refusal to certify.
- **Trap: "Dillon held a late ratification invalid."** It held only that Congress may set a deadline.
- **Trap: "Coleman gave Congress the final say."** No majority: Hughes's opinion had three votes; Black and three others (four Justices) went further and said Congress has "sole and complete control."
- **Trap: "A court validated the Twenty-seventh Amendment."** No court has, "because Congress has not yet attempted to violate it."
- **Canned hook:** "Article V's text should control: it fixes no time limit, and where the framers wanted one they wrote it (the 1808 proviso), so Dillon's contemporaneity requirement is dictum the certifying officer need not follow (OLC Opinion)."

## 6. Supremacy and the oath: Article VI

**Clause.** "This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties made, or which shall be made, under the Authority of the United States, shall be the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby" — Art. VI cl. 2 (Art.VI-2). The oath "to support this Constitution" and the religious-test bar — Art. VI cl. 3 (Art.VI-3); the Debts Clause — Art. VI cl. 1.

**Bottom line.** Valid federal law beats conflicting state law, and a State may not prosecute a federal officer for doing what federal law authorizes ([[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]]). The Supremacy Clause is a priority rule, not a grant of power; the oath binds every officer to the Constitution and names no expositor.

### The test

1. **Which category of supreme law?** Constitution (unqualified); statutes "made in Pursuance thereof"; treaties "under the Authority of the United States." Executive agreements are not listed ([[A Note on Executive Agreements]]).
2. **Priority or scope?** "[A]ny Thing . . . to the Contrary notwithstanding" decides conflicts; how large federal law is depends on the enumerated power ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]).
3. **Who is bound?** The Supremacy Clause names state judges; the oath binds federal and state legislators and officers; the President's oath is in Art. II § 1.
4. **For executive action, the Neagle chain:** habeas statute → marshal's sheriff-like powers → Attorney General's letter → Take Care duty. Each link is weak.
5. **For the oath, whose reading?** The officer's own — route to [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]]. The religious-test bar reaches federal office only.

### Cases and who said what

- [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]] (1890, 6–2, Miller; Lamar with Fuller dissenting; Field not participating) → "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law'"; there is "a peace of the United States." (Neagle, Lamar): "[n]o officer . . . is placed . . . above responsibility to the legal tribunals of the country."
- [[A Note on Executive Agreements]] → the counter-case. Distinguishing line: authority traced to the Constitution, a statute or a treaty displaces state law; a bare presidential agreement does not unless Congress authorized it (*Dames & Moore*; *Medellín*).
- [[Martin v. Hunter's Lessee]] (1816) → the Supreme Court may review state supreme courts in Article III cases; the Supremacy Clause's "Judges in every State" is the strongest text. See [[Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause]].
- [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] → uses "the Judges in every State" to distinguish *Testa v. Katt* (Printz, Stevens: the oath binds state executives too). See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- [[Article VI - other materials]] → the oath as the seed of departmentalism ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]: "as he understands it"); [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] reads it the other way.
- **Professor-scholarship hook.** Baude and Paulsen, "The Sweep and Force of Section Three," 172 U. Pa. L. Rev. 605 (2024): the Fourteenth Amendment § 3 disqualification keys on a prior oath "to support the Constitution of the United States" — the Article VI oath — and on their reading operates of its own force. Paulsen co-authors the casebook.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Off track.** Neagle reads Take Care as a source of power where Congress was silent — a [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] twilight-zone case ([[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]]) — while "Laws . . . made in Pursuance thereof" describes the product of a procedure, Lamar's reading.
- **Should.** The best understanding should accept Neagle's result through the originalist route — executive power at common law included keeping the peace, preserved by the marshal-as-sheriff statute — while rejecting Miller's open-ended "inferrible" obligation.
- **Strongest counter.** Without Neagle the government is "without the means of self-protection," and an officer forced to stand trial in a hostile state court is deterred from his duty.

### Fact triggers and traps

- This is a supremacy problem when a State prosecutes, taxes or regulates a federal officer or instrumentality, or when a presidential agreement is said to override state law.
- **Trap: "The Supremacy Clause grants federal power."** It is a priority rule.
- **Trap: "The Article VI oath binds the President."** His oath is in Art. II § 1.
- **Canned hook:** "The Supremacy Clause decides which law wins, not how far federal power reaches; the officer is protected only if his act traces to a federal 'law' — here, as in Neagle, a duty fairly inferrible from the Constitution — and Lamar's 'in Pursuance thereof' objection is the strongest reply."

# Part VIII — The Q3 Bank: Normative and Synthesis Questions

## 1. The Q3 template

Every Baude Q3 has been a normative essay with no fact pattern, worth 20–30 points:

- **2018:** "Which of these [ten federalism] cases would you overrule" to make the law "better justified as a matter of constitutional interpretation" and "much more coherent and consistent"?
- **2021:** "What insights do you get from looking at the cases [in chronological order], rather than divided up by doctrine or topic? Are there connections you see that we did not focus on much in class?"
- **2023:** "Write one amendment to the Constitution… give the exact text," then "What are the most significant ambiguities in your proposed amendment, and how might later interpreters disagree about them?"

**The shape of a top answer**

1. **Thesis in sentence one, as a standalone sentence.** "I would overrule Wickard and Garcia and nothing else."
2. **State the criterion.** The prompt usually supplies two (2018: justified *and* coherent). Say how they are weighed against each other.
3. **Numbered moves,** each tied to a named case, Justice, or episode, each ending in a verdict.
4. **The strongest counter, named to a holder** (Ginsburg in NFIB; Stevens in Printz; Sotomayor in Slaughter; Rakove on liquidation), then a weight judgment.
5. **Tie-break with a default rule** where considerations split: stare decisis, reliance, or "when in doubt, follow the text."
6. **Restate the thesis in the last sentence.**

**Budget:** at 30 words per point, a 25-point Q3 is about 750 words. That is three numbered moves of 150–180 words, a 100-word counter, and 60 words of thesis and close.

## 2. Pack A — "Which cases would you overrule?"

### A1. The 2018 federalism set

The set: [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]], [[Gibbons v. Ogden]], [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]], [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]], *Heart of Atlanta Motel*, [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]], [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]], [[United States v. Comstock]], [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]], [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]].

**Thesis (recommended): overrule Wickard; keep Raich only as Justice Scalia justified it; keep everything else.**

1. **Keep McCulloch and Gibbons as the foundation.** Every modern limit is quarried from McCulloch: legitimate end, "plainly adapted" means, "letter and spirit," and the "great substantive and independent power" that cannot be implied (NFIB, Roberts; Baude, *Rethinking the Federal Eminent Domain Power*). Gibbons supplies the definition of commerce as "intercourse" and the plenary-within-its-sphere rule. Overruling either destroys the vocabulary every other case uses.
2. **Keep Darby, split in two.** The shipment ban is the Commerce Clause itself — the power to regulate includes the power to prohibit crossing the line, and motive is "for the legislative judgment" (Darby, Stone). The wage-and-hour half reaches production through McCulloch's means-end logic. That second half is correct only as a Necessary and Proper argument, which is how the modern Court re-grounds it.
3. **Overrule Wickard.** Aggregation with no market tether turns "substantially affects" into a power with no stopping point — Framing A, "commerce alone," where magnitude is the only screen and aggregation removes even that. Wickard also releases the pretext check Marshall reserved in McCulloch. Without Wickard, the remaining cases cohere: Lopez (no economic activity, no scheme), Raich (a means to make a comprehensive interstate-market regulation effective — Scalia's concurrence), and NFIB ("proper").
4. **Keep Raich on Scalia's ground, not Stevens's.** The home-grown marijuana ban is a necessary and proper means to the end of an interstate-market prohibition, because home-grown supply is fungible with and leaks into that market. On Stevens's aggregation ground Raich falls with Wickard; on Scalia's it survives.
5. **Keep Comstock.** Breyer's five considerations are McCulloch applied to a custodial regime Congress already runs; Alito's concurrence supplies the tether: the power is not "carte blanche," and the link to an enumerated power must not be too attenuated.
6. **Keep Garcia and Printz together.** They answer different questions: Garcia, whether Congress may subject States to laws that also bind private parties; Printz, whether Congress may command state officers as officers. The private-actor-parity line reconciles them. Ground Printz in "proper" and political accountability, not in Scalia's Article II argument — Scalia accepts voluntary cooperative programs that bypass presidential control just as completely.
7. **Heart of Atlanta survives** as a channels case: motels serving interstate travelers are channels and instrumentalities, with no aggregation needed.

**Counter (reliance and the regulatory state).** Overruling Wickard threatens the agricultural, labor, and environmental statutes built on it (Raich, Stevens; Darby's means-end reasoning). **Answer:** most of that edifice regulates economic activity within comprehensive interstate schemes and survives on Framing B; what falls is regulation of local non-economic conduct with no market tether, which Lopez already forbids. Reliance is weak for a rationale the Court has already narrowed.

**Alternative thesis (pure coherence, less justification): overrule Lopez.** If the criterion is coherence alone, Wickard and Raich (Stevens) form a consistent line and Lopez is the outlier. Baude rewards picking a side; this side is defensible but has to concede that it leaves no judicially enforceable limit on the commerce power — Justice Thomas's objection in Lopez.

### A2. The separation-of-powers analogue (predicted)

Plausible set: [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]], [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]], [[Myers v. United States|Myers]], [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]], [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]], [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]], [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]], [[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]], [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]], [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]], [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]].

**Thesis: overrule Morrison and narrow Trump v. United States; keep Slaughter, and justify Cook only on liquidation grounds — or concede that Cook is the outlier.**

1. **Morrison is the functionalist outlier.** Slaughter's test ("no ifs, ands, or quasis") cannot coexist with a for-cause prosecutor exercising core law-enforcement power (Morrison, Scalia dissenting). Keeping Morrison keeps a second, incompatible theory alive.
2. **Slaughter is right on liquidation.** [[The Decision of 1789]] satisfies all three liquidation elements on the narrow point that removal belongs to the President and not the Senate, and the Court quotes Madison's "liquidate & settle." The independent-agency practice from 1887 onward never produced acquiescence: Presidents contested it from Roosevelt's firing of Humphrey onward. "No branch may rely on adverse possession" (Slaughter, Roberts).
3. **Cook is coherent only as liquidation.** If the First Congress that decided 1789 chartered an insulated Bank in 1791, then 1789 and 1791 are one liquidation, and the Fed inherits it. If the Banks were private and "possessed no sovereign power" (Cook, Thomas dissenting), Cook is policy — and Baude's own pre-decision proposal (keep Humphrey's for the Fed under stare decisis discretion) was the more candid route.
4. **Narrow Trump v. United States to the power, not the motive.** Absolute immunity for the "conclusive and preclusive" core should protect the act (removal, pardon), not the purpose; the evidentiary bar should go (Trump v. United States, Barrett concurring). Slaughter enlarges the set of officers the President may remove at will, so an immunity keyed to the removal power reaches far more conduct than Nixon-era doctrine contemplated.
5. **Keep Chadha and Youngstown** as the formalist and functional anchors, read together: Chadha supplies the "legislative in character" test; Jackson's categories supply the decision procedure when the President acts with or against Congress.

**Counter.** Sotomayor (Slaughter dissent): 1789 chose a default, not a rule; ninety years of reliance built the modern state; Hamilton's "displace" in Federalist 77. **Weight:** the default-rule reading is the strongest point against Slaughter, because Madison's own coalition in 1789 was split across three theories. Answer: liquidation does not need agreement on theory, only settlement of the result — and the result settled was presidential removal.

## 3. Pack B — The chronology question

Use the chronology in Part X. The answer is a thesis about time, then four or five insights that cross doctrinal lines.

**Thesis (recommended):** "Read chronologically, the course is a story of settlement by practice, unsettlement by crisis, and resettlement by the Court — and the Court increasingly claims to be enforcing the first settlement against the second."

1. **The First Congress was the great liquidator.** 1789 (removal), 1791 (Bank), 1791 (post roads), 1792–93 (the Justices' refusal to advise) — in three years the political branches and the Justices settled removal, implied powers, delegation, and advisory opinions. Slaughter and Cook in 2026 both turn on 1789–91.
2. **1803–05 was a single settlement of judicial independence,** not three cases: [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] accepted the repeal of the Judiciary Act, [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]] claimed judicial review while denying the remedy, and [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] established that judges are not impeached for their rulings. The Court bought independence by acquiescing in the Republicans' structural move.
3. **Crisis Presidents make the hard precedents.** Lincoln 1861–63 ([[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Merryman]], [[The Prize Cases (1863)|Prize Cases]], [[The Emancipation Proclamation]]); Truman 1952 (Youngstown); Nixon 1973–74 (War Powers veto, [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]]). Practice made in emergency is the weakest candidate for liquidation, because acquiescence is not deliberate.
4. **1937 is a single hinge across every doctrine.** Court packing ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]), the commerce turn ([[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]]), the spending cases ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis]], [[Helvering v. Davis]]), and [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] arrive together. The Senate defeated the plan; the Court changed anyway.
5. **Formalism returns in waves** — 1976–83 (Buckley, Chadha), 1995–97 (Lopez, Printz), 2010–26 (Free Enterprise Fund, NFIB, Seila Law, Slaughter) — and each wave is justified by founding-era history rather than by the intervening precedent.
6. **The same Justices write both halves of structure.** Roberts writes NFIB (limits on Congress) and Trump v. United States and Slaughter (expansions of the President). The connection not stressed in class: a Court that shrinks Congress's enumerated powers and expands the President's removal and immunity is not neutral between branches — or, on the other view, is restoring a single original allocation.

## 4. Pack C — "Write one amendment"

Each entry: exact text, why, what it accomplishes, and the ambiguities (the 5-point part B).

### C1. The Presidential Accountability Amendment (recommended — richest doctrine, cleanest ambiguities)

> **Section 1.** No person shall be immune from criminal prosecution for any act undertaken while serving as President, except as provided in this article.
> **Section 2.** A sitting President shall not be arrested or brought to trial; the running of any period of limitation shall be suspended while the President holds office.
> **Section 3.** Congress may by law protect the confidentiality of communications between the President and officers of the United States, but no such protection shall bar evidence in a criminal trial on a showing of particularized need.

- **Why:** [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] created absolute immunity for "conclusive and preclusive" acts and presumptive immunity for other official acts, plus an evidentiary bar. Trump v. United States already treats removal as core, conclusive and preclusive power; [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]] enlarges the set of officers that core power reaches to every principal officer exercising executive power (save the Fed and the categories Slaughter reserved). Impeachment is the only remaining check, and the Impeachment Judgment Clause (Art.I-3.7) already contemplates trial "according to Law" after conviction.
- **Accomplishes:** restores the pre-2024 assumption (the dissent's hypothetical — "order Seal Team 6" — gets an answer), keeps [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]]'s balancing for privilege, and keeps the sitting-President rule from the OLC opinions.
- **Ambiguities:** (1) Does Section 1 make every federal statute apply to the President, or only those that a clear-statement rule reaches? (2) Can the President self-pardon, or pardon co-conspirators, to defeat Section 1 — is the Pardon Clause (Art.II-2.1) amended by implication? (3) Does "act undertaken while serving" include acts within the "conclusive and preclusive" core, such as a removal, or only prosecutions for motive? (4) "Particularized need" imports Nixon's standard but leaves its content to courts. (5) Does "brought to trial" bar indictment of a sitting President?

### C2. The Independent Agency Amendment

> **Section 1.** Congress may by law provide that the members of a board or commission of not fewer than three members, no more than a bare majority of whom shall belong to the same political party, shall be removable by the President only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office.
> **Section 2.** A removal under this article shall be reviewable by the courts of the United States.

- **Why:** Slaughter overruled [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's]]; [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]] saved only the Fed, on a contested history. If independence is valuable, Article V — not a reading of 1791 — should supply it.
- **Ambiguities:** (1) May Congress give such a board rulemaking and enforcement power, or does the amendment authorize only Humphrey's-style "quasi" bodies? (2) Does it permit a second layer of protection below the board ([[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]])? (3) Is "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance" the 1935 statutory list — which Cook called "too stringent" — or the common-law "cause"? (4) Does Section 2 commit cause to courts de novo or deferentially? (5) Does the amendment by negative implication confirm that single-headed agencies are always at will?

### C3. The Supreme Court Terms Amendment

> **Section 1.** The Supreme Court shall consist of nine Justices. Each Justice shall serve a single term of eighteen years, after which the Justice shall continue in office as a judge of an inferior court.
> **Section 2.** One term shall begin on the first day of July of each odd-numbered year. If the Senate does not consent to a nomination within one hundred twenty days of its receipt, the President may appoint the nominee.

- **Why:** fixes the size the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] fought over, removes the incentive for strategic retirement, and makes each President's influence predictable — a structural answer to the court-packing, impeachment, and jurisdiction-stripping pressure covered in Part IV § 4.
- **Ambiguities:** (1) Is a Justice who has rotated off still an Article III judge "during good Behaviour," and may that judge be recalled to the Supreme Court? (2) How do sitting Justices transition — does the amendment apply to them? (3) Does Section 2's deemed consent displace the Senate's power to reject, or only to delay? (4) Is "nine" a floor and ceiling, preventing Congress from adding seats, or only the regular complement? (5) What happens on death mid-term — does the successor serve the remainder or a full term?

### C4. The Article V Amendment (the dead-hand answer)

> **Section 1.** An amendment proposed by two-thirds of both Houses shall be valid when ratified by the legislatures of three-fifths of the several States, or by a majority of voters in a national election held for that purpose in which a majority of voters in three-fifths of the States also approve.
> **Section 2.** A proposed amendment shall be inoperative unless ratified within ten years of its submission to the States.

- **Why:** the dead-hand problem is sharpest because Article V is the hardest amendment process in the world; lowering the threshold makes the Constitution's legitimacy rest on living consent. Section 2 resolves the [[The Congressional Pay Amendment|Twenty-Seventh Amendment]] and [[The Equal Rights Amendment|ERA]] fights over time limits that [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] and [[Coleman v. Miller]] left to Congress.
- **Ambiguities:** (1) May a State rescind a ratification before the threshold is reached? (2) Does the ten-year limit apply to amendments already pending (the ERA)? (3) Is the national-vote route subject to the Equal Suffrage proviso, which bars depriving a State of equal Senate suffrage without its consent? (4) Who certifies the result — the Archivist, Congress, or courts — and is that a political question? (5) Can this amendment be used to amend itself back?

**Other seeds (one line each):** a War Powers Amendment (Congress must authorize hostilities beyond 60 days; ambiguity: "hostilities"); a General Welfare Amendment (spending conditions must relate to the grant's purpose; ambiguity: "relate"); a Nondelegation Amendment (Congress must enact rules that impose private liability; ambiguity: "rules"); an Anti-Commandeering Amendment (codifying Printz; ambiguity: generally applicable laws).

## 5. Pack D — Predicted 2026 Q3 prompts

Each pack: thesis, three moves, steelman counter, dismissal.

### D1. "Slaughter and Cook were decided the same day. Are they consistent?"

- **Thesis:** They are consistent only if historical practice can create an exception to a textual rule, and if it can, Slaughter's rejection of ninety years of agency practice needs a principle that distinguishes 1791 from 1935.
- **Moves:** (1) Liquidation supplies the principle: 1791 was deliberate, contemporaneous with 1789, and acquiesced in; 1935 onward was contested by Presidents throughout. (2) Thomas's dissent in Cook is the strongest objection: the Banks were private, and "possessed no sovereign power." (3) Baude's pre-decision proposal — keep Humphrey's for the Fed alone under stare decisis — would have been more candid about the pragmatic stakes.
- **Counter:** Federalist 70's unity argument applies to a seven-member Board as much as to a five-member FTC.
- **Dismissal:** unity is a default the text sets; liquidation is the mechanism for settling where the text is indeterminate, and the Fed is the one place the First Congress itself insulated an institution.

### D2. "Who should settle what the Constitution means — the Court, practice, or each branch?"

- **Thesis:** Each branch should interpret for itself; judgments bind; and practice that satisfies liquidation's three elements should bind all three.
- **Moves:** (1) [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]] grounds judicial review in the duty to decide a case, which supports judgment-supremacy, not opinion-supremacy. (2) [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]]'s claim that the Court's interpretation is "the supreme law of the land" overreads Marbury; Lincoln on [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] shows why. (3) Madison's Bank reversal shows practice binding a dissenter who still thought the original view right.
- **Counter:** departmentalism invites defiance ([[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Merryman]]; Little Rock).
- **Dismissal:** Baude's judgment rule answers it — a judgment within jurisdiction must be enforced, so departmentalism never licenses ignoring a court order, only declining to treat the reasoning as law for new cases.

### D3. "Was there a federalism revolution?"

- **Thesis:** A revolution in method, not in results: Lopez, Printz, and NFIB moved the limits from the Commerce Clause's magnitude test to Necessary and Proper's "proper," but Congress's regulatory reach is nearly unchanged.
- **Moves:** (1) Lopez and Morrison invalidated statutes with jurisdictional-hook fixes. (2) Raich showed that any comprehensive scheme can reach local conduct. (3) NFIB's real bite was the spending holding, the first coercion ruling ever.
- **Counter:** NFIB's commerce holding forecloses mandates, a real constraint.
- **Dismissal:** the tax holding gave the same mandate back; the limit is a drafting rule.

### D4. "Should Congress be able to discipline the Court?"

- **Thesis:** Congress may shape the Court's jurisdiction and size, but may not impeach Justices for their rulings or strip jurisdiction to dictate outcomes.
- **Moves:** (1) [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] liquidated "no impeachment for rulings." (2) [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|McCardle]] permits withdrawing jurisdiction, but *Klein* forbids prescribing a rule of decision in a pending case. (3) The [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] treats size changes aimed at outcomes as an attack on independence, even though size is set by statute.
- **Counter:** the Exceptions Clause is plenary on its face.
- **Dismissal:** "exceptions" presupposes a rule; an exception that swallows appellate review entirely is not an exception (Hart's dialogue, as known).

### D5. "Which doctrine is most off track?"

- **Recommended pick: nondelegation**, because the text is clearest (Art.I-1 "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress") and the doctrine has been least enforced (two invalidations, both in 1935). Five Justices have signalled interest ([[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]]: Gorsuch, Roberts, Thomas dissenting; Alito concurring; Kavanaugh later). Counter: [[The Post Roads Debate]] and [[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]] show the First Congress delegating broadly. Dismissal: the 1790s delegations concerned matters already within executive or proprietary power — distribution of benefits, post-route details — not rules of private conduct.
- **Alternative pick: the spending power**, because "general welfare" has been abandoned as a substantive limit — "I think it's a mistake that the Court has basically given up on reading 'general welfare' as a substantive requirement" is the exemplar register.

### Insights from reading chronologically

- **The First Congress liquidated two questions at once, and Cook reads them together.** The same body made [[The Decision of 1789]] and chartered the Bank in 1791. [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]] treats 1789 as settling removal; [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]] treats 1791 as founding a tradition of insulation. Either the First Congress was inconsistent, or 1789 settled less than Slaughter says.
- **1803–05 is one settlement, not three events.** [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]] claimed review without an order, [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] accepted the Repeal Act, and the Senate refused to convict Chase. The Court got review; the political branches got control of the courts' structure; neither took the other's core.
- **The Bank is the course's best experiment in practice.** Constitutionality settled by 1816 (Madison) and judicially intact the whole time ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]); President Jackson killed the institution in 1832–36, but his constitutional view never became settled, so Baude does not count it as liquidation the other way.
- **Lincoln's 1861–63 trio tests every branch's limits under pressure.** [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Merryman]] (suspension and defiance), [[The Prize Cases (1863)|Prize Cases]] (war without declaration), [[The Emancipation Proclamation]] (war power over property). Youngstown later builds the framework the Civil War left implicit.
- **1937 is the hinge for four doctrines in one year.** Commerce ([[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]]), spending ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward]], [[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering]]), court packing ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]), and standing ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Levitt]]). The Court yielded on federal power and tightened access to itself; the Senate defended the Court's independence in the same year the Court gave up its limits on Congress.
- **Formalism returns in waves, each aimed at a different actor.** 1976–83 (Buckley, Chadha) polices Congress; 1995–97 (Lopez, Printz) polices federal power over States; 2010–26 (Free Enterprise Fund, Seila Law, Slaughter) polices limits on the President. Each wave left the delegations that made agencies powerful largely intact ([[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]]).
- **2024–26 makes the President the most formally protected actor in the course.** [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] immunizes "conclusive and preclusive" acts; Slaughter enlarges the set of officers that core removal power reaches (save the Fed, limited-duty inferior officers, and the reserved adjudicatory bodies). Impeachment, appropriations ([[Trump v. IRS settlement agreement]]) and elections are the checks left.
- **Taxing is the escape valve each time commerce narrows.** Hammer (1918) is followed by Bailey (1922); Lopez and NFIB's commerce holding are followed by NFIB's tax holding. Expect the tax question whenever a fact pattern blocks the commerce route.

## Justice cheat sheet

- **Marshall** — (McCulloch) "a constitution we are expounding," "power to tax involves the power to destroy," same result "were the question entirely new"; (Marbury) "province and duty"; (Gibbons) commerce is intercourse, the power "complete in itself."
- **Story** — (Martin v. Hunter's Lessee) federal review of state courts; cited in Slaughter with Marshall and Kent as treating removal as settled.
- **Paterson** — (Stuart v. Laird) practice and acquiescence "irresistible"; (Hylton) direct taxes narrow, a third class of indirect taxes.
- **Taney** — (Dred Scott) the framers' meaning is fixed; (Merryman) only Congress may suspend; (Luther v. Borden) Congress decides which state government is lawful.
- **Curtis** — (Dred Scott diss.) "political reasons" are not rules of interpretation; the Cuba hypothetical.
- **Chase (Salmon P.)** — (McCardle) no inquiry into legislative motive; (Texas v. White) indestructible Union.
- **Grier** — (Prize Cases) the President meets war "in the shape it presented itself"; (Texas v. White diss.) statehood is "a political fact."
- **Miller** — (Neagle) "law" includes obligations inferrible from the Constitution.
- **Holmes** — (Hammer diss.) "regulation means the prohibition of something."
- **Taft** — (Myers) removal belongs to the President; (Bailey) "a court must be blind not to see" a penalty; (J.W. Hampton) intelligible principle.
- **Sutherland** — (Humphrey's) quasi-legislative, quasi-judicial agencies may be protected; (Mellon) no taxpayer standing.
- **Hughes** — (Jones & Laughlin) close and substantial relation; (Coleman) Article V timing is political.
- **Stone** — (Darby) motive is for Congress; the Tenth Amendment "a truism."
- **Jackson (Robert)** — (Youngstown conc.) three categories, "conclusive and preclusive," emergency powers "kindle emergencies"; (Wickard) aggregation, restraints are political.
- **Frankfurter** — (Youngstown conc.) "the gloss which life has written"; (Lovett conc.) attainder "defined by history."
- **Black** — (Youngstown) power from a statute or the Constitution, nowhere else; (Lovett) the pay cutoff is an attainder.
- **Douglas (William O.)** — (Youngstown conc.) a seizure is a taking only Congress can fund. Not Stephen Douglas of the 1858 debates.
- **Vinson** — (Youngstown diss.) the President holds the status quo until Congress acts.
- **Burger** — (Chadha) the Framers ranked other values above efficiency; (United States v. Nixon) privilege yields to demonstrated need.
- **White** — (Chadha diss.) the legislative veto is "an important if not indispensable political invention"; (Buckley) joined the strict appointments holding.
- **Powell** — (Chadha conc.) the House acted as a court; (Garcia diss.) the States' role is constitutional law, not legislative grace.
- **Blackmun** — (Garcia) process, not result.
- **Brennan** — (Dole diss.) the Twenty-first Amendment bars the condition.
- **Rehnquist** — (Lopez) three categories, economic activity; (Dole) mild encouragement.
- **O'Connor** — (Raich diss.) Lopez becomes a "drafting guide"; (Dole diss.) the condition regulates drinking; (Garcia diss.) "letter and spirit"; (Printz conc.) voluntary reporting left open; (New York) no commanding legislatures.
- **Scalia** — (Raich conc.) substantial effects runs through N&P; (Printz) no commandeering, Article II accountability; (Morrison diss.) "this wolf comes as a wolf"; (Clinton diss.) "not a dime's worth of difference"; (Noel Canning conc.) practice cannot override text.
- **Kennedy** — (Lopez conc.) the New Deal cases stand; (Clinton conc.) Congress cannot yield its powers.
- **Stevens** — (Raich) economics is production, distribution, consumption; (Clinton) cancellation is repeal; (Printz diss.) the oath binds state executives, and commandeering serves the States.
- **Breyer** — (Lopez diss.) cumulative effects; (Printz diss.) foreign federal systems; (Clinton diss.); (Comstock) multi-link means; (Noel Canning) post-founding practice counts.
- **Thomas** — (Lopez conc.) commerce is "selling, buying, and bartering"; (Raich diss.) personal medical use is separable; (Seila Law) overrule Humphrey's; (Cook diss.) the Banks had no sovereign power, so the majority's ground is policy; (Trump v. United States) doubts the Special Counsel's appointment.
- **Ginsburg** — (NFIB) forgoing insurance is not "doing nothing"; "proper" bars only commandeering; "You will know it when you see it"; Medicaid is "a single program."
- **Roberts** — (NFIB) no compelled commerce, mandate not "proper," valid tax, "gun to the head"; (Free Enterprise Fund, Seila Law, Slaughter) removal formalism; (Cook) the Fed exception rests on history; (Trump v. United States) core-power immunity.
- **Alito** — (Gundy conc.) the fifth vote that keeps the intelligible principle alive; (Cook diss., with Gorsuch) would not rest on the statute; with Barrett, objected to deciding the Article II question on a stay application; (Pork Producers) joined Roberts's partial dissent.
- **Gorsuch** — (Gundy diss.) Congress "passed the potato"; (Slaughter conc.) the President now wields delegated legislative and judicial power; (Pork Producers) "the type of pork chops . . . is not on that list."
- **Kavanaugh** — (Cook) removal for cause remains open; did not sit in Gundy.
- **Barrett** — (Trump v. United States) frames the issue as an as-applied challenge; (Cook diss.); (Pork Producers) incommensurable benefits, but a burden was pleaded.
- **Kagan** — (Gundy plur.) strict nondelegation would condemn most of government; joined the Cook majority and the Slaughter dissent.
- **Sotomayor** — (Slaughter diss.) 1789 chose a default, and reliance counts; (Trump v. United States diss.) "a king above the law"; joined the Cook majority.
- **Jackson (Ketanji Brown)** — joined the Slaughter dissent and the Cook majority; joined Roberts's partial dissent in Pork Producers.
