# Baude — exam writing profile

Built from both courses he has set for Ashwin: Con Law I 2018/2021/2023 (five
circulated answers) and Elements 2019/2021/2024 (seven circulated answers).
Twelve exemplars in total, which makes this the best-evidenced professor in the
vault.

## Mechanics

Three questions, 100 points. **Weights move every year** — 30/50/20, then
50/20/30, then 45/30/25 — at a constant **35 words per point**.

## Exam-day materials — confirmed in office hours

Asked directly whether it is closed-book with no text at all, his answer was no:

> "(1) will get one sheet of paper, front and back, (2) Might get a full copy of the constitution"

Two consequences for the one-pager. **Do not spend lines on constitutional
text** — if the Constitution is supplied, every clause you transcribe is a
wasted line, and if it is not, the clauses you need are the handful you should
know cold anyway. Spend the sheet on closed lists that are not in the
Constitution: the Youngstown categories with the conclusive-and-preclusive
carve-outs, *Dole*'s conditions plus coercion, the *Casey* and *Ramos* factor
sets, Lopez's three categories, and case-plus-Justice pairings.

This year's syllabus changes the constraint:

> "your grade for this course will be based on an **in-class, closed-book exam**."

Every circulated Con Law answer was written over eight hours with the book open
and, from 2021, the internet. The craft transfers; the time and lookup budget
does not.

Four instructions do real work.

> "**Each question has a word limit. I won't read additional words.**"

> "My advice is to sketch an outline to each of the questions before you actually begin to write. If you just dive in, you may get lost halfway through and you may run out of space."

> "**If there are additional facts you need to know, please say what they are and how they would affect your analysis.** And of course when explaining your answers be sure to explain your reasoning. (**Don't assume that I'll know you know the basics; show me!**)"

> "Grades will be based on your understanding, analysis, and clarity of exposition (so please proofread)."

Every exam closes: "REMINDER: Did you check your word count for each question?"

The syllabus states what is being tested:

> "One is what the Supreme Court has said the Constitution means – i.e., the doctrine. The other is what the Constitution actually requires – i.e., places the doctrine may be off track. **Both of these things will be tested on the exam.**"

## Question archetypes

**A role is assigned almost every time, and it is institutional** — White House
lawyer, Senate Judiciary staffer, district clerk, adviser to a governor. Never a
private client, never damages, never a remedy.

**Q3 is a normative reform essay every single year**, worth 20–30 points, with
no fact pattern at all:

- 2018: "Which of these cases would you overrule in order to do that? Explain."
- 2021: "What insights do you get from looking at the cases this way, rather than divided up by doctrine or topic?"
- 2023: "Write one amendment to the Constitution that you would recommend to improve it. **Please give the exact text of the Amendment you would want**" — plus "What are the most significant ambiguities in your proposed amendment, and how might later interpreters disagree about them?"

**Q1 and Q2 are reliably structure-and-federalism.** The tax-power-vs-penalty
question (*Bailey* / *NFIB* / *Veazie Bank*) appears in 2018 Q1, 2021 Q1 and
2023 Q2 — three for three. [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]
anchors two of the three Q1s.

**The most distinctive instruction in either corpus**, and the clearest statement
of what the course is:

> "First (20 points), is this bill constitutional under current Supreme Court doctrine? Second (30 points), **proceeding as a departmentalist**, is the bill constitutional under the best understanding of the Constitution that the President should adopt? (The President has not thought carefully about constitutional interpretation, so **you must both tell the President how the Constitution should be interpreted, and how that interpretation applies to the statute**.)"

State an interpretive methodology, defend it, then apply it — and it is worth
more points than the doctrinal analysis.

## Opening moves

Across roughly thirty answer units in the Elements exemplars, **twenty-eight open
with the bottom line in sentence one.** Three make it a standalone one-sentence
paragraph:

> "The Court should stand by the Standard Oil interpretation."
> "The Court of Appeals should not overturn Riggs."
> "Courts should interpret treaties similarly to how they interpret statutes."

The Con Law answers do the same thing in a drier register — first sentence is a
legal proposition with the authority attached, often disposing of a threshold
issue in under twenty words:

> "Bicameralism and presentment requirements are met (Art.I-7)."
> "Assuming all the plaintiffs have suffered an injury in fact, they have standing to sue (Levitt)."
> "Assuming Biden's lawyers/advisors did a good job, we're not in a Youngstown Category 1 situation because the President has no statutory authority."

**Zero of fourteen Con Law openings restate the question.** The richest variant
states the holding, names the factors, and pre-scores them before any analysis:

> "I believe that the judge should not allow Professor Vector to present this evidence to the jury. There are three main considerations here: whether the evidence is directly related to the case in question, whether the evidence is specific, and whether the probabilities involved are significant and large. The evidence is not directly related to the case, but it appears to be both specific and large. Altogether, I think that this suggests that the evidence will be more prejudicial than probative."

Six of seven Elements exemplars restate the thesis in the final sentence.

## Structure

**Mirror the prompt's own labels at the top level, then invent a sub-taxonomy.**
2023 Q1 is `A.` / `B.` / `C.` because the prompt is; inside B, both circulated
answers independently converted the prompt's verbs into headings — `Can?` /
`Should?` and `Could` / `Should`. Converting the prompt's verbs into headings is
the single most reproducible structural habit in the set.

2023 Q2's headings are the prompt's five locations in the prompt's own order —
Post Office · Federal Courts · Train Stations · State Court · School — with one
answer adding `Tax?` at the front and `Entire Regulation?` at the back.

2021 Q1 builds a two-level mirror of the prompt's 20/30 split: `1. Current
Doctrine` / `2. Ideal Doctrine`, with the same four issues listed under each.
The parallel structure is itself the argument.

**Run-in bold topic labels** are the most space-efficient structure in the
corpus — navigational benefit of a heading at the cost of two words:
"Branch:", "Commissioners Appointments:", "Removal:", each introducing a 60–150
word block.

**Paragraphs are short** — two to five sentences, 40–90 words. Sections between
headings run as short as fourteen and twenty words:

> "**Removal Power** — I'd want to do more background reading, but this doctrine seems fine to me."
> "Same as State Courts, except replace DOJ with the Department of Education. It's also not responsible for collecting revenue."

Cross-reference instead of repeating. Cover a listed issue in fourteen words and
signal it needs no more.

**Numbered and enumerated lists are used freely** — the opposite of Buccola:

> "This raises the following three questions: (I) Can Congress pack the Court?; (II) Can Congress impeach the Justices for this kind of behavior?; and (III) If so, would these actions be 'legislative'?"

> "the GFSPT is not a tax under Congress's taxing-powers: (1) The cost of the tax is probably high enough to be a 'prohibitory financial punishment'; (2) there's no scienter requirement; (3) the 'tax' is collected by other agencies."

Three answers print their own word counts at the end of each question — "WC:
1500" — which is a visible compliance signal against "I won't read additional
words."

## Authority

**Statutory sections: effectively zero.** Constitutional clause cites instead, in
a compressed house format — `Art.I-7`, `Art.II-2.2`, `Art.I-9.7` — up to
seventeen of them in 3,500 words.

**Case names are bare, parenthetical, and stacked**, and the signature form is
**case plus Justice**, which cites a position *within* a case in two words:

> (Raich, O'Connor) · (Gundy, Kagan) · (Seila Law, Thomas) · (Youngstown, Jackson) · (NFIB, dissent)

**Named Justices do argumentative work.** Six to thirteen per script. The move is
"Justice X would say Y, but":

> "Scalia would argue the IP test is an unmanageable one, but likely allow this delegaton because it is one of carrying out the law."
> "The Morrison majority would argue that they are inferior officers… But I think Edmond controls here."

Counting votes is rewarded: "unclear whether that holding had 5 votes to make it
binding."

**Non-case authority sits in the same parenthetical slot as cases** — (Madison,
Veto Message), (Dellinger Memo), (Decision of 1789), (Federalist 49), (B&C 278),
"as Professor Hills noted." The 2021 Q3 prompt lists legal episodes alongside
cases, so non-judicial authority is formally on the syllabus.

In Elements the equivalent currency is **named theorists as compressed
positions**, one every two to three sentences:

> "Per Easterbrook, we should only look to legislative history to elucidate the meaning of the text when it is genuinely ambiguous. Otherwise, we should prioritize the text because the intent of a collective body is fictive."

Canon labels always carry a gloss, never naked Latin.

**Caution.** The densest quotation in the corpus — 24 quoted strings per 1,000
words — came from an eight-hour open-book exam with internet. The *form* is
reproducible closed-book (short clause-length quotes, woven into the sentence,
never block-quoted); the *volume* is not. The clause cites and the
case-plus-Justice parentheticals are the cheap, high-yield half.

## Commitment

**"Should" is the workhorse** — nineteen of twenty-six verdict hits in the
highest-commitment script, which is what you get when the prompt asks "What
should she say?" and the answer replies in kind. Verdicts land as one-sentence
recommendations:

> "The House should impeach and the Senate should convict."
> "The judge should find it unconstitutional."
> "Raich: Overturn!"

**Ranking arguments by strength is a distinct, repeated, graded move:**

> "California's strongest challenge is based on the 'ambiguity' factor."
> "There's a strong argument that a train station is a 'channel or instrumentality of interstate commerce' (Lopez)… There's a slightly weaker theory is that this is a valid commerce clause regulation under Raich."
> "It's shakier, but she should uphold this provision"

**Counterarguments are attributed to a named holder and always answered**, often
with an explicit weight judgment: "This is a fairly weak argument, however" ·
"On the other hand, and more convincingly" · "I think this argument is quite
strenuous."

**Hedges attach to the argument, never to the writer's own verb.** Soft modals
run 5.7–14.2 per 1,000 words in the Elements exemplars — he does not reward
sparse hedging, he rewards hedging in the right grammatical place. "Arguably"
is nearly absent; "on the other hand" appears in one exemplar only.

**Where factors genuinely split, break the tie with a default rule** rather than
shrugging:

> "These 4 considerations could support both a decision to uphold or overturn Loper Bright… However, because Loper Bright is a permissible reading of the APA, I would recommend that they honor the principle of stare decisis and uphold the precedent."

**Missing facts are named as conditionals that would flip the result, and then
the answer is given anyway.** Never "we cannot say."

> "However that would change with more information about the tribes well-being or whale and seal population numbers."
> "[I'm assuming this is a departmentalist judge who views OLC memos as precedential.]"

**The register is first-person and irreverent, and arguing the Court is wrong is
graded content:**

> "I think it's a mistake that the Court has basically given up on reading 'general welfare' as a substantive requirement"
> "When the court talks about 'legislative power,' it talks out of both sides of its mouth—and maybe also a nostril."
> "The Janus-faced invocation of Chadha here… is admittedly incoherent, but no more so than the rest of the Court's administrative law jurisprudence."

## Budgeting

**Cap utilisation is near-total: 98.1–100.0% on fifteen of fifteen questions
across five scripts, seven of them exactly at cap.** Nobody leaves words on the
table and nobody goes over. Within multi-part questions, allocation tracks the
internal point split — 2023 Q3 is 20/5 points and runs 586/162 words.

This year's exam is in-class and the syllabus does not state word limits, so the
numbers may not recur, but the discipline — read the weights off the page,
budget from them, spend all of it — transfers to any timed exam. Ashwin already
executes this on Elements at 98/99.7/99.2%.

## What is not graded

Worth knowing, because it is visible and cheap to over-invest in. The circulated
Elements exemplars contain misspelled case names ("Hivey" for *Hively*,
"Sardras" for *Zardra*, "exclusio unius"), one answer that stops mid-clause with
no concluding sentence, and no memo formatting at all where the prompt said
"write him a short memo." Length is not the variable either: 2,485 to 4,210
words on the same exam, both circulated.

**The position is free; the defence of it is not.** On 2019 Q2 the exemplars
split don't-overrule / overrule / don't-overrule. On 2024 Q3, admit / exclude /
exclude. There is no right answer being rewarded.

## Where this cuts against Ashwin's habits

- **The thesis must survive sentence two.** Elements Q1A 2025 opened "should be bound by 9th circuit precedent" and then "Instead of following the decision from ChatGPT…" Twenty-eight of thirty exemplar units put a single clean holding in sentence one.
- **Headings are missing on two of three questions.** Elements Q2 and Q3 2025 ran 748 and 1,042 words with no internal structure; every exemplar at least mirrors the prompt's labels.
- **Element-marching earns here** — the opposite of Buccola. "Don't assume that I'll know you know the basics; show me!"
- **Grade the counterargument.** The weight-judgment move is everywhere in the exemplars and absent from the 2025 answer.
- **Name the missing fact** — he asks for it in terms, and it is free points.
