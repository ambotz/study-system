## Synthesis — six things that run through all 26

### 1. The question is almost always *who decides*, not what the clause means

This is the single most repeated move of the course. Youngstown asks what
Congress has said, not what Article II means. Departmentalism asks whose reading
governs. Standing asks whether anyone may ask at all. Jurisdiction stripping
asks whether Congress may remove the forum. Impeachment is a political question
and no court supervises it. When a question looks like interpretation, check
first whether he has actually posed an allocation problem — and say so, because
that framing is usually the answer.

### 2. Standing is the universal off-ramp, and he accepts the dead end

Sessions 5, 8, 14 and 21 all end there. The appropriations power may be
unenforceable; nobody had standing over the unreported CIA budget; non-enforcement
of the TikTok ban injures nobody in particular. His stated position is that
sometimes no one has standing, and he does not strain around it. The instinct to
manufacture a plaintiff so you can reach the merits is answering a question he
did not ask. Where the honest answer is that the constraint is political rather
than judicial, say that.

### 3. Practice is the contested mode, and liquidation is its test

He ranked text and structure above practice from Youngstown onward — a thing
having happened before does not make it lawful — then told you to dig deep on
liquidation. The test has two conditions and both must be argued: the practice
must be **reasoned on constitutionality** and **repeated or settled**. Any
argument that runs on "this is how it has always been done" has to clear them.
Frankfurter's narrower version is the fallback: systematic, unbroken, known to
Congress, never questioned.

### 4. Keep the three argument types separate

Policy argument; constitutional argument; constitutional argument a court will
accept. He said you will be asked to make them separately and that the way
answers go bad is inconsistency. Track which one you are making. There is no
right answer and there are many wrong ones.

### 5. The questions he posed and left open are the exam

He rarely withholds an answer by accident. Collected, these are hypo-shaped:

- Do Gorsuch's three categories of permissible delegation differ from the intelligible principle at all?
- May Congress delegate outside the executive branch — to a private body?
- Is a tax on inactivity a new kind of direct tax, because it cannot be escaped?
- Where between oral argument and judgment does the jurisdiction-stripping line fall?
- Is the administrative state a fourth branch the Constitution does not allow?
- Granting the President may direct the Attorney General, may he use that power for any purpose? (The dissent's Seal Team Six hypothetical drew no majority response.)
- Can the two removal theories — unitary executive and congressional control — meet in the middle? He marked this one as exam material in terms.

### 6. He teaches contradictions as contradictions

Do not resolve these; name them and argue from the tension.

- **Cooper v. Aaron** declares judicial supremacy flatly; **Jackson's bank veto** reads the same oath the opposite way.
- **Marbury** is strategically modest — power asserted in dicta, relief denied; **Dred Scott** disclaims jurisdiction and rules anyway, passing straight through Marshall's gate.
- **Garcia** says the States' remedy is the political process; **Printz** says the Court is the safeguard. Buccola's counterpart move, but Baude does it here too.
- **Emergency recurs and never confers power** — Youngstown, the Prize Cases, Merryman, habeas. Repelling an attack is a carve-out; martial law is a step beyond it.

## Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care

`3/high` · Art. II § 3 — the Take Care Clause, prosecutorial discretion and the abdication limit

**Baseline.** The President must enforce the laws and may not suspend a statute or excuse people from it in advance, but the executive chooses whom to prosecute and how to spend limited resources, and courts do not second-guess those choices.

**Gloss.** Baude reads Take Care as conferring discretion, and works Cox's four opinions as a spectrum — Jones that signing cannot be compelled, the dissent that the case be brought so the declination is public, Brown's middle position of prepare-but-not-sign, Wisdom's strong discretion with a federalism overlay. Adams supplies the limits: statutory mandate, general policy of non-enforcement, or appropriated resources misused under the guise of discretion. Office hours sharpened two things. The baseline expectation is that a law will not be fully enforced, which is a separate baseline from the presumption that it is constitutional — so a non-enforcement hypo starts from under-enforcement being normal, and the argument is about what makes this instance different. And the practical fix of writing 'you must enforce this' into a statute is not much of a limiting principle, which is the same line-drawing problem that produced admin law. Still ask his two questions: is a case-by-case backdoor left open, and is the President claiming unconstitutionality or mere policy disagreement. Expect standing, not the merits, to be the real obstacle — on marijuana non-enforcement he said an Adams challenge is clearly available as a general policy, and the whole problem is who could bring it.

**Traps.**

- **"Cox has one holding."** There were two different 4–3 majorities: signing cannot be compelled, drafting can, and the order was reversed only because it required both. Getting the split wrong is the first cold-call trap.
- **"Heckler v. Chaney holds that Article II confers absolute non-enforcement power."** [[Heckler v. Chaney]] is an APA presumption about reviewability, not a constitutional holding, and its footnote 4 preserves the Adams exception rather than burying it.
- **"The Supreme Court has held wholesale non-enforcement unconstitutional."** [[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]] is a court of appeals decision on a funding program; the Supreme Court has repeatedly declined either to adopt or to reject the abdication exception.

## Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle

`3/high` · Art. I § 1 — the vesting of legislative power and the intelligible-principle test for delegation

**Baseline.** Congress may give the executive broad discretion in carrying out a statute, but it may not hand over the power to make the law itself.

**Gloss.** Baude framed nondelegation as a line-drawing problem: Congress cannot delegate legislative power, but the post roads debate sets up a Zeno's paradox, so the question is only where the line falls, and he gave three ways to draw it — policy, historical context, practice. On Gundy he posed rather than answered whether Gorsuch's categories differ from the intelligible principle; in office hours he supplied the answer, and it is the distinction to lead with: Gorsuch's objection is not that the AG decides whether the law applies, which would be ordinary prosecutorial discretion, but that he decides what the law is, which is a step above it. Two further points from office hours. A delegation holding is constitutional precedent, not statutory, so Congress cannot fix an improper-delegation ruling by legislating; only a later Court can, and it will do so by applying the intelligible-principle test differently rather than by changing the statute's meaning. And admin law largely absorbs nondelegation, because a statute broad enough to raise the question is usually held to supply an intelligible principle anyway — with Loper Bright pulling the other way by giving courts the meaning over the agency's.

**Traps.**

- **"Gundy reaffirmed the intelligible-principle test."** Only four Justices applied and endorsed the test; three would replace it, Alito concurred only in the judgment and endorsed neither analysis, and Kavanaugh did not sit. The test survived by default, not by a majority opinion.
- **Reciting the intelligible principle and stopping.** The plurality spends most of its opinion on statutory interpretation, because the constitutional answer follows from the construction; an answer that never narrows the statute skips the step that decides most cases.
- **Treating the 1935 cases as a live general doctrine.** They are the only two invalidations, in one year, and the Schechter statute let private trade groups write codes with the President's discretion "virtually unfettered." Sunstein's frame: "one good year, and 211 bad ones (and counting)."

→ [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]

## Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories

`3/high` · Art. II § 1 — the Vesting Clause and the Youngstown categories of presidential power

**Baseline.** The President needs authority from a statute or from the Constitution itself, and when he relies on the Constitution alone, the strength of his claim depends on what Congress has said.

**Gloss.** Baude used the Civil War materials to probe where the Youngstown framework stops working. The Prize Cases are distinguishable from Youngstown on two facts he named — Congress was out of session so its position is genuinely unknown rather than contrary, and the war was domestic rather than in a foreign theatre, which is Black's line. The Emancipation Proclamation he puts squarely back inside Youngstown because it is a seizure of property on land. So in an emergency hypo, resist the pull toward category three: establish first whether Congress has actually spoken, and if it has not, argue the zone of twilight and let the domestic-or-foreign character of the action do the rest of the work. He also pressed the limit on his own framing — martial law is a step beyond repelling an attack, and the Proclamation reached only the South, so a war power justified by hostilities does not obviously extend to places where there are none.

**Traps.**

- **"Youngstown holds that the President was at his lowest ebb."** Black's test is whether the act is legislative in character; Jackson's test is what Congress has said. Name the opinion you are applying.
- **"Congress did not authorize it, so the President is at his lowest ebb."** Silence is category two. Category three requires an express or implied denial: a rejected proposal, an occupied field of inconsistent schemes, or a prohibition. [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]], [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]] and [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] are silence cases, and the executive won all three.
- **"Youngstown denies the President emergency power."** The Court held only that this seizure, against Congress's expressed will, was unlawful. It did not deny emergency power in general or all domestic reach of the Commander in Chief Clause, and Frankfurter reserved the short, self-terminating seizure.

## Modes of constitutional argument

`3/high` · No single governing provision — the five types of constitutional argument, liquidation, and the problem of ranking the modes

**Baseline.** Constitutional arguments come in five types (text, historical context, structure, precedent and practice, and consequences), and a good answer uses several, then says which one controls ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).

**Gloss.** Baude introduced the five modes and immediately ranked them against Youngstown, putting text and structure above practice; the ranking, not the list, is what he does with them. His exam instruction sharpens this into three argument types to keep separate and make separately — policy, constitutional, and constitutional a court will accept — and his grading view is that there is no right answer but many bad ones, the way answers go bad being inconsistency. On structure, office hours closed a tempting move: you cannot argue that Article I outranks the Bill of Rights because it comes first, nor that amendments matter less for being amendments; the available flip is that a later amendment clarified or shed light on the original meaning. Liquidation is the piece he told you to dig deep on — practice settles ambiguous meaning if it is reasoned on constitutionality and repeated or settled, and he calls it one step stronger than custom, the move from norm to law. Precedent is a subset of practice, being the courts' own. The Bank is the worked example of both directions: Madison liquidated it as constitutional, and Jackson's veto did not liquidate the other way, because it never became settled consensus.

**Traps.**

- **Listing the five types and stopping.** The casebook's questions are about ranking, whether some types draw content from others, and whether some apply only when others run out. Name the controlling type and say why.
- **Treating "practice" as one idea.** Liquidation claims a practice fixed what the Constitution *means*; historical gloss claims only that long, unbroken practice known to Congress deserves weight; ordinary precedent claims neither. [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]'s twelve years of founding-era circuit riding and the eighty years of withdrawals in *United States v. Midwest Oil* are different kinds of evidence.
- **Citing McCulloch for the proposition that practice settled the Bank question.** Marshall says he would reach the same result "were the question entirely new," so practice is a makeweight there. [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] is the authority for practice as decisive, and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] is the evidence that the settlement did not hold.

→ [[Modes of constitutional argument]]

## Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies

`3/high` · Art. III § 2 — cases and controversies, the injury-traceability-redressability test, and the timing doctrines

**Baseline.** A federal court may decide a constitutional question only while resolving an actual dispute brought by a plaintiff with something personal at stake.

**Gloss.** Baude's own position leads: where a court has jurisdiction its judgment binds the parties, and everything beyond that is not binding. Standing is jurisdictional, so no standing means no merits, and the order is particular injury then relief — Levitt failed because nobody could show the appointment operated on them, and Buckley shows the fix, a pending case in which the challenged power is exercised over you. Mellon closes the shortcuts: no taxpayer standing, and a State needs its own theory; the one orthodox exception is that a taxpayer challenging a tax he pays has standing, and on an illegal tax everyone it is collected from does. Office hours added the modern doctrinal hook worth naming: where the theory of standing rests on a complicated chain of causation the Court is more skeptical if you are not the party directly regulated — Diamond Alternative Energy and Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine — and a traceability failure often gets restated as a failure of injury in fact, so a fanciful chain makes the injury look less particularised. Where the honest answer is that nobody has standing, say so and say the constraint is political.

**Traps.**

- **"Levitt held Justice Black's appointment lawful."** The Court said nothing about Art. I, § 6, cl. 2. A dismissal for want of standing decides only who may ask; the question moved to the President, the Senate and Black's own oath.
- **"Standing and political question are the same bar."** They are separate: a plaintiff with a concrete injury can still lose on political-question grounds, even though [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] calls the State's abstract claim "political, and not judicial in character."
- **"No concrete injury" as the whole answer.** Frothingham fails injury, traceability and redressability, and the timing doctrines can defeat a suit independently; run every element.

## Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances

`3/high` · Art. I § 1, Art. II § 1 and Art. III § 1 — the vesting clauses, the asymmetry of "herein granted," and the system of checks

**Baseline.** Each branch may exercise only the power the Constitution gives it, and one branch cannot take over another's power even with that branch's consent.

**Gloss.** Youngstown is the anchor and Baude flagged it for full command: the executive must cite an authority for every action, statutory or constitutional, with no residual power, and past practice does not supply one where the practice was wrong — Frankfurter narrows the practices that could ever count to those emergency, temporary and congressionally permitted, and Douglas reaches the same result through the Takings Clause, since only Congress can compensate. The vesting clauses are the textual engine of the other side: Article II vests 'the executive Power' without Article I's 'herein granted', and says 'a President' where Article III contemplates inferior officers. Against that sits Article I's power to establish offices, which on the congressional-control reading carries appointment and removal terms with it. His own rule is the one to apply first: the energy-and-accountability argument only works where Congress has not spoken, so establish Congress's position before choosing a theory. On whether the administrative state is a fourth branch, his answer is that the Constitution does not allow one — so the move is to re-characterise the body into an existing branch and test it there, which is exactly what Humphrey's did by calling the FTC quasi-legislative.

**Traps.**

- **"Youngstown held that the President was at his lowest ebb."** Black wrote for the Court and never used the categories; the three categories are Jackson's concurrence. Name the opinion you are applying.
- **"Both branches agreed, so the arrangement is valid."** [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] and [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] struck schemes the President and Congress had accepted. Jackson's category one enlarges the President's executive discretion; it cannot give him a share in the § 7 lawmaking procedure, which is why Breyer's reliance on category one failed in Clinton.
- **"Clinton forbids the President to decline to spend."** [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] held only that a statutory power to "cancel" enacted provisions, depriving them of "legal force or effect," violates the Presentment Clause, and did not reach delegation. Scalia's "not a dime's worth of difference" between cancellation and discretionary spending shows the rule turns on drafting.

## State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment

`3/high` · U.S. Const. amend. X — the anti-commandeering rule, the political safeguards, and the spending workaround

**Baseline.** Congress may regulate a State on the same terms as private parties, but it may not order a State's legislature to legislate or its officers to administer a federal program.

**Gloss.** Buccola aside, this is Baude's densest office-hours topic. The commandeering line is at means: Congress may regulate States like private parties and may regulate private parties directly, but using state machinery as the means fails the 'proper' half of Necessary and Proper. Scalia's Article II argument leads — Congress cannot conscript state officers to escape federal enforcement — but office hours demoted it: accountability is doing more work than Article II, which is why cooperative federalism is completely fine and he told you not to try to challenge it, since everyone can still point to state officials and hold them accountable. Nondelegation also does not reach delegation to States, on Gorsuch's own Gundy analogy that the delegation just returns power the recipient started with; the national-parks example is the illustration. On Garcia's political safeguards, his answer is narrow — States are better represented than individuals, purely — and the honest critique is that substantive due process has fallen as federalism protection has risen, with Blackmun himself having written Roe. The strongest sympathetic version is that Congress leaves vast powers unused, so the hypothetical limits matter less than they look; the strongest answer against is minority States and pre-constitutional sovereignty.

**Traps.**

- **"The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not expressly delegated."** The word "expressly" is not in the Amendment. Day inserted it in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]], Marshall had already rejected it in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]], and [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] made the Amendment a truism.
- **"Garcia was overruled."** It never was. [[New York v. United States]] and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] route around [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] by separating laws that regulate States alongside private parties from laws that command States to govern.
- **"Anti-commandeering immunizes a State from federal burdens."** A State gets no relief from a wage law or a data-sale ban that also binds private parties. The rule forbids ordering a State to govern, and it is not preemption either: forbidding States to *authorize* sports betting is a direct order (*Murphy v. NCAA*), but regulating private actors and displacing contrary state law is not.

## Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3

`3/high` · Art. I § 8 cl. 3 — the three categories, the substantial-effects test and its limits

**Baseline.** Congress may regulate commerce that crosses state lines, and local activity whose regulation is needed to reach that commerce, but not every activity with some economic ripple. [[United States v.

**Gloss.** Lopez supplies three categories — channels, instrumentalities, substantial effects — and only the third requires economic activity, which is why a train-robbery statute survives under channels with no economic nexus. Read Lopez as building the test first and applying it to guns second; Raich then loosens it by treating economic activity as a class of activities, making the case a framing contest over how large the scheme is, with Scalia reaching substantial effects through Necessary and Proper. His standing instruction is to level-set which prong you are in and which case supplies it, and these federalism cases do not overrule each other, so the stack is cumulative. NFIB adds the activity limit, where Ginsburg answers that everyone enters the healthcare market eventually and Baude's own reply to the broccoli parable is that Congress might compel the purchase but not the eating. Office hours supplied the test case for how far aggregation reaches — under Raich, Congress could ban slavery on substantial effects in the aggregate; under Lopez alone probably not, because the activity is not purely economic. And on Rehnquist's point that § 922(q) is unrelated to any larger scheme, his answer is that the statute need only allude to commerce within a larger regulation, which is how motive-irrelevance survives.

**Traps.**

- **"The activity substantially affects commerce, so the law is valid."** After [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]] the first question is whether the activity is economic; the effects inquiry is reached only for economic activity or for a noneconomic measure embedded in a market scheme.
- **"Wickard lets Congress reach anything with an economic ripple."** Filburn grew a commodity under a statute regulating the volume of that commodity in commerce, and [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]] conceded that "there is no decision of this Court that such activities may be regulated where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce"; the holding rests on substitution plus aggregation.
- **"Filburn ate all 239 bushels."** The opinion says "[t]he intended disposition of the crop here involved has not been expressly stated"; the point is that the Act reached the excess wheat regardless of use.

## Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession

`3/high` · U.S. Const. pmbl. and art. IV § 3 — the constituent power, compact theory, and the unlawfulness of secession

**Baseline.** No State may leave the Union by its own act, because the Constitution rests on the people rather than on the States as parties to a revocable compact.

**Gloss.** Baude laid out four readings of 'we the People' and marked two of them wrong, which is unusual enough to be worth carrying: Jefferson, that the people means the States of the people with no federal body, is wrong; Wilson, that it means the nation with no real State conventions, is equally wrong; Madison's quasi position and Marshall's, that it means States plus people with the federal government supreme only within its limited powers, are the live ones. Marshall's version is the usable formulation because it builds the limit into the supremacy claim. He also closed the Bank arc with liquidation and flagged it as exam material: practice over time settles meaning, which is why Jackson's veto did not unsettle the Bank's constitutionality and Madison turned out to be right by the mechanism he had himself described.

**Traps.**

- **"Texas v. White is a purely nationalist case."** The "indestructible States" half is quoted more often for the States than against them, and it underlies [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] and the dissents in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]: the States' separate existence, not just their reserved powers, is constitutionally protected. The phrase confers no power on a State, however: the same opinion suspends Texas's membership rights and gives restoration to Congress.
- **"'Absolutely null' voids everything the rebel government did."** Applied literally it voids marriages, deeds, judgments and tax collections; the workable rule is *Horn v. Lockhart*'s, and a bona fide purchaser of bearer bonds has no place in Chase's theory.
- **"Buchanan, Lincoln and Davis are answering the same question."** [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] holds secession unlawful *and* coercion unlawful, coherent only if the sole response is war on a State as such. Lincoln never answers the coercion question; he denies it arises, which works only because the seceding States seized federal property. Had South Carolina let the customs house operate and the mails run, Lincoln would have had nothing to execute.

→ [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]]

## Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7

`3/high` · Art. I § 7 — the finely wrought procedure, the legislative veto and the line-item veto

**Baseline.** A law can be made, changed, or repealed only by the same text passing both Houses and going to the President.

**Gloss.** Baude's organising question was what counts as legislative versus executive power, and his answer is procedural: the House and the Attorney General were doing functionally the same thing in Chadha, so the difference lies in how the decision was reached. Legislative power is whatever must pass bicameralism and presentment; executive power is the residue. Clinton turns on the same line — the Item Veto Act let the President change a bill's text, which is not execution, even though declining to spend is. So when a branch reaches a defensible result by a doubtful route, do not argue about the result: identify the procedure used, ask whether that procedure is the one Article I § 7 requires for an act of this character, and look for the two tells he gave — an action that amends the law's operation, and an action fixating on named individuals, which is administration.

**Traps.**

- **"The defect in Chadha was that only one House acted."** Presentment is an independent requirement, so a two-House concurrent resolution fails too; that is why § 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution, removal of forces "if the Congress so directs by concurrent resolution," is void.
- **Running the argument straight off cl. 3.** By its terms cl. 3 covers only votes "to which the Concurrence of the Senate and House . . . may be necessary," and a one-House veto requires no concurrence. The textual case depends on first classifying the action as legislative under § 1 and cl. 2, which is where White's original-meaning reply lives.
- **"Both branches agreed, so the scheme is valid."** Agreement saved neither the legislative veto, which Presidents accepted "as the price for a broad delegation of authority," nor the Line Item Veto Act, which Congress passed and the President signed. [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] category one can enlarge the President's executive discretion but cannot give him a share in the § 7 process.

→ [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]]

## Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II

`3/high` · Art. II § 1 — executive privilege for presidential communications and criminal immunity for official acts

**Baseline.** The Constitution gives the President no express immunity or privilege, but the Court infers both from the structure of Article II.

**Gloss.** Baude maps Trump v. United States as a core with an undefined perimeter: directing the Attorney General is core and absolutely immune, talking with the Vice President presumptively immune, everything else unclarified — which is where a hypo sits. The question he pressed is the one the majority does not answer: granting the President may direct a cabinet officer, may he use that power for any purpose. He set out the Court's two defences with counters attached — immunity runs only to the President though he may pardon everyone else, and it is meant to stop the DOJ being weaponised against predecessors though the decision itself stopped a prosecution — and left the dissent's Seal Team Six hypothetical standing with no majority response. Office hours added the reading that makes Roberts coherent: the consequentialist worry about successive prosecutions is forward-looking rather than backward-looking, and 'energy' attaches to the current office rather than to what a future office may do. Argue from the purpose-versus-power gap and the unanswered hypothetical, not from the core, which is conceded.

**Traps.**

- **"Nixon holds that the President can be prosecuted."** [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]] decided a subpoena for evidence in seven other defendants' trial; the President was an unindicted coconspirator, and whether he could be prosecuted stayed open until 2024.
- **"Trump gives absolute immunity for all official acts."** That was Trump's argument, and the Court rejected it; absolute immunity attaches only to conclusive and preclusive powers, and other official acts get presumptive immunity with the burden on the Government.
- **"The act was unofficial because it was illegal or corruptly motivated."** Both moves are foreclosed: courts "may not inquire into the President's motives," and an alleged violation of a generally applicable law does not make an act unofficial.

→ [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]]

## Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes

`3/high` · Art. I § 8 cl. 1 — apportionment of direct taxes, uniformity, and the tax/penalty line

**Baseline.** Congress may tax almost anything, so long as the exaction is really a tax and is laid in the required form.

**Gloss.** Baude separates the two limits by mischief: apportionment guards against a uniform tax with disparate impact, uniformity against a disparately targeted statute. He said in terms that he really likes the NFIB opinion, which makes its sequence the template — commerce, then necessary and proper, then tax as the back-up reached by constitutional avoidance. On the tax step run Bailey's four factors (prohibitory rate, scienter, regulating agency, prescribed code of conduct) while remembering he calls Bailey very ambiguous. If it is a tax, ask whether it is direct: income under Pollock, capitation and land under Hylton, and Roberts escapes because the payment fits no established category. Office hours gave the clean framing of the Hylton–Pollock pair: both start from apportionment being hard and weird, and reach opposite conclusions — Hylton says therefore call it not a direct tax, Pollock says therefore the tax fails. Use that symmetry rather than treating the cases as irreconcilable. His open question is whether a tax on inactivity is a new kind of direct tax because it cannot be escaped, and his aside is that Congress could have avoided everything by calling it an income tax.

**Traps.**

- **"The Sixteenth Amendment ended the direct-tax question."** The Amendment exempts only taxes "on incomes, from whatever source derived," so a wealth tax still runs into Hylton, Pollock and the open question in *Moore v. United States*.
- **"Congress's label decides whether it is a tax."** The label controls the Anti-Injunction Act and not constitutional power: Bailey holds that a thing called a tax is a penalty, and NFIB holds that a thing called a penalty is a tax.
- **"Apportionment and uniformity are the same requirement."** A uniform tax has the same rate everywhere; an apportioned tax has the same amount per person everywhere. Only a capitation satisfies both, which is why classifying a tax as direct is usually fatal to it and why NFIB had to show the payment was not a capitation.

→ [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]]

## War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief

`3/high` · Art. I § 8 cl. 11 — declaring war, the Commander in Chief, and the repel-attacks carve-out

**Baseline.** Congress decides whether the nation goes to war; the President, as Commander in Chief, conducts a war once begun and may use force without prior authorization to repel attacks.

**Gloss.** Baude's structural move is that the Commander in Chief Clause had to be written down at all, which cuts against an inherent military executive — if the power came with Article II there would be no need to name it. The operative distinction is Madison's, read out of the framing debates: the change from 'make' to 'declare' war leaves the President an inherent power to repel attacks but not to initiate, so the question in any hostilities hypo is which side of repel-versus-initiate the facts sit on, and Lincoln's blockade is the model of the former. On the War Powers Resolution he splits the two provisions and you should too: § 5(c)'s concurrent-resolution mechanism is unconstitutional under Chadha and presentment, which everyone accepts, while § 5(b)'s sixty-day clock is only unconstitutional if the President has authority to continue hostilities indefinitely without Congress — so argue the premise, not the provision.

**Traps.**

- **"The Prize Cases approve unilateral presidential war."** Grier says the opposite: "He has no power to initiate or declare a war." The holding is a power to respond to force already used against the nation.
- **"Only a formal declaration authorizes war."** The Convention placed the decision in Congress, not a form of words; only five wars have been formally declared, and a specific statutory authorization is Congress's decision.
- **"Continued appropriations authorize the war."** WPR § 8(a)(1) forbids the inference, the Kosovo supplemental is the standard trap fact, and the Declare War Clause would do no work beyond the Appropriations Clause if funding counted.

→ [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]]

## Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors

`3/high` · Art. II § 4 — "high Crimes and Misdemeanors" and whether an impeachable offense must be an indictable crime

**Baseline.** The President, the Vice President and civil officers can be removed only by impeachment in the House and conviction by two thirds of the Senate for "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors." Practice, not any court, has fixed the core of the standard: the Convention struck "maladministration" because "[s]o vague a term . . . will be equivalent to a tenure during pleasure of the Senate," and the acquittals of [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase|Justice Chase]] (1805) and [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson|President Johnson]] (1868) put policy disagreement and good-faith legal error outside it.

**Gloss.** Baude fixes 'high Crimes and Misdemeanors' between two poles: it need not be an indictable offence — it is a term of art from English practice meaning abuse of office or violation of public trust — but it must be more than unsatisfactory performance or policy disagreement, which is what the Chase acquittal establishes ex post. Johnson supplies the paradigm, the concrete ground being removal of the Secretary of War, with the irony that Slaughter now makes that removal lawful. Justiciability closes the judicial route: impeachment is a political question, so the real constraint is the Senate's two-thirds. Use that bar comparatively — he ranked the three responses to judicial disagreement as impeachment (textual, highest bar), jurisdiction stripping (narrow, surgical), and court packing (no text at all), and the two-thirds requirement is what makes impeachment the sound-but-unavailable option while the other two need only a majority.

**Traps.**

- **"Johnson's second impeachment shows that an indictable crime is required."** The House needed a statute to get past its own 1867 failure but still lost in the Senate; the Chase managers argued the opposite and the Senate never resolved the principle; and Article X against Johnson charged nothing but "intemperate, inflammatory, and scandalous harangues."
- **"Madison's deletion of 'maladministration' proves a statutory crime is needed."** The deletion shows only that the standard is narrower than bad government, lest tenure become "during pleasure of the Senate"; it says nothing about whether the misconduct must be indictable.
- **"A President must be impeached and convicted before he can be prosecuted."** [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] rejected that reading: the Clause "does not address whether and on what conduct a President may be prosecuted if he was never impeached and convicted."

→ [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]]

## The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations

`3/high` · Art. I § 9 cl. 7 — the Appropriations Clause, funding conditions and riders, and who may enforce the purse

**Baseline.** No federal money may be spent unless a statute appropriates it: "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." Congress may therefore fund, refuse to fund, or fund on conditions, and a condition the executive thinks unconstitutional does not free the executive to spend, because voiding a restriction creates no appropriation.

**Gloss.** Baude treats the purse as a real check with an internal limit: Congress cannot use appropriations to accomplish what another clause forbids, which is Lovett, and the same end-run principle blocks curtailing a war through funding. Running the other way he took Scalia's position in Clinton that the President must spend what Congress mandates, with impoundment discretion surviving only where the mandate is not absolute. Office hours gave the two conflicting themes in their sharpest form, and he said to hold both: the purse is Congress's ultimate check on the executive, and Congress cannot use spending to do what the Constitution otherwise forbids. The live fight is a defunded office — Obama's response to § 2262 was effectively 'I am going to keep the roles', which is either a Burwell violation, since voiding a restriction creates no appropriation, or a Take Care defence, plus the President's argument that Congress cannot be so specific as to stop him executing the laws. Note what actually decides it: the executive treated the rider as unconstitutional, which works under departmentalism and fails under judicial supremacy — so route the answer through that choice rather than through the Appropriations Clause alone.

**Traps.**

- **"Congress's control of the purse is unlimited."** [[United States v. Lovett (1946)]] holds that punishment "through the instrumentality of an Act specifically cutting off the pay of certain named individuals" is "no less galling or effective than if it had been done by an Act which designated the conduct as criminal."
- **"An unconstitutional rider frees the President to spend."** Voiding a restriction creates no appropriation, so for a pure funding denial the President's only remedy is not to spend ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]], Note 2).
- **Reading [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] as a purse case.** Clinton protects the § 7 procedure; Scalia's impoundment history and Stevens's concession leave the President's statutory discretion to decline to spend intact. See [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].

→ [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]]

## Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18

`3/high` · Art. I § 8 cl. 18 — implied powers, the meaning of "necessary and proper", and the limits on incidental means

**Baseline.** Congress may choose any reasonable means of carrying out its enumerated powers, but a means must serve those powers rather than amount to a new power of its own.

**Gloss.** Baude marked McCulloch as an exam item in the form of an instruction: enumerate each participant's view and say how Marshall answered it — necessary is not indispensable, because where there are two ways neither is indispensable yet both are necessary; the Bank is incidental, which concedes Madison's framing and disputes the application, with the post office as the classic response to Madison's reply that a corporation is an end; and necessary is still not everything convenient, because the end must be legitimate, meaning itself constitutional. The operative limit is pretext, which is the hook to press against Wickard. NFIB is where that limit finally bites and the move is worth memorising: the mandate fails not because it is unnecessary but because it is too great to be incidental and so must be an independent power, with Roberts adding that it is too affirmative to be incidental in the Printz sense since people cannot be commandeered. Ginsburg's attack is the counter to have ready — that there is no workable line between what is incidental and what is too much.

**Traps.**

- **"McCulloch means deference, full stop."** The opinion also contains the two limits that decide modern cases — "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution" and the pretext reservation — and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] both quarry [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] for a limit rather than for deference.
- **"'Necessary and proper' is a single idea."** Marshall read "proper" as softening "necessary," but the cases that strike laws down give "proper" independent force; an answer that never separates the two words cannot reach the holdings of Printz or NFIB.
- **"The Tenth Amendment is the limit."** It "states but a truism" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]), and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] says that "[w]hat destroys the dissent's Necessary and Proper Clause argument . . . is not the Tenth Amendment but the Necessary and Proper Clause itself."

→ [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]]

## Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line

`3/high` · Art. II § 2 cl. 2 — the officer/employee line, principal and inferior officers, and who may appoint

**Baseline.** Every officer of the United States must be appointed by the President with the Senate's consent or, for an inferior officer, by the President alone, a court of law or a department head if Congress so provides; Congress may create an office but never fill it.

**Gloss.** Baude runs the officer line off Buckley's 'significant authority' test and ties it back to Chadha: the question is a threshold of power, not a job title, and not everyone exercising federal functions crosses it. He pressed the structural puzzle that Federalist 76 presents appointment as a presidential choice checked by the Senate, yet inferior officers can be placed beyond the President's appointment entirely. On the congressionally appointed officer he set up, the distinction to draw is between Congress prescribing who may serve and Congress choosing who serves — a qualification like 'learned in the law' narrows the President's field and is valid, while naming the appointee transfers the choice and is not — and severability is the right second move, since the Act survives with only the appointment mechanism struck. Where the officer is odd, his working answer from the removal class transfers: ask who appointed the officer's superior.

**Traps.**

- **"Congress may appoint inferior officers."** It may not. The alternative list is "the President alone, . . . the Courts of Law, or . . . the Heads of Departments," and Congress and its officers appear nowhere in the Clause for any officer, principal or inferior.
- **"Congress's label decides where the agency sits."** [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]] ignored the statute's placement of the FEC in the legislative branch and looked to the authority exercised. Be ready to say why that method is hard to square with [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]], which accepted Congress's description of the FTC as performing duties "as a legislative or as a judicial aid."
- **"Appointment and removal rise and fall together."** Congress may neither fill an office nor fire its holder, but a limit on the President's own removal power is a separate question with a separate answer; see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

→ [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]]

## Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size

`3/high` · Art. III § 2 cl. 2 — the Exceptions Clause, the Madisonian Compromise, and Congress's control of jurisdiction, size and tenure

**Baseline.** Article III fixes little about the federal courts and leaves the rest to Congress: whether inferior courts exist, what they hear, how many Justices sit, and which cases reach the Supreme Court on appeal.

**Gloss.** Baude marked this one as an exam question in terms: what are Congress's limits on jurisdiction stripping. His own answer starts from the text — Art. III § 2 cl. 2's Exceptions power is real, and exceptions are not a total ban, which is the core theory — and then adds the timing limit McCardle exposes: Congress repealed jurisdiction after oral argument, and he thinks it cannot strip after the Court rules even though the Constitution does not say so. The question he leaves for you is where between argument and judgment the line falls, and the way to answer is to argue from what the limit is protecting rather than from text that does not exist. Two further distinctions do work: lower-court jurisdiction is freely strippable because Congress creates those courts under § 1, which makes the Supreme Court the only hard case; and the Marbury-style prudential reading is available, that the Court acquiesced to preserve its authority for later.

**Traps.**

- **"McCardle holds that Congress may cut off all Supreme Court review."** McCardle repealed one statutory route and said so expressly. Outside the assigned readings, *Ex parte Yerger* took jurisdiction months later through the reserved route, so Congress had not in fact stopped Supreme Court review of military detention.
- **"Jurisdiction stripping overrules the Court."** Stripping removes the occasion for a ruling; it does not change the Court's view of the Constitution or disturb judgments already entered.
- **"[[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] upheld abolishing the 1801 judgeships."** The opinion never mentions good-behaviour tenure, the Appointments Clause or the Compensation Clause. It decides a transfer-of-causes question and a circuit-riding question, sustains the Repeal Act by silence, and rests the circuit-riding holding entirely on twelve years of practice.

→ [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]]

## Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants

`3/high` · Art. I § 8 cl. 1 — the general welfare clause and the limits on conditional grants to the States

**Baseline.** Congress may spend on objects outside its other enumerated powers and may attach conditions that buy from the States what it could not command.

**Gloss.** Baude teaches spending as the Hamiltonian view the Court came around to, against Madison's narrower reading. Run Dole as four prongs plus coercion — and office hours fixed the label, which matters: the Dole factors decide whether the exercise of the spending power is valid or invalid, not whether it is anti-commandeering, so do not reach for the commandeering vocabulary. Post-NFIB the factors remain current with coercion as the fifth. O'Connor has two separate objections and they should be argued separately: she does not buy the safety connection, and she thinks the test has no categorical limit. His answer to her categorical limit is the drafting pathway — make the spending specific enough (highways where teenage accidents occur) and the limit is bypassed while the bill's point survives, which forces O'Connor back to the weaker relatedness objection. NFIB's Medicaid expansion is invalid spending rather than commandeering, and it goes beyond the ministerial door Printz left open because Medicaid is a quasi-state programme. Where a federal programme uses conditional spending rather than command, go straight to Dole plus coercion without passing through commerce. The political-process argument from Garcia is available in support, paired with a collective-action story: States all want the regulation but none will move alone, so a nationwide conditional programme is the process working.

**Traps.**

- **"Dole's fourth limit means Congress may not do indirectly what it cannot do directly."** That is the reading Dole rejected. The limit forbids only conditions that would induce the State to act unconstitutionally; the point of the case is that Congress may buy what it may not command.
- **"The general-welfare limit screens spending."** It is the limit Madison predicted no court would enforce, and none has. The work is done by germaneness and coercion, and the majority's shared-purpose germaneness test is looser than even [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s standard for means ([[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]]).
- **"There are four limits."** Coercion sits outside the four as a caveat drawn from Steward Machine, and it is the only one that has ever invalidated a spending statute. Count five things.

## Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath

`3/high` · Art. VI cl. 3 — the oath as the text of departmentalism, against judicial supremacy

**Baseline.** Judicial review is not judicial supremacy: a court's duty to prefer the Constitution in a case does not by itself bind non-parties to the court's reasoning.

**Gloss.** Baude runs the oath as the engine on both sides: it is Marbury's reason for judicial review and equally the reason a President who thinks a statute unconstitutional, with no ruling against him, may decline to enforce it. The sorting rule is sequential — no ruling yet, the executive may act on its own reading; a ruling exists, judicial review binds; and a statute both political branches accept can still fall, as the Line Item Veto Act did. Among departmentalists the split is remedial: most accept the judgment binds even where the opinion does not, the hard version would disobey the judgment too, and his practical check is that other executive officers may refuse the President. Departmentalism does not travel past the three federal branches, which is why Brown is a Supremacy Clause case and Cooper answers with a flat supremacy claim. Office hours added the reason supremacy wins in practice rather than in theory: lower courts can enjoin the action, so even a President who rejects the ruling is stopped by the next court. Note he also observed the President takes an extra oath.

**Traps.**

- **"Marbury said the judiciary is supreme in exposition."** Marbury holds that a court must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute in the case before it. The sentence "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" is Cooper's, written in 1958, and appears nowhere in Marbury.
- **"Cooper's supremacy passage is a holding."** The Court says the case was already disposed of. [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] could have rested on judgment supremacy alone, since the Board was a party to a decree and the United States held an injunction against the state officials.
- **"Lincoln was Faubus."** Lincoln disclaimed resisting the judgment; Faubus used troops to block a federal decree. A departmentalist who respects judgments condemns Faubus without adopting Cooper.

→ [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]]

## Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception

`3/high` · Art. II § 1 — the Vesting Clause removal power and what survives of the Humphrey's Executor exception after Trump v. Slaughter

**Baseline.** The President may fire at will any officer who exercises executive power, so a for-cause limit on removing that officer is unconstitutional, whether the agency has one head or five ([[Trump v.

**Gloss.** Baude teaches removal as a spectrum from unitary executive to congressional control, and flagged the middle ground as exam material. The history is a wash — 1789, the Tenure in Office Act, Myers, Humphrey's, Morrison, Free Enterprise Fund, Seila Law, and now Slaughter and Cook — with no side able to claim the practice. Read Humphrey's narrowly: it holds the FTC quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial rather than adopting congressional control, so the exception survives only as far as that characterisation does. The pressure points Slaughter leaves open are civil servants several layers down, non-Article III judges, and odd appointees like the Registrar of Copyrights, where his working answer is that whoever appointed the officer's superior may remove them — and office hours confirmed the end of that logic: the President may in theory fire down the chain until the office is emptied, at the cost of a Saturday Night Massacre, and the law is unclear. Office hours also gave the comparative argument worth deploying: congressional control is the less partisan theory, because Congress is many people and its statute binds every administration, while the unitary executive runs on one ship that does not carry forward.

**Traps.**

- **"Humphrey's was flatly overruled."** The overruling is conditional in form: the "observation" about bodies with no executive power survives, and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]], *Wiener* and the non-Article III courts are untouched. An answer treating every for-cause protection as void overshoots.
- **"A multimember commission is safe because it is multimember."** That was the *Seila Law* and *Collins* line. After [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] the number of heads is irrelevant; only the character of the power matters.
- **"Cook holds that Cook cannot be removed."** The ground of decision was statutory process, on a stay application. The Court did not decide whether the mortgage allegations are cause and did not reach due process; Kavanaugh stresses that removal for cause remains open.

## Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause

`3/high` · Art. III §§ 1–2 — judicial review as an incident of deciding cases, and the Art. VI "in Pursuance thereof" hook

**Baseline.** A federal court deciding a case must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute and refuse to give the statute effect. [[Marbury v.

**Gloss.** Baude teaches Marbury as strategic rather than syllogistic: Marshall did not want to find out whether Jefferson would obey, so the power to read the Constitution sits in dicta, Jefferson is told he is wrong, and relief is denied on original jurisdiction so nothing needs enforcing. Press 'in Pursuance thereof' in Art. VI cl. 2 as the strongest textual argument against the holding. Office hours gave the precise contrast with Dred Scott, which is the comparison to reproduce: in Marbury the want of jurisdiction *was* the holding — an unconstitutional statute meant no jurisdiction, and the rest was dicta — whereas in Dred Scott there was no jurisdiction to begin with and the Court reached past that to strike a statute anyway. The direction of the arrow is the whole point. Severability follows and he treats it as genuinely open: the test asks what Congress would have wanted, which is the impossible question that produced textualism, and the Court simply assumed the rest of the Judiciary Act survived. Note Congress can pre-empt the question with a non-severability clause.

**Traps.**

- **"Marbury established judicial supremacy."** Marbury holds that a court must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute in the case before it. The line "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" is [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]]'s (1958), in a passage Cooper conceded was unnecessary, and Cooper's supporting authorities speak of judgments, not opinions.
- **"The Court struck down (annulled) the statute."** On the readings' own account the power is "the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]), and the law is "censured" but "not abolished" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]). The vocabulary matters whenever the question is who else is bound.
- **"Marbury had to reach the constitutional question."** Section 13's mandamus language sits in a sentence about appellate jurisdiction, so it can be read to supply a remedy only where jurisdiction already exists. On that reading Marbury loses on the statute and there is no conflict with Article III.

## Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott

`3/medium` · Art. IV § 3 cl. 2 — the Territories Clause, congressional power over the territories, and national citizenship after Dred Scott

**Baseline.** Congress governs federal territory, and on the better reading the Territories Clause is itself a grant of legislative power, including power to exclude slavery.

**Gloss.** Baude uses Dred Scott to show originalist argument failing on its own terms: Taney rests citizenship on what the founders perceived, and the history cuts the other way, because contemporaries including preachers read 'all men' to include black men. On the merits he treats the Territory Clause move as a ridiculous argument from a textualist perspective — the clause grants Congress power over territories — and reads the real hook as the Fifth Amendment due process claim that the Missouri Compromise takes property without process, with the Territory Clause discussion serving only to bolster it. The structural trap is that Taney disclaims jurisdiction and rules anyway, passing straight through the gate Marshall built in Marbury; when a court tells you it lacks jurisdiction and then reaches the merits, that is the argument, and the answer is that everything after the jurisdictional holding is advisory.

**Traps.**

- **"Some part of [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] is still good law."** The Thirteenth Amendment removed the property premise and the Citizenship Clause reversed the citizenship holding. The Citizenship Clause also inverts Taney's premise that a State cannot make a national citizen: national citizenship is now primary, and state citizenship follows residence.
- **"The Missouri Compromise holding was just dictum."** That answer has to be argued. Each part "purports to be part of the Opinion of the Court," and Taney's best reply is that the territorial ruling is an alternative jurisdictional ground. Curtis's answer is that nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" a judgment of dismissal. [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] also decided the merits before finding no jurisdiction; the difference is that Marbury's extra discussion invalidated no statute.
- **"Taney held that Congress has no power over the territories."** Taney confined the Territories Clause to 1787 land and then found the power anyway, implied from the New States Clause. The holding that voided the statute is the due process holding.

→ [[Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott]]

## Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2

`2/medium` · Art. I § 9 cl. 2 — who may suspend the privilege of the writ, and what follows when the writ is defied

**Baseline.** Only Congress may suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and only during a rebellion or invasion that endangers the public safety. [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] held that the privilege "could not be suspended, except by act of congress," so the President can neither suspend it himself nor "delegate that discretionary power to a military officer." Suspension withdraws only the remedy: Taney called due process, the warrant requirement and the speedy-trial right "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend." The contest is over who decides and what follows when the writ is refused. [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Attorney General Bates]] answered that a judgment "binds only the parties to the case decided" and that "the whole subject-matter is political and not judicial," which makes the Clause the sharpest test of [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art.

**Gloss.** Baude ran Merryman and the Bates opinion as the departmentalism problem in its sharpest form rather than as a habeas doctrine: the clause sits in Article I, which is the textual argument that suspension is Congress's to make, and Lincoln suspended anyway. Bates supplies the structural answer he quoted on the other side — if any one branch may determine the extent of its own powers and those of the other two, that branch controls the whole government — which is why the question is who decides rather than what the clause means. Carry it as the emergency case that tests everything else: a President acting against the clause's placement is at his lowest ebb on Jackson's framework, so the defence has to be that the power is conclusive and preclusive, and the historical practice of 1861 is the only support for that. McCardle is the companion move, since Congress reached the same result by stripping jurisdiction rather than by suspending.

**Traps.**

- **"Lincoln defied an order to release Merryman."** Taney issued a writ and a contempt attachment, both refused, and his final opinion ordered only that the record be filed and transmitted to the President. What was adjudged matters, because the judgment-supremacy debate turns on it.
- **"Merryman is a Supreme Court decision."** Taney sat alone as Circuit Justice, and Bates exploits that — "a judge, at chambers." The casebook, by contrast, says Taney ruled "from the bench" in the circuit court; either way the ruling carries the weight of one Justice on circuit, not of the Court.
- **"The Suspension Clause names Congress."** The Clause does not say who suspends. Taney's answer is structural (placement in Art. I, § 9, and the writ's history against the Crown), not a quotation from the Clause.

## Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3

`2/low` · Art. I § 9 cl. 3 — legislative punishment of named individuals without a judicial trial, including by appropriations rider

**Baseline.** Congress may not single out particular people and punish them by statute instead of leaving guilt and punishment to a court.

**Gloss.** Baude drew the attainder line at valence rather than at specificity: Congress may not single out an individual for a negative, criminal judgment, but a positive, targeted, civil act aimed at named people is constitutionally fine. The clause therefore does no work against most targeted legislation, and the hypo to watch is a burden dressed as a civil measure — the argument runs on whether the act is punitive in character, not on whether it names someone.

**Traps.**

- **Looking for a crime.** The Clause is not limited to criminal punishment; § 304 designated no conduct as criminal and was still an attainder, because permanent exclusion from a vocation is punishment.
- **"It is only an appropriation, so it is Congress's call."** "[N]o matter what their form" is the operative phrase, and the Court expressly rejected the argument that a pay cutoff is an unreviewable political matter.
- **"Any statute that names a person is an attainder."** Specificity is necessary but not sufficient; there must also be punishment, imposed without a judicial trial.

## Constitutional themes and the dead hand

`1/low` · U.S. Const. pmbl. and art. VI — the six recurring themes, supremacy, and why a founding-era document binds

**Baseline.** The Constitution is supreme written law made by the people, it binds every officer who exercises power, and it can be changed only through Article V.

**Gloss.** Baude opened the course by splitting the document into three jobs — rights, separation of powers, federalism — and used that split as the organising frame for the whole term rather than as background. He drew the line between constitutional and statutory law at two features: the Constitution is supreme over statutes and it is more ambiguous, which is why its fights run at a higher level of abstraction. On reading the text he warned that punctuation and capitalisation may not carry much meaning. The exam instruction he gave in the first class was to make constitutional arguments to a fact set.

**Traps.**

- **"The Constitution is just, so read rights into it to close any gap."** The sixth theme says the opposite, and Roane's "nose of wax" names the failure mode.
- **"Federalist 39 shows the government is national."** Madison's scorecard is deliberately split, "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both," and each of its five categories gets a different answer.
- **"Popular sovereignty means the states (or the nation) are sovereign."** "We the People" means neither is. That premise is what [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] uses against Maryland and what the secession readings fight over.

→ [[Constitutional themes and the dead hand]]
