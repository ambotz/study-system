## Snapshot

Congress answered [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] by taxing 10 percent of the net profits of any mine or factory employing children below the prescribed ages or hours. A furniture company that employed one under-age boy paid $6,312.79 under protest and won a refund in the district court. The Court held the exaction a penalty rather than a tax, invalid because the power to regulate child labor rests with the states. Article I, § 8, cl. 1 and the Tenth Amendment are in play.

## Issue

- Is the Child Labor Tax Law a valid exercise of Congress's power to lay and collect taxes, or is the 10 percent exaction a penalty enforcing a regulation of child labor?
- If it is a penalty, is there any enumerated power to support it, after [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] denied Congress power to reach child labor through commerce?

## Rule

An exaction Congress calls a tax is a penalty, and so must be supported by some other enumerated power, when the statute on its face prescribes a detailed course of conduct and enforces departures from it with a heavy exaction. The classification is made from the four corners of the act — "[w]e must construe the law and interpret the intent and meaning of Congress from the language of the act" — not from legislative history and not from the label Congress chose. A tax laid with "the incidental motive of discouraging" its subject does not lose its character as a tax; it loses that character when "there comes a time in the extension of the penalizing features" that it "becomes a mere penalty, with the characteristics of regulation and punishment." The distinction only matters where the taxing and regulating powers sit in different sovereigns, which is why it is dispositive against Congress and would not be against a state legislature.

**The threshold.** Weight alone is not enough. On "an excise on a commodity or other thing of value," the Court "might not be permitted under previous decisions of this court to infer solely from its heavy burden that the act intends a prohibition instead of a tax." What takes the act past that threshold is that it "provides a heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business."

**The four features that mark the exaction as a penalty.** Taft never says how many are required or how heavy is too heavy; he reads them together.

1. **Code of conduct/Regulation: a detailed and specified course of conduct written into the statute.** The act does not describe a taxable event; it prescribes ages, hours and shifts, and charges for departing from them. This is the threshold factor — it is what makes the other three admissible.
2. **Prohibitory and unproportional: an exaction out of all proportion to the departure.** The amount "is not to be proportioned in any degree to the extent or frequency of the departures, but is to be paid by the employer in full measure whether he employs 500 children for a year, or employs only one for a day."
3. **Scienter: liability only for a knowing violation, with a defence for honest mistake.** The charge falls "only where he knowingly departs from the prescribed course." "Scienters are associated with penalties, not with taxes." A levy an honest mistake defeats is pricing culpability, not revenue.
4. **Enforcing agency: collection and inspection by officers whose function is not revenue.** Inspection runs to the Secretary of Labor as well as the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, and the Secretary's "normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers."

## Operative facts

- **[[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (1918) held 5–4 that Congress could not exclude child-made goods from interstate commerce, the goods being harmless and production local.** *The absence of a regulatory power is what makes the tax label load-bearing; this case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart."*
- **Within months Congress wrote the same policy into the revenue laws as Title XII of "[a]n act to provide revenue and for other purposes," approved February 24, 1919, 40 Stat. 1057, 1138 — eight sections beginning at § 1200, headed "Tax on Employment of Child Labor."** *An end-run enacted while the ink was dry; the heading is the only place the word "tax" does any work. The casebook misprints the cite as "1057, 1038" at 5th ed. 606 — use 1138.*
- **Section 1200 prescribed minimum ages — 16 in mines and quarries, 14 in mills, canneries, workshops and factories — capped those aged 14 to 16 at eight hours a day and six days a week, and barred work after 7 p.m. or before 6 a.m., exempting only a bona fide boys' or girls' canning club.** *That is "a detailed and specified course of conduct in business," the feature that converts the exaction into a penalty. An exemption written for canning clubs is a labor-policy judgment, not a revenue classification.*
- **The exaction was 10 percent of the employer's entire net profits for the year, owed in the same amount whether he used five hundred children for a year or one child for a day.** *"The amount is not to be proportioned in any degree to the extent or frequency of the departures" — heavy, and untethered from the violation.*
- **Section 1203 made the exaction turn on knowledge, excusing an employer who acted "under a mistake of fact as to the age of such child and without intention to evade the tax," or who relied on an age certificate issued by state authorities or by a federal board.** *"Scienters are associated with penalties, not with taxes." A tax that an honest mistake defeats is collecting culpability, not revenue.*
- **Section 1206 opened the premises to inspection "at any time" by the Commissioner of Internal Revenue and, on his request, by the Secretary of Labor, and made refusal of entry or obstruction punishable by fine or imprisonment.** *An enforcing agency "whose normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers" is another tell; so is a criminal sanction for resisting the inspection.*
- **Drexel Furniture employed one boy under fourteen in its North Carolina factory during 1919 and was assessed $6,312.79 on the whole year's profits of the business.** *One child, a year's profits: the disproportion in miniature.*
- **It paid under protest, was refused a refund, and sued Bailey, the collector of internal revenue; the district court held the law unconstitutional (276 Fed. 452) and the Government appealed.** *A refund suit puts the question up with no standing or remedy problem in the way.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (8–1; Taft, C.J., for the Court; Clarke, J., dissenting without opinion)

- **A penalty, not a tax.** The act's own provisions prove its character, and the judgment for Drexel Furniture is affirmed.
- **No power supports it.** Because the exaction is a penalty it must rest on a power to regulate child labor, and [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] holds there is none.

**Taft, C.J., for the Court**

- The question is framed as a binary at the outset: "Does this law impose a tax with only that incidental restraint and regulation which a tax must inevitably involve? Or does it regulate by the use of the so-called tax as a penalty?" There is no middle category, and no deference once the second branch is reached.
- The four features are read together, and the detailed course of conduct is what makes the rest admissible: "this act is more" than an excise whose weight alone might be suspicious.
- Purpose is found in the enacted text and treated as a matter of plain sight rather than proof: "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children within the age limits prescribed. Its prohibitory and regulatory effect and purpose are palpable. All others can see and understand this. How can we properly shut our minds to it?"
- Striking the act is cast as an obligation, not a choice: it is "the high duty and function of this court . . . to decline to recognize or enforce seeming laws of Congress, dealing with subjects not intrusted to Congress," and the duty cannot be avoided "even though it require us to refuse to give effect to legislation designed to promote the highest good." Hence the warning that "[t]he good sought in unconstitutional legislation is an insidious feature," because it recruits legislators of good purpose to breach "the ark of our covenant."
- The line is one of degree. Taxes laid with "the incidental motive of discouraging" an activity "do not lose their character as taxes," but "there comes a time in the extension of the penalizing features of the so-called tax when it loses its character as such and becomes a mere penalty, with the characteristics of regulation and punishment."
- Why classification matters: "Where the sovereign enacting the law has power to impose both tax and penalty, the difference between revenue production and mere regulation may be immaterial, but not so when one sovereign can impose a tax only, and the power of regulation rests in another."
- The slippery slope is the heart of the opinion. Congress could take over any reserved subject by enacting "a detailed measure of complete regulation of the subject" and enforcing it "by a so-called tax upon departures from it"; to give such "magic to the word 'tax'" would "break down all constitutional limitation of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states."

**Clarke, J., dissenting without opinion**

- He had dissented in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] too, the only member to take Congress's side in both. His position, which Taft never answers: McCray held that courts may not police the purposes behind a lawful power, and this statute does nothing a tax cannot do.

## Arguments rejected

- *The act is "a mere excise tax levied by the Congress of the United States under its broad power of taxation conferred by section 8, article 1," and the Court's own decisions forbid inferring prohibition from a tax's weight.* Taft concedes the premise and escapes it by making the statute's structure, not its burden, the evidence: on a plain excise he "might not be permitted . . . to infer solely from its heavy burden that the act intends a prohibition instead of a tax," but "this act is more." Weight alone is not the test; weight attached to a written code of conduct is.
- *The presumption of constitutionality should carry a revenue act past this kind of inspection.* Taft: the Court has "gone far to sustain taxing acts as such, even though there has been ground for suspecting, from the weight of the tax, it was intended to destroy its subject" — but here "the presumption of validity cannot prevail, because the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions." The presumption protects motive, not text.
- *The License Tax Cases, Veazie Bank and McCray sustained taxes at least as prohibitory as this one, so the precedents control.* Taft distinguishes all three on factor 1 — none of those statutes presented "the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern and business with a heavy exaction to promote the efficacy of such regulation" (the formulation the casebook quotes at note 4, 5th ed. 606; it falls in the part of the opinion the Canvas PDF elides). The answer reconciles the outcomes without reconciling the reasoning, which is what note 4 presses.
- *The taxing power is as complete as the commerce power, and legislative motive in its exercise is "just as free from judicial suspicion and inquiry."* Taft accepts the parallel and turns it: [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] held that Congress stopping commerce "in ordinary and necessary commodities, unobjectionable as subjects of transportation," to coerce compliance with a regulation of state concerns "was not in fact regulation of interstate commerce." "So here the so-called tax is a penalty to coerce people of a state to act as Congress wishes them to act in respect of a matter completely the business of the state government."
- *Congress has an independent taxing power, so it needs no regulatory power to support an exaction collected by the Treasury.* Taft: the difference between revenue and regulation is immaterial only "[w]here the sovereign enacting the law has power to impose both tax and penalty," and not "when one sovereign can impose a tax only, and the power of regulation rests in another." Child labor belongs to the states, so calling the exaction a tax is the question, not the answer.

## Where it sits

**The two-case story.** [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (1918) held 5–4 that Congress could not exclude child-made goods from interstate commerce; Congress answered within months and the Court struck the tax 8–1. Congress proposed the Child Labor Amendment in 1924, never ratified; Hammer was overruled in [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] (1941).

**The outlier in the taxing survey.** In [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]] the pattern runs the other way — the *License Tax Cases* (1866), *Veazie Bank v. Fenno* (1869), *McCray v. United States* (1904) — and after Bailey the Court tacked back in *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis* (1937) and *United States v. Kahriger* (1953), never reconciled with it.

**NFIB runs the factors backwards.** [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] (2012) asks Bailey's questions — how heavy, is there scienter, who collects — and gets opposite answers. Bailey holds a thing called a tax is a penalty; NFIB holds a thing called a penalty is a tax, and keeps Bailey as the outer limit.

**Pretext.** Taft rests the result on the pretext passage in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]], 4 Wheat. at 423, quoting it in full: "should Congress, under the pretext of executing its powers, pass laws for the accomplishment of objects not intrusted to the government; it would become the painful duty of this tribunal . . . to say that such an act was not the law of the land." Bailey is the survey's only case to cash that caution — the one place an enumerated power is held unusable because of its object. [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] buried the same inquiry for commerce: motive is "for the legislative judgment."

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

The Canvas PDF is the opinion alone, but the casebook's notes on Bailey are assigned the same day: the case is **note 4** of [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]], 5th ed. 605–607, which reprints the statute and the two central passages and then asks the questions below. Note 6 returns to Bailey through *Kahriger*, and note 7 is the summary question for the whole survey.

> **Note 4.** "Even though Hammer v. Dagenhart had been a 5-4 decision, the vote in Bailey was 8-1! Three justices who thought that the original child labor statute was permissible under the Commerce Clause still rejected Congress's attempt to impose a child labor tax. (Only Justice Clarke dissented in both cases.) Is that a sign that the Court saw this as an obvious end-run around its own authority? Or is there some other way the tax was more intrusive than the commerce regulation?"

Both, and the second is the better answer. The end-run reading is real — the statute was enacted months after [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer]] and the Court says the case "cannot be distinguished" from it. But the intrusiveness point is what explains the three switched votes: Hammer's ban operated on goods *after* they left the factory, while the tax operates *inside* it, prescribing ages and shifts and putting Labor inspectors on the premises under threat of fine or imprisonment. A Justice could think Congress may not close the channels of commerce to harmless goods and still think the tax worse, because the tax regulates the local activity directly rather than conditioning its access to a market.

> **Note 4, continued.** "Is Bailey right that there has to be some point where something labeled as a 'tax' is really a penalty, and therefore must be justified (if at all) by the other enumerated powers? If so, does Bailey actually tell us how to find that point, or it just ad hoc? Also, is Bailey consistent with the License Tax Cases, Veazie, and McCray? If not, which cases are correct?"

Three separate questions; keep them apart. **That there must be a line** is hard to resist: without one, the enumeration is optional, which is Taft's "magic to the word 'tax'" point. **Whether Bailey locates it** is the weak part — four factors, no weighting, no threshold, and the only one Taft calls indispensable (the written code of conduct) is the one Congress can drop by moving the standards into regulations or, as in *Steward Machine*, into a credit. **Consistency** is the question to have an answer ready for: Taft's own distinction is that in those cases the statute did not present "the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern and business with a heavy exaction to promote the efficacy of such regulation." That reconciles the outcomes but not the reasoning — *McCray* said motive is off-limits, and reading a regulatory purpose off the face of the statute is still reading purpose.

> **Note 6.** "What happened to the Child Labor Tax Case? Can it be distinguished from Kahriger? Or does Kahriger prove that the Child Labor Tax Case couldn't come up with an administrable line between taxes and penalties? And what do you make of Justice Jackson's reservations?"

*Kahriger* (1953) upheld a tax on bookies although "the intent to curtail and hinder, as well as tax," was manifest, on the ground that "regardless of its regulatory effect, the wagering tax produces revenue." Distinguishable on factor 1: the wagering tax prescribed no course of conduct, it taxed a status. But the distinction concedes that the line is about drafting rather than about coercion, which is the force of the second half of the question. Jackson's "doubt that if the minority agreed upon an opinion which did not impair legitimate use of the taxing power I probably would join it" is the honest position: he cannot defend the line and will not abandon it.

> **Note 7, the summary question.** "Must a tax actually raise some revenue to be a tax? If so, how much?"

Bailey does not ask this, and that is its gap — the Child Labor Tax raised revenue. *Kahriger* makes revenue sufficient; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] makes it close to necessary. Carry this question into NFIB, which is where the casebook sends it.

### Further drilling

- **Why isn't this just McCray?** McCray refused to ask *why* Congress taxed oleomargarine; Taft says he is reading what Congress wrote. That holds only if a statute's structure is evidence of a <u>different</u> kind from its legislative history — real, since the text is enacted, but thin, because Congress can drop the code and leave it to regulations.
- **Hypo: 10 percent of net profits on every manufacturer, with a credit for any manufacturer certified by a state labor department as complying with state law.** That is Steward Machine's architecture, and it survived: the conduct is no longer prescribed federally and the credit does the work. If that is valid and Bailey is not, the rule is about drafting technique.
- **Hypo: the same tax without the scienter requirement, collected only by the IRS; or a flat $500 licence fee on any factory employing anyone under sixteen.** The first leaves only weight and detail, and under [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] weight alone can still be prohibitory. The second looks like the *License Tax Cases* and is probably valid — so Bailey is about how hard Congress may press, not whether it may press.
- **Federalism, not liberty — and the unit's punch line.** Taft's objection is that child labor is "reserved to [the states] by the Tenth Amendment," the instinct behind [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]. The argument fails against a state legislature, which holds both powers. Meanwhile the taxing power reaches what [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] and Hammer put beyond commerce; Bailey forbids only writing the regulation into the revenue statute.
- **Memorable lines.** "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children." Such "magic to the word 'tax'" would "wipe out the sovereignty of the states." "The good sought in unconstitutional legislation is an insidious feature." The numbers: 10 percent of net profits, $6,312.79, 8–1.
