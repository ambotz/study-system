## Rule

Article V "implies that amendments submitted thereunder must be ratified, if at all, within some reasonable time after their proposal," so Congress may fix a definite ratification period when it proposes an amendment; seven years is reasonable, and an amendment takes effect on the date the thirty-sixth state ratifies, not on the Secretary of State's proclamation.

## Facts

- **Congress put a seven-year ratification limit in the resolution proposing the Eighteenth Amendment.** *The only fact the first three holdings turn on. Without the limit, there is no question about Congress's power to impose one, and the Court would have had to decide whether a limit is implied without one — the question [[The Congressional Pay Amendment]] raises.*
- **Ratification was consummated on January 16, 1919, and the Secretary of State proclaimed it on January 29.** *Thirteen days apart, and the whole fourth holding lives in that gap: the Amendment's one-year delay ran from the earlier date.*
- **Dillon was held for transporting intoxicating liquor under the National Prohibition Act.** *Timing is everything: he was arrested after January 16, 1920 but within a year of the proclamation, so he was guilty under the ratification date and not yet subject to the Amendment under the proclamation date.*
- **He sought habeas corpus, attacking the Amendment itself.** *A collateral attack on the Constitution's text as the defense to a criminal charge, which is why the Court reaches questions no ordinary case presents.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (unanimous; Van Devanter, J.)

- Article V implies that ratification must come "within some reasonable time after their proposal."
- Ratification "must be sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of states to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period" — the contemporaneous-consensus principle.
- Congress may "fix a definite period for the ratification," which the Court treats as "a matter of detail which Congress may determine as an incidental" to the power of proposal.
- "Seven years, the period fixed in this instance, was reasonable."
- The Amendment became effective on ratification by the requisite states on January 16, 1919, not on proclamation, so the one-year delay expired January 16, 1920 and Dillon's detention was lawful.

**Rejected**

- *Article V's text says nothing about time, so Congress may not add a limit; the only requirements are two-thirds and three-fourths.* The Court reads the requirement as implicit in the nature of ratification: an amendment expresses the people's will, and ratifications scattered across generations do not express one will at one time.
- *An amendment is not part of the Constitution until officially proclaimed.* Proclamation is ministerial evidence of ratification, not the operative act; the states' ratifications complete the amendment.

## Context

The only Supreme Court holding on Article V that Class 26 rests on, and it is doing two jobs. Its narrow holding — Congress may set a deadline, and seven years is reasonable — is uncontroversial and answers Question 2 of [[The Riddles of Article V]]. Its dictum about contemporaneous consensus is what makes the chapter hard: if ratification must reflect one generation's will, then [[The Congressional Pay Amendment]], ratified over 203 years, is in trouble, and Congress's 414–3 and 99–0 acceptance votes cannot cure a constitutional defect.

*Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433 (1939), pulled back. It treated the timeliness of a ratification of the Child Labor Amendment as a question for Congress, either as a matter of congressional power or as a political question, without a controlling majority opinion. Together the two cases leave the deadline question with a judicial rule that courts then declined to enforce, which is why [[The Equal Rights Amendment]] fight was conducted between Congress, OLC and the Archivist rather than in court.

Note what Dillon does *not* decide: whether a deadline may be extended, whether a state may rescind, and whether an amendment proposed without any deadline can ever expire. Those are the live questions in this class, and the case's contemporaneous-consensus language is the best argument on one side of each.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Habeas corpus. Dillon, held by a federal officer under the National Prohibition Act, petitioned for the writ; the district court denied relief and discharged the writ, and he appealed directly to the Supreme Court.

### Facts

Congress proposed the Eighteenth Amendment in December 1917 with a seven-year ratification limit written into the proposing resolution. The requisite number of state legislatures ratified by January 16, 1919, and the Secretary of State proclaimed the Amendment on January 29, 1919. By its own terms the Amendment took effect one year after ratification. Dillon was arrested for transporting intoxicating liquor in violation of the National Prohibition Act, which Congress enacted to enforce the Amendment. He argued both that the Amendment was invalid because Congress lacked power to impose the seven-year condition, and that even if valid it had not yet taken effect, because the year should run from the proclamation rather than from the ratification.

### Issue

- May Congress fix a definite time within which the states must ratify a proposed amendment?
- Was the seven-year period reasonable?
- Does an amendment take effect on ratification by the last necessary state, or on the Secretary of State's proclamation?

### Holding

- Yes. The power is incidental to the power of proposal, and Article V implies that ratification must occur within a reasonable time.
- Yes. Seven years is reasonable, assuming the power to set a definite period exists at all.
- On ratification. The Amendment became effective January 16, 1919, so its one-year delay expired January 16, 1920, and Dillon's detention was lawful. Affirmed.

### Rule

- Amendments must be ratified "within some reasonable time after their proposal."
- Ratifications must be "sufficiently contemporaneous . . . to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- Congress may fix a definite ratification period as an incident of proposing an amendment.
- Ratification, not proclamation, is the operative act.

### Reasoning

**(Van Devanter, J., for the Court)**

- Article V is silent on time, but silence is not permission for indefinite pendency: the Court treats proposal and ratification as successive steps in a single undertaking rather than unrelated acts, so they belong to the same period.
- An amendment is an expression of the people's will at a given moment; ratifications separated by generations would aggregate the wills of different peoples.
- Because a reasonable time is implied, fixing what is reasonable in advance is a detail Congress may settle, and settling it avoids litigation over reasonableness after the fact.
- Proclamation is evidence, not operation. The states' act completes the amendment, and the executive's certificate announces what has already happened.

### Dissent / concurrence

Unanimous, with no separate opinions. The unexplored tension: the Court derives a time requirement from structure and purpose while conceding the text says nothing, then hands the content of that requirement to Congress. *Coleman v. Miller* took the next step and made the whole question one for Congress, which leaves Dillon's constitutional principle with no judicial enforcement.

### Cold-call notes

- **What is the holding, precisely?** Congress may set a ratification deadline, seven years is reasonable, and ratification (not proclamation) is the effective date. The contemporaneous-consensus language is the *reasoning*, not a holding that any particular ratification came too late.
- **Where does the time limit come from, given Article V's silence?** Structure and purpose: proposal and ratification as steps in one process, and an amendment as an expression of one people's will at one time. Be ready to press the textual objection — Article V specifies majorities and modes with precision, and a drafter who wanted a clock could have written one, as Congress later did in amendment texts.
- **Does Dillon decide the Twenty-seventh Amendment's validity?** No, and this is the trap. There was no deadline in the 1789 proposal, and Dillon addresses a proposal with one. Its dictum cuts against the Twenty-seventh; its holding does not reach it.
- **If contemporaneous consensus is a real requirement, who enforces it?** After *Coleman v. Miller*, likely nobody: Congress judges, or the question is political. That answer determines how the ERA and pay-amendment fights actually get resolved — by OLC opinions, an Archivist's certification and congressional votes.
- **Vary the facts.** A deadline in the amendment's text rather than the resolution (the Eighteenth had both features across amendments; the Twentieth through Twenty-second put the limit in the text) — does Congress's power to extend change? A proposal with no deadline ratified over forty years? Over two hundred ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]])? A deadline extended before it expires ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]), versus revived after?
- **Why did the effective-date holding matter to Dillon personally?** The thirteen days between ratification and proclamation moved the Amendment's one-year grace period, and his conduct fell inside the gap. A defendant's liberty turned on which act completes an amendment.
- **Memorable line.** Ratification must be "sufficiently contemporaneous . . . to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
