## Rule

Article V implies that ratification must come "within some reasonable time after the proposal," so Congress may fix a definite ratification period as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification; seven years is reasonable.

## Facts

- **The proposing resolution for the Eighteenth Amendment declared the Amendment "inoperative unless ratified within seven years."** *The whole case. Without that limit Dillon has no argument that the Amendment is invalid, and the Court has no occasion to ask where a time limit could come from.*
- **Article V says nothing about time.** *Van Devanter concedes this at the outset — "neither that it shall be unlimited nor that it shall be fixed by Congress" — which forces the Court onto implication rather than text, and gives the OLC its opening seventy years later.*
- **Four amendments proposed long ago were still pending in 1921 — two from 1789, one from 1810, one from 1861.** *The consequence that makes the contrary reading unattractive: ratifications "by representatives of generations now largely forgotten" could be completed by a later generation. One of those four is the Congressional Pay Amendment, ratified in 1992.*
- **Dillon was in custody under the National Prohibition Act for transporting intoxicating liquor.** *A criminal defendant attacking the constitutional amendment his statute enforces; the Court reaches an Article V question because a habeas petitioner raised it.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Van Devanter, J.)

- Article V's silence is not permission for indefinite pendency: "with the Constitution, as with a statute or other written instrument, what is reasonably implied is as much a part of it as what is expressed."
- Three grounds support an implied time limit. Proposal and ratification are "not treated as unrelated acts but as succeeding steps in a single endeavor." Amendments are proposed only when "deemed to be a necessity," implying they are "to be considered and disposed of presently." And because ratification "is but the expression of the approbation of the people," it "must be sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of States to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- The Court adopts Judge Jameson's conclusion that an amendment not ratified "early while that sentiment may fairly be supposed to exist" should be "regarded as waived, and not again to be voted upon, unless a second time proposed by Congress."
- Congress may fix a definite period: whether to do so "is, in our opinion, a matter of detail which Congress may determine as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification."
- Seven years "was reasonable, if power existed to fix a definite time," and that is confirmed by "the periods within which prior amendments were ratified."

**Rejected**

- *Article V sets out its requirements with precision — two-thirds, three-fourths, two modes of ratification — so a court may not add a condition the drafters left out.* The Court answers that what is reasonably implied is part of the instrument, and finds the implication in Article V's own structure rather than in policy.
- *A proposed amendment stays alive until three-fourths of the states act, whenever that happens.* The Court calls this "quite untenable," pointing to the four long-pending proposals whose old ratifications could be completed by a later generation.

## Context

The only Supreme Court opinion the Article V chapter excerpts, and the casebook prints it to set up a fight rather than to state settled law. [[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment|The 1992 OLC opinion]], printed immediately after it, says Dillon's reasoning "is unpersuasive in both its specific arguments and in its broader methodology" and that the contemporaneity requirement "is not within the holding" — the Twenty-seventh Amendment exists because the executive branch acted on that view.

*Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433 (1939), is the hinge. Chief Justice Hughes, writing for three Justices, treated Dillon's three considerations as dictum and concluded that whether the Child Labor Amendment had "lost its vitality through lapse of time" was for Congress when the time came to promulgate; the casebook stresses that Coleman has no controlling majority opinion. After Coleman the rule survives without a court to enforce it, which is why the [[The Equal Rights Amendment|ERA]] fight runs through Congress, OLC and the Archivist.

Two pieces of the opinion the casebook's excerpt leaves out. Dillon also held that ratification was consummated when the thirty-sixth state ratified (January 16, 1919), not on the Secretary of State's proclamation (January 29, 1919), so the Amendment's one-year delay ended on January 16, 1920 — which is why Dillon himself lost, because his conduct fell inside the thirteen-day gap. And the excerpt elides, after "the periods within which prior amendments were ratified," the Court's survey of those periods. Neither is in the assigned pages; use them only as background.

Set the case against [[The Congressional Pay Amendment]], which is the fact pattern Dillon said was "quite untenable," and against [[Hollingsworth v. Virginia]] (1798), the other early Article V holding: an amendment need not be presented to the President.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from an order denying a petition for habeas corpus. Dillon was held under § 26 of Title II of the National Prohibition Act on a charge of transporting intoxicating liquor in violation of § 3.
- He attacked the Eighteenth Amendment itself, so the Court reached Article V in a criminal-custody posture rather than in a suit about an amendment's validity.

### Facts

Congress proposed the Eighteenth Amendment in a resolution, 40 Stat. 1050, declaring that it would be inoperative unless ratified within seven years. The states ratified within that period, and Congress passed the National Prohibition Act to enforce it. Dillon, arrested for transporting liquor, argued first that the seven-year condition was beyond Congress's power and so invalidated the Amendment, and second that the Act's provisions had not yet taken effect when he was arrested, because the Amendment's one-year delay should run from the Secretary of State's proclamation rather than from the completion of ratification. The casebook excerpts only the first question.

### Issue

- Does Article V permit Congress to fix a definite period within which a proposed amendment must be ratified?
- If so, was seven years a reasonable period?

### Holding

- Yes. Ratification must occur within a reasonable time, which is implied by Article V, and fixing that period in advance is a matter of detail incident to Congress's power to designate the mode of ratification.
- Yes. Seven years was reasonable, measured against the time prior amendments had taken.

### Rule

- "[T]he fair inference or implication from Article V is that the ratification must be within some reasonable time after the proposal."
- Ratification must be "sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of States to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- Congress may fix a definite period "as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification."

### Reasoning

**(Van Devanter, J., for the Court)**

- Start from silence: the Article says nothing either way, and neither the federal convention's debates nor the state ratifying conventions "shed any light on the question."
- Implication fills the gap, on the ordinary principle that what is reasonably implied in a written instrument is part of it.
- The three structural points — single endeavor, necessity implying prompt disposition, ratification as a contemporaneous expression of popular approbation — all point the same way.
- The consequences confirm it: on the contrary view, four long-dormant proposals could be revived by a later generation, a result "few would be able to subscribe" to.
- Fixing a period avoids "speculation on what is a reasonable time," which is a practical argument for letting Congress decide in advance rather than leaving courts to decide after the fact.

### Dissent / concurrence

None; the Court is unanimous, and the opinion is short. The tension worth naming is internal. The Court derives a timing requirement from structure and purpose while conceding the text is silent, then hands the content of that requirement to Congress — so the constitutional principle and the institution that applies it come apart. *Coleman* widened that gap, and the OLC opinion drives through it.

### Cold-call notes

- **What exactly did Dillon hold?** That Congress may set a ratification deadline and that seven years is reasonable. The contemporaneity language is reasoning, not a holding that any particular ratification came too late. Getting this right is what lets you follow the OLC's attack on the case.
- **Name the three grounds.** Single endeavor; proposal only on necessity, implying prompt disposition; ratification as a contemporaneous expression of the people's approbation. The OLC answers each: the first assumes its conclusion, the second confuses necessity with emergency, the third assumes consensus must be contemporaneous.
- **Where does the Court get authority to add a term to Article V?** From implication in a written instrument. Press the counter: Article V states its numbers precisely and expressly fixed one deadline — no amendment before 1808 touching the slave-trade and capitation clauses — so the drafters knew how to write a time rule when they wanted one.
- **Would the Court apply Dillon to the Twenty-seventh Amendment?** Dillon's dictum says no ratification over 203 years; Coleman says it is for Congress; the OLC says the premise is wrong. The honest answer is that the question has never been litigated because Congress has not tried to violate the Amendment.
- **Vary the facts.** A deadline in the proposing resolution rather than the amendment's text (the Eighteenth and the Twentieth through Twenty-second put the seven years in the text; the ERA's was in the resolution). A deadline extended before it expires ([[The Equal Rights Amendment|the ERA]]). An amendment with no deadline ratified over forty years rather than two centuries — is there a line, and who draws it?
- **Memorable lines.** "[S]ucceeding steps in a single endeavor." "[S]ufficiently contemporaneous . . . to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period." Jameson's "regarded as waived."
