## Snapshot

The President purported to remove a Federal Reserve Governor for cause, citing alleged mortgage fraud that predated her service; she won a preliminary injunction, and the Court denied the Government's stay application. It held that she had received none of the notice and opportunity to respond a for-cause removal requires, so the removal was void, and that the Governors' protection is consistent with Article II because the Fed follows the First and Second Banks' tradition of independence from the President. 12 U.S.C. § 242 and Article II are the provisions in play; the Canvas edit is built around the Article II question.

## Issue

- **Constitutional (the assigned excerpt).** Is the Governors' for-cause protection consistent with Article II after [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]]?
- **Statutory.**
    - Is the President's determination of "cause" judicially reviewable?
    - What does "for cause" in 12 U.S.C. § 242 require?
    - May a court keep a removed Governor in office while the removal is litigated?
    - Did the President give Cook the process the statute requires?

## Rule

The Fed is the exception [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] reserved: the Governors' for-cause protection "is consistent with the Constitution" because the Fed follows the First and Second Banks' tradition of insulating monetary policy from the President. The tradition is read at the level of its principle, not its particulars, so the modern Fed's greater power does not take it outside the exception.

- The founders "guaranteed [the First Bank's] independence from Presidential control," "[t]heir successors did the same for the Second Bank," and "[t]he Federal Reserve follows in this lineage."
- The central bank is not "trapped in amber"; what matters is that the Fed remains "consistent with the principles that underpin" the Banks — "that monetary policy should not be subject to political interference."
- History gives "essential content to undefined provisions in the frame of our government" (*Youngstown*, Frankfurter, J.).

**Statutory rules (not in the excerpt).**

- "Cause" is a common-law term of art, judicially reviewable and pitched at a substantial threshold, turning "on the seriousness of the alleged misconduct, and the extent of any nexus that may exist to the Governor's professional duties."
- A fixed term with removal for cause carries a common-law right to notice and an opportunity to respond.
- Removal "is a decision only the President can make (short of impeachment)," but that does not mean "that he may make that decision for any reason, or no reason."
- Equity may keep a Governor in office pending litigation if she is otherwise entitled to a preliminary injunction.

## Operative facts

- **Section 242 gives each of the seven Governors a staggered fourteen-year term and allows removal only "for cause."** *The protection Slaughter's logic would void unless the Fed's history saves it.*
- **The Fed has two layers: twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks, privately owned by their member commercial banks, and two national bodies — the Federal Open Market Committee, which sets monetary policy, and the Board of Governors, which supervises the system.** *The quasi-private regional layer is the Banks' closest descendant; the Board is the federal agency Thomas says the Banks never were.*
- **Cook was appointed to the Board in 2022 to finish an unexpired term and confirmed in 2023 to a full term running to 2038.** *A fixed term triggers the 1901 rule that notice and hearing are essential.*
- **August 2025: the Director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency posted a letter to the Attorney General accusing Cook of claiming two homes as her principal residence in 2021 to obtain better mortgage terms.** *The conduct alleged predates her service, which raises whether pre-office conduct can be cause at all.*
- **Within half an hour the President posted that Cook "must resign, now!!!"** *The only notice she ever received, and the ground of decision: it stated no charge and invited no response.*
- **Days later he purported to remove her for cause, citing "deceitful and potentially criminal conduct" that at a minimum showed "gross negligence."** *Alleged misconduct rather than policy, so the Court cannot treat this as a disguised policy firing and must say what "cause" means.*
- **Cook sued; the District Court preliminarily enjoined the removal, and the D.C. Circuit declined to stay the injunction, Judge Katsas dissenting.** *The procedural rationale the Court adopts was already the narrow ground below.*
- **The Government applied for a stay, the Court heard argument on January 21, 2026, and denied the stay on June 29, 2026, the day it decided Slaughter.** *Only likelihood of success is decided, so whether Cook may lawfully be removed for cause remains open.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (5–4; Roberts, C.J., for the Court, joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh and Jackson; Kavanaugh and Jackson concurring; Thomas dissenting; Alito, joined by Gorsuch, dissenting; Barrett dissenting)

- **Constitutional.** "The protection from removal enjoyed by Governors of the Federal Reserve is consistent with the Constitution."
- **Stay denied.** None of the Government's arguments is likely to prevail; each "would in effect transform the Federal Reserve's for-cause protection into at-will employment."
- **No process, so no valid removal.** The President "failed to afford Cook the procedural protections to which she was entitled by statute," making the removal "erroneous and void"; the due process claim is not reached.

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court — the history (Part I-A)**

- **The problem.** The Continental Congress printed so much money to finance the war that its currency was "[n]ot worth a Continental"; Washington warned of "mutiny." Congress "would have to tie its own hands" by placing the power to create money in an entity that could be "a principal Pillar of American Credit . . . by the ties of private interest" (Robert Morris).
- **The First Bank (1791).** Chartered at Hamilton's urging, part-owned by the Government but "run by directors accountable only to private stockholders." Hamilton's Report on a National Bank: confidence requires "a private not a public Direction" able to resist "the temptations of momentary exigencies," because "suspicion" of political manipulation "would continually corrode the vitals of the credit of the Bank."
- **The Second Bank (1816).** After the First Bank's charter lapsed in 1811, financial instability following the War of 1812 "convinced those who were most prejudiced against the measure of its necessity" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]], Marshall, C.J.). Madison, once "an ardent foe," supported it. Only five of its twenty-five directors were appointed and removable by the President.
- **Jackson.** Independence "was to prove its downfall": Jackson called the Bank a "[h]ydra of corruption" and vetoed recharter in 1832, refusing "to make the rich richer and the potent more powerful."
- **The panics and the Fed.** Without a central bank came the panics of 1837, 1857, 1873, 1893 and 1907 — a system "devised for fair weather, not for storms." The Fed followed in 1913, restructured in 1933 and 1935, "[m]odelled upon our federal political system" (Carter Glass).

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court — the answer to Thomas**

- **Founders as authority.** The Court disagrees with Thomas, "as did 'the founders of our Government and framers of our Constitution' when they 'were actively participating in public affairs'" — quoting *Myers*, the founding case for presidential removal, to ground the exception to it.
- **Lineage.** Knowing the "calamities that could arise from even the 'suspicion' of political manipulation of monetary policy," the founders guaranteed the First Bank's independence, their successors the Second's, and both served as the "great regulating wheel" of the early financial system.
- **Level of generality.** The tradition "has not stood still," and the Fed is "more powerful than its predecessors," but the central bank need not be "trapped in amber" (*Rahimi*; *Noel Canning*). It must remain "consistent with the principles that underpin" the Banks, and it "maintains the 'balance struck by the founding generation'" under "modern circumstances."
- **History's role.** The Court looks to history "not as an end in itself" but to give "essential content to undefined provisions in the frame of our government" (*Youngstown*, Frankfurter, J., concurring).
- **Stability.** The Court will not "sow doubt as to the status of one of our Nation's (and the world's) most important financial institutions," or unsettle this "special arrangement sanctioned by history" — Alito's phrase from his *CFPB v. Community Financial Services* dissent, turned against his dissent here.

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court — the statute (not in the excerpt)**

- **Reviewability and cause.** Congress neither made Governors removable at will nor barred review, and it legislated "against the backdrop of the common law." Because "the appearance of independence is key to the Federal Reserve's design," a lower threshold would let any "alleged misstep" supply "a ready pretext."
- **Process.** Notice and hearing "are essential" where "the term of office is for a fixed period," though only notice and "an opportunity to be heard in his defense" are required, not a trial.

**Thomas, J., dissenting**

- **Article II admits no exception.** Limits on removing executive officers "are unconstitutional," and the principle "admits no exceptions": "[t]he Constitution places all Executive power in the hands of the President" ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]]). The Court has not upheld a removal restriction for a principal officer since *Wiener* (1958), which rested on Humphrey's "philosophy."
- **The Board is executive.** It makes rules with the force of law (Cook can change debit-card fees), imposes penalties and assessments, examines private books and has issued orders banning individuals from banking. So "[t]he President . . . may remove Cook for any reason that he wants and by any procedure that he wants."
- **The analogy fails.** "[T]he First and Second Banks were banks with no executive power, whereas the Board is unquestionably a federal agency that wields considerable executive power." The Board "is not a 'bank'" but a novel federal agency, and the Court "does not deny that the First and Second Banks of the United States exercised no executive power."
- **Statute (not in the excerpt).** "Cook's office was not her 'property'"; apparent mortgage fraud was "a 'cause' to remove Cook," and the statute "says nothing about notice or a hearing."

**Alito, J., dissenting (joined by Gorsuch, J.), and Barrett, J., dissenting** (not in the excerpt)

- As the majority describes them, Alito would not rest on the statute when the courts below ruled on due process alone; Barrett objects to discussing the Fed's constitutionality at all.

## Arguments rejected

- *(Thomas) The Board is an executive agency, the Banks were not, and Article II requires at-will removal.* Roberts: the tradition "has not stood still," the central bank is not "trapped in amber," and the Fed keeps the founding generation's principle that monetary policy be free of political interference.
- *"For cause" commits the judgment to the President alone.* Roberts: there is no such "textual commitment," and the common law treated the "sufficiency and reasonableness of the cause of removal" as "questions for the courts."
- *"Cause" means any concern about fitness, excluding only "mere policy disagreement."* Roberts: the common law required cause "substantial, reasonable and just," and never "suggested that any reason would do."
- *(Cook) "Cause" incorporates only the causes other statutes recognized in 1935, so pre-office conduct cannot qualify.* Roberts: the statute says "cause," a common-law concept, not "causes that Congress has otherwise recognized as adequate."
- *Equity cannot interfere with removals, so backpay is all a court can give.* Roberts: those cases bar equity from deciding title finally, not from protecting officers de facto "pending a litigation."
- *The President's social-media post gave Cook notice and a chance to be heard.* Roberts: the post "did not suggest that a response from Cook would be appropriate." "That will not do."

## Where it sits

**The reservation, taken up.** Cook answers the question [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]] Part III-B left open. The reservation goes back to *Seila Law* n.8, and the 2025 order in *Trump v. Wilcox* had already called the Fed a "uniquely structured," "quasi-private" entity in the First and Second Bank tradition.

**A different five.** Kavanaugh and the three Slaughter dissenters join Roberts, while Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch and Barrett, all in the Slaughter majority, dissent. The unit ends with two lineups and two methods: text and structure for the FTC, history for the Fed.

**Banks, and McCulloch.** The history runs from the Bank of North America through the First Bank (1791) and the Second Bank (1816) to Jackson's veto of recharter, by way of Marshall's account in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] of why Congress revived the Bank. The institution that tests the Necessary and Proper Clause in Classes 16–17 supplies the Article II exception here.

**Rahimi's method, moved to Article II.** The "trapped in amber" and "principles that underpin" language comes from the Second Amendment's history-and-tradition test. Cook applies the same level-of-generality move to a structural question.

**What Humphrey's never reached.** Roosevelt's firing of Humphrey for policy disagreement remains the paradigm of what a for-cause protection forbids, and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] stopped there. Cook adds judicial review of the cause given, a substantive threshold, and pre-removal process.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the Canvas excerpt, which is built around the Article II question.

- **Why does the Fed survive when the FTC does not?** Not because the Board exercises no executive power — Thomas's list of its powers goes unanswered — and not because independence is good policy. The answer is historical: the First Congress chartered a bank insulated from the President, and *Myers* treated the founders' practice "when actively participating in public affairs" as the best evidence of Article II's meaning.
- **What is the strongest objection?** Thomas's: the Banks were private corporations with no executive power, and the Board is a federal agency with a great deal of it. Thomas wins on the facts; the majority wins only if the relevant tradition is the *function* insulated (monetary policy) rather than the *kind of entity* insulated. Be ready to say which level of generality you would pick and why.
- **Is the Rahimi move legitimate here?** Rahimi lets a modern law match a founding-era principle without a "historical twin." Cook imports that into Article II. Pressure points:
    - Slaughter reads text and structure at a high level of generality ("all" executive power); Cook reads history at a high level of generality to carve out an exception to that rule.
    - If principles, not particulars, govern, why does the principle stop at monetary policy? Workplace safety and nuclear energy also suffer from "suspicion" of political manipulation.
    - Sotomayor's Slaughter dissent asks exactly these questions: how close an analogue must be, whether "quasi-private" status matters, and what happens when Congress adds powers such as $1 million-a-day civil penalties.
- **Is the exception about the Board or the system?** The FOMC sets monetary policy; the Board supervises banks, writes rules and enforces them. The Banks' tradition maps well onto monetary policy and poorly onto bank supervision. Hypo: Congress transfers all bank supervision to the Board and adds civil penalties — is the Board still "in this lineage"?
- **How does this sit with McCulloch?** [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] upheld the Second Bank as a necessary and proper means of executing federal powers, which cuts toward Thomas: an instrument for executing the laws should answer to the executive. Cook instead treats the Bank as a model of insulation, and cites McCulloch for the Bank's necessity.
- **What about Slaughter's own premises?** [[The Federalist No. 70]]'s objection to plurality applies to a seven-member Board as much as to a five-member Commission, and [[The Decision of 1789]] concerned the executive departments. The majority's move is that 1789 and the Bank of 1791 must be read together, as the same First Congress's exposition.
- **Who is quoted against whom?** The majority grounds its exception in *Myers*, the case for at-will removal, and in Alito's own "special arrangement sanctioned by history." Slaughter does the same with *Ramos* against Sotomayor. Each side borrows the other's authority, which makes these quotations good exam currency.
- **Is the constitutional holding dictum?** The Government conceded constitutionality and the ground of decision was statutory process, so the question is fair. Alito and Barrett objected to deciding Article II on a stay application.
- **What was the actual ground of decision?** Statutory procedure. Be precise: the Court did not hold that Cook cannot be removed, or that mortgage fraud is not cause.
- **Vary the facts.**
    - Written notice of the charges and a week to respond, then removal: the defect is cured, and serious fraud with a thin nexus to monetary policy is a close call.
    - Removal for voting against a rate cut: policy disagreement, which even the Government conceded is not cause.
    - In-office insider trading on FOMC information: the nexus is strong, so cause is likely.
    - The same process applied to an FTC commissioner: [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]] makes cause irrelevant, because the protection is void.
- **Formalism or functionalism?** Cook's constitutional reasoning is historical, with a strong functional undertone of credibility and stability. Thomas calls that an "ahistorical analogy"; the defense is that a historical exception is formalist if the history is right.
- **Memorable lines.**
    - "[N]ot worth a Continental" and "trapped in amber" (Cook, Roberts).
    - "[A] private not a public Direction" (Hamilton, Report on a National Bank).
    - "[F]or any reason that he wants and by any procedure that he wants" (Cook, Thomas dissenting).
    - "Cook must resign, now!!!" (the President's post).
