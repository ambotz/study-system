## What it is

Optional pre-quarter background, pp. 42–44: the casebook's article-by-article overview of the Constitution and a summary of the amendments. It is meant to be read against the text at [[The Constitution of the United States|p. 1]].

## What to notice

- **Preamble.** States the purposes and the authority: popular sovereignty, "We the People."
- **Article I.** Congress's composition, the selection and qualifications of members, the rules for lawmaking, and "all legislative powers herein granted," which makes a government of limited, enumerated powers. § 8 lists the powers, § 9 the limits on Congress, § 10 the limits on the states.
- **Article II.** "The executive Power" in "a President": a **single, independent** chief executive, not a plural executive or a prime minister. The House-selection backup was expected by some founders to be used most of the time. Some powers are shared with the Senate (treaties, appointments); some are the President's alone (pardons). The President and all civil officers are impeachable, unlike the English king.
- **Article III.** "The judicial Power" in a Supreme Court and such lower courts as Congress creates; appointment with Senate confirmation and life tenure; limited to "cases" and "controversies." Congress's control over jurisdiction is disputed. Judicial review is not mentioned but is implicit in supremacy and in the judges' duty to follow supreme law.
- **Article IV.** Interstate relations: full faith and credit, privileges and immunities, return of fugitives (and originally fugitive slaves), the territories, new states, and the guarantee of a republican form of government. Disputes over Article IV led to Dred Scott and contributed to the Civil War.
- **Article V.** The only constitutional route to constitutional change, deliberately hard: proposal by two-thirds of both houses or by a convention called by two-thirds of the states, then ratification by three-fourths. Two entrenched provisos: equal Senate suffrage and the slave-trade clause before 1808.
- **Article VI.** "[T]he supreme Law of the Land," binding state judges "notwithstanding" contrary state law, and an oath "to support this Constitution" for federal **and state** officers.
- **Article VII.** Effective on ratification by nine states.
- **The Bill of Rights.** The First Congress proposed twelve amendments; ten were ratified. The Ninth protects unenumerated rights; the Tenth confirms limited, enumerated federal power. Another of the twelve became the Twenty-seventh Amendment.
- **Later structural amendments.** The Eleventh (1798) reversed a Supreme Court decision and removed suits against a state by citizens of another state. The Twelfth (1804) separated the votes for President and Vice President after the Jefferson–Burr tie of 1800. The Fourteenth is now understood to apply most of the Bill of Rights to the states.
