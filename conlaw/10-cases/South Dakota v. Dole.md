## Snapshot

Congress withheld 5% of federal highway funds from any State that let people under twenty-one buy or publicly possess alcohol. South Dakota, which allowed nineteen-year-olds to buy weak beer, sued and lost in both courts below. The Court affirmed, 7–2, upholding the condition as a valid exercise of the spending power and declining to decide whether Congress could legislate a drinking age directly. The spending power and § 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment are in play.

## Issue

The casebook asks "what is the scope of Congress's power to attach strings" to federal money.

- Is 23 U.S.C. § 158 a valid exercise of the spending power?
- Does § 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment operate as an independent constitutional bar?
- Is the loss of 5% coercive rather than merely persuasive?
- **Argued, not decided.** Could Congress legislate a drinking age directly?

## Rule

Congress may buy with conditional grants what it could not command directly. Four limits apply: the spending must serve the general welfare; the condition must be unambiguous, germane to the federal interest in the program, and not a direction to the State to act unconstitutionally. Beyond them lies an outer boundary where "pressure turns into compulsion."

- "Congress may attach conditions on the receipt of federal funds" to "further broad policy objectives by conditioning receipt of federal moneys upon compliance . . . with federal statutory and administrative directives."
- The expenditure must be "in pursuit of 'the general welfare,'" but "courts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress."
- Congress "must do so unambiguously . . . , enabl[ing] the States to exercise their choice knowingly, cognizant of the consequences of their participation" (*Pennhurst*).
- The fourth limit bars only conditions inducing "activities that would themselves be unconstitutional."

## Operative facts

- **South Dakota permitted persons nineteen or older to buy beer containing up to 3.2% alcohol.** *Without a drinking age below twenty-one the State has nothing to complain of, and the whole case rests on this one provision of state law.*
- **A Presidential commission found that differing state drinking ages gave young people "an incentive to drink and drive" across state lines.** *The empirical link between the condition and safe interstate travel, and the only support the germaneness holding really rests on.*
- **In 1984 Congress enacted the National Minimum Drinking Age Amendment, 23 U.S.C. § 158, directing the Secretary of Transportation to withhold highway funds otherwise allocable to any State in which purchase or public possession of alcohol by a person under twenty-one was lawful.** *Congress conditioned money rather than legislating a drinking age, which is what lets the Court reserve the Twenty-first Amendment question.*
- **The withholding was 5% of the money available under specified highway grant programs.** *The number that decides coercion. Change it and the case changes, as [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] later shows.*
- **The condition governed state legislation rather than the use of the money.** *O'Connor's whole case: a requirement that does not specify how the funds are spent is a regulation, not a condition.*
- **By the time the case reached the Court the statute had been conspicuously effective, and South Dakota offered that success as proof that no State could refuse.** *An argument from magnitude that the Court declines to turn into a legal test, and the seed of the coercion inquiry that matures in NFIB.*
- **South Dakota sued the Secretary in federal district court for a declaratory judgment; the District Court rejected its claims and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.** *No facts were in dispute, so the case arrives as a pure question of congressional power.*
- **South Dakota conceded that the condition was not "unrelated to a national concern in the absence of the Twenty-first Amendment," and both parties briefed the Amendment rather than the spending power.** *The concession disposes of germaneness for the majority, and O'Connor spends several paragraphs denying it was one.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (7–2; Rehnquist, C.J., for the Court; O'Connor and Brennan dissenting separately)

- **Valid under the spending power.** The expenditure serves the general welfare, the conditions "could not be more clearly stated," and the drinking age is "directly related to one of the main purposes for which highway funds are expended—safe interstate travel."
- **No independent constitutional bar.** Raising the drinking age "would not violate the constitutional rights of anyone."
- **Not coercive.** Losing 5% is "relatively mild encouragement," so coercion "is shown to be more rhetoric than fact" and enactment "remains the prerogative of the States not merely in theory but in fact."

**Rehnquist, C.J., for the Court**

- Chooses the narrower ground: Congress acted "indirectly under its spending power to encourage uniformity in the States' drinking ages," lawful "even if Congress may not regulate drinking ages directly."
- Starts from *United States v. Butler*'s Hamiltonian premise that the spending power "is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution."
- Rests germaneness on the State's concession and the highway program's safety purpose.
- Narrows the fourth limit by what the cases did rather than said: in *Oklahoma v. Civil Service Commission* a Tenth Amendment bar on regulating state employees' politics did not reach grant conditions.
- Treats coercion as a question of magnitude, answered by the 5% figure.

**O'Connor, dissenting**

- Accepts the framework, calling the split "a disagreement about the application of a principle rather than a disagreement on the principle itself."
- Breaks on germaneness: a minimum drinking age "is not sufficiently related to interstate highway construction to justify so conditioning funds appropriated for that purpose."
- Denies the concession: South Dakota said only that the Amendment removes the subject from federal power, conceding nothing about whether "the condition imposed by § 158 is reasonably related to highway construction."
- Calls § 158 "far too over and under-inclusive": it stops teenagers drinking nowhere near a highway, while "[e]ighty-four percent of all highway fatalities involving alcohol occur among those whose ages exceed 21."
- Offers a categorical limit: Congress may not "impose requirements on a grant that go beyond specifying how the money should be spent," so § 158 is "a regulation determining who shall be able to drink liquor."

**Brennan, dissenting**

- Rests on a different ground: the minimum age of liquor purchasers "falls squarely within the ambit of those powers reserved to the States by the Twenty-first Amendment," and "Congress cannot condition a federal grant in a manner that abridges this right."

## Arguments rejected

- *Congress may not buy what § 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment forbids it to regulate, or the reservation to the States is worthless.* The Court: "the constitutional limitations on Congress when exercising its spending power are less exacting than those on its authority to regulate directly."
- *The program's success proves no State could realistically refuse.* "We cannot conclude, however, that a conditional grant of federal money of this sort is unconstitutional simply by reason of its success in achieving the congressional objective."
- *A State already taxed for the money has no real choice about taking it back on Congress's terms.* Steward Machine: "to hold that motive or temptation is equivalent to coercion is to plunge the law in endless difficulties." The State keeps "the 'simple expedient' of not yielding."
- *A drinking age is not a specification of how highway money is spent, so the condition is not germane.* A shared purpose suffices: highway funds buy safe travel, which drunk teenage drivers crossing state lines defeat.

## Where it sits

**The line to NFIB.** *United States v. Butler* (1936) adopts Hamilton's reading of the spending power ([[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]]) over Madison's ([[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]]), then strikes the Agricultural Adjustment Act anyway; *Helvering v. Davis* (1937) drops Butler's restrictive half; Dole turns the settlement into a four-part test. Butler remained the last decision striking an Act as beyond the Spending Clause until [[NFIB v. Sebelius]].

**The workaround to anti-commandeering.** Under *New York v. United States* and [[Printz v. United States]] Congress could not order a State to enact a drinking age, yet Dole lets it buy the same statute. Printz made commandeering a categorical wrong, and Dole makes that wrong avoidable at a price. [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]] had already left the States to the political process.

**Germaneness as Necessary and Proper.** Asking whether a condition relates to "the federal interest in particular national projects or programs" is close to asking whether a means is adapted to executing the appropriation under [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].

**The founding-era problem, inherited.** [[The Savannah Fire Debate]] asked whether Congress could spend outside the enumeration; Dole answers yes and asks instead what Congress may demand in return. [[United States v. Lovett]] shows the fourth limit biting.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "The question presented . . . becomes whether Congress has the power to coerce states to pass their own 21-year-old drinking ages, by denying them highway funds if they do not."**

- Yes, once the Twenty-first Amendment is set aside.
- The framing leaves the structural question: anti-commandeering survives in form while Congress buys the identical statute.

**Note 2. "What are the constraints that the majority identifies for the spending power? . . . Which ones are more constraining, and which ones are less?"**

- General welfare, nearly toothless; unambiguous notice, from *Pennhurst*'s contract theory; germaneness, suggested without elaboration in *Massachusetts v. United States*; and the independent constitutional bar. Coercion sits outside the four, a caveat from *Steward Machine*.
- Only germaneness and coercion could ever constrain, and O'Connor agrees on the other three; Dole applies both so loosely that neither works.

**Note 3. "Is the question whether a spending condition is sufficiently related or germane simply another way of asking whether the condition is 'necessary and proper' for carrying into effect its highway appropriation?"**

- Both ask whether a means is closely enough connected to a legitimate end, so yes.
- The standard would then be [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]'s, which the majority's shared-purpose version falls short of and O'Connor's roughly matches.

**Note 4. "The Twenty-first Amendment thus gives every state an indefeasible right to be a dry state. . . . But is the converse true? Does the Twenty-first Amendment stop Congress from banning alcohol in states that want to allow it?"**

- No. The text reaches "transportation or importation into any State . . . in violation of the laws thereof," which protects state prohibition and says nothing about state permission.
- The Amendment repealed the Eighteenth's grant and left the commerce power intact — the Secretary's position, which would have made the case easy.

**Note 5. "Is it possible to draw a principled line? Is this a question of degree, or does it turn on the conceptual distinction between germane and non-germane conditions? . . . could Congress just redefine the purpose of its highway spending to get around O'Connor's rule?"**

- Rehnquist gives a percentage without a theory of why any percentage matters: a line, not a principled one.
- O'Connor trades degree for category, asking whether the requirement specifies how the money is spent; the test is administrable but forbids much ordinary conditional spending.
- Her rule probably does not survive a well-drafted preamble: relabel the program as funding safe interstate travel and a drinking age looks germane.

### Further drilling

- **Raise the 5%.** What happens at 20%, at all highway funds, at all federal funds? [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] answers at the far end, where the condition threatened a State's entire Medicaid grant.
- **The Twenty-first Amendment test case.** If every State has an indefeasible right to be dry, could Congress condition highway funds on repealing prohibition? The majority's rule appears to say yes — Brennan's objection.
- **Run the casebook's hypotheticals.** No Social Security for Arkansans unless the legislature repeals a state statute; none for Coloradans until recreational marijuana goes. Both fail germaneness and coercion.
- **Memorable lines.** Rehnquist: "pressure turns into compulsion." O'Connor: "a regulation determining who shall be able to drink liquor"; "It remains a Government of enumerated powers."
