## Rule

Congress may buy with conditional grants what it could not command directly, subject to four limits — the spending must serve the general welfare, the condition must be stated unambiguously, it must be related to the federal interest in the program, and it must not direct the State to do something independently unconstitutional — and to an outer boundary where financial inducement becomes so great that "pressure turns into compulsion."

## Facts

- South Dakota let persons 19 or older buy beer of up to 3.2% alcohol. *Without a drinking age below 21 the State has no case.*
- 23 U.S.C. § 158 directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold federal highway funds from States allowing purchase or public possession of alcohol by anyone under 21. *Congress conditioned money instead of legislating a drinking age, so the Twenty-first Amendment question could be reserved.*
- The withholding was 5% of the funds obtainable under specified highway grant programs. *The number that decides coercion; change it and the case changes.*
- Differing state drinking ages gave young people "an incentive to drink and drive" across state lines. *Links the condition to safe interstate travel.*
- South Dakota admitted the condition was not "unrelated to a national concern in the absence of the Twenty-first Amendment." *Disposes of germaneness for the majority; O'Connor denies it was a concession.*
- The condition governs state legislation, not the use of the money. *O'Connor's case: a requirement that does not specify how funds are spent is regulation.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (7–2; Rehnquist, C.J., for the Court)

- Incident to the taxing and spending power, "Congress may attach conditions on the receipt of federal funds," and may use them "to further broad policy objectives by conditioning receipt of federal moneys upon compliance by the recipient with federal statutory and administrative directives."
- Objectives outside Article I's "enumerated legislative fields" "may nevertheless be attained through the use of the spending power and the conditional grant of federal funds," because [[United States v. Butler|Butler]] settled that the spending power "is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution."
- **General welfare.** The expenditure must be "in pursuit of 'the general welfare'" but "courts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress," since "the concept of welfare or the opposite is shaped by Congress" ([[Helvering v. Davis]]).
- **Unambiguous notice.** Congress "must do so unambiguously . . . , enabl[ing] the States to exercise their choice knowingly, cognizant of the consequences of their participation" ([[Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman|Pennhurst]]). Here the conditions "could not be more clearly stated."
- **Germaneness.** Conditions "might be illegitimate if they are unrelated 'to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs'" ([[Massachusetts v. United States]]). Section 158 satisfies it: the condition is "directly related to one of the main purposes for which highway funds are expended—safe interstate travel."
- **Independent constitutional bar.** Other provisions may bar a conditional grant, but the limitation "is not . . . a prohibition on the indirect achievement of objectives which Congress is not empowered to achieve directly"; it means only "that the power may not be used to induce the States to engage in activities that would themselves be unconstitutional," such as "invidiously discriminatory state action or the infliction of cruel and unusual punishment." Raising the drinking age to 21 "would not violate the constitutional rights of anyone."
- **Coercion.** An inducement "might be so coercive as to pass the point at which 'pressure turns into compulsion'" ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis]]), but losing 5% is "relatively mild encouragement," and "the argument as to coercion is shown to be more rhetoric than fact."
- The Twenty-first Amendment question is reserved: "we need not decide in this case whether that Amendment would prohibit an attempt by Congress to legislate directly a national minimum drinking age."

**Rejected**

- *Congress may not use the spending power to purchase what § 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment forbids it to regulate directly; otherwise the Amendment's reservation to the States is worthless.* The Court answers that "the constitutional limitations on Congress when exercising its spending power are less exacting than those on its authority to regulate directly," and that in [[Oklahoma v. Civil Service Commission]] a Tenth Amendment limit on regulating state officials' political activity did not limit the conditions attachable to federal grants — the Federal Government "does have power to fix the terms upon which its money allotments to states shall be disbursed."
- *The program's near-universal success proves that no State could realistically refuse, and effectiveness of that order is coercion.* "We cannot conclude, however, that a conditional grant of federal money of this sort is unconstitutional simply by reason of its success in achieving the congressional objective."
- *A State that has already been taxed for the money has no genuine choice about taking it back on Congress's terms.* [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]: "[E]very rebate from a tax when conditioned upon conduct is in some measure a temptation. But to hold that motive or temptation is equivalent to coercion is to plunge the law in endless difficulties." The alternative is "the acceptance of a philosophical determinism by which choice becomes impossible." The State retains "the 'simple expedient' of not yielding."
- *The condition is not germane: a drinking age is not a specification of how highway money is spent.* The Court treats germaneness as satisfied by a shared purpose — highway funds are spent for safe interstate travel, and drunk teenage drivers crossing state lines make travel unsafe — and leans on South Dakota's concession.

## Context

Dole is the last stop before [[NFIB v. Sebelius]], and the whole unit is built to get there. The doctrinal line runs: [[United States v. Butler]] (1936) adopts Hamilton's reading of the spending power from [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]] and rejects Madison's from [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]], then strikes the Agricultural Adjustment Act anyway on the ground that the tax, the appropriation and the disbursement "are but means to an unconstitutional end"; [[Helvering v. Davis]] (1937) upholds Old Age Benefits, declares the contest "now settled by decision," and quietly ignores Butler's restrictive half; Dole (1987) converts the settlement into a four-part test for conditions. O'Connor's observation is worth keeping: Butler is "the last case in which this Court struck down an Act of Congress as beyond the authority granted by the Spending Clause" — a record that stood until the Medicaid expansion holding in [[NFIB v. Sebelius|NFIB]].

The reason conditional spending matters structurally is that it is the workaround to anti-commandeering. Note 1 makes this explicit: under [[New York v. United States]] and [[Printz v. United States]] Congress could not simply order a State to enact a 21-year-old drinking age, yet Dole lets it buy the same statute. [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] had left the States' protection to the political process; [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] pulled back and made commandeering a categorical wrong; Dole makes that wrong avoidable at a price. If the price can always be met, the anti-commandeering rule is a drafting rule.

The germaneness limit is a Necessary and Proper Clause question wearing different clothes (Note 3). Asking whether a condition is "related to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs" is close to asking whether the means is adapted to executing the appropriation under [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]. The majority's version is satisfied by a shared ultimate purpose, which is weaker than McCulloch's already-generous test; O'Connor's version, requiring that the condition affect how the funded program works, is closer to it.

Dole also inherits the founding-era problem the casebook has been building. [[The Savannah Fire Debate]] asked whether Congress could spend on an object outside the enumeration; Dole answers that it can, and asks instead what it may demand in return. And Madison's fourth objection in the Bonus Bill veto — that general-welfare questions are "unsusceptible of judicial cognizance and decision" — is vindicated by the majority's treatment of the first limit as near-automatic.

Compare the appropriations material in Class 5. [[United States v. Lovett]] is the mirror image: Congress attaching a condition to an appropriation to accomplish against three named individuals what it could not enact as substantive law, and the Court striking it under an independent constitutional bar — the Bill of Attainder Clause. Lovett is what Dole's fourth limit looks like when it actually bites.

## Class layer

### Posture

- South Dakota sued the Secretary of Transportation in federal district court for a declaratory judgment that 23 U.S.C. § 158 exceeded the spending power and violated the Twenty-first Amendment.
- The District Court rejected the State's claims and the Eighth Circuit affirmed; the Supreme Court affirmed. No facts were in dispute, so the case arrives as a pure question of congressional power.

### Facts

South Dakota permitted nineteen-year-olds to buy beer containing up to 3.2% alcohol. In 1984 Congress enacted the National Minimum Drinking Age Amendment, 23 U.S.C. § 158, directing the Secretary of Transportation to withhold federal highway funds otherwise allocable to any State in which purchase or public possession of alcohol by a person under twenty-one was lawful. The withholding was 5% of the money available under specified highway grant programs. Congress acted on the finding of a Presidential commission that differing state drinking ages drew young people across state lines to drink and drive home. By the time the case reached the Court the statute had been conspicuously effective, which South Dakota offered as proof that no State could really refuse. Both parties spent most of their briefing on the Twenty-first Amendment rather than on the spending power.

### Issue

- Is 23 U.S.C. § 158 a valid exercise of the spending power under the limits on conditional grants?
- Does § 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment operate as an "independent constitutional bar" to conditioning highway funds on a State's minimum drinking age?
- Is the withholding of 5% of certain highway funds so coercive that it exceeds the spending power?

### Holding

- Yes. The expenditure serves the general welfare, the condition is unambiguously stated, and it is germane because a uniform drinking age addresses an obstacle to safe interstate travel, which is a main purpose for which highway funds are spent.
- No. The "independent constitutional bar" limit does not forbid buying indirectly what Congress could not command directly; it forbids only conditions that would induce a State to act unconstitutionally, and a State that raises its drinking age violates no one's rights. Whether Congress could impose a national drinking age directly is left undecided.
- No. Losing 5% of specified highway grants is "relatively mild encouragement," and enactment of drinking-age laws "remains the prerogative of the States not merely in theory but in fact."

### Rule

- Congress may attach conditions to federal funds and may thereby pursue objectives outside its "enumerated legislative fields."
- Four limits: the expenditure must be for the general welfare; the condition must be unambiguous; the condition must be related to the federal interest in the particular national project or program; and the condition must not violate an independent constitutional prohibition.
- The general-welfare limit is nearly toothless — "courts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress."
- The independent-bar limit means only that Congress may not "induce the States to engage in activities that would themselves be unconstitutional."
- A financial inducement may be invalid if it is "so coercive as to pass the point at which 'pressure turns into compulsion.'"

### Reasoning

**(Rehnquist, C.J., for the Court)**

- Decide the case on the spending power and leave the Twenty-first Amendment alone: "Here, Congress has acted indirectly under its spending power to encourage uniformity in the States' drinking ages," and that effort is "within constitutional bounds even if Congress may not regulate drinking ages directly."
- Start from [[United States v. Butler|Butler]]'s Hamiltonian premise that the spending power is not bounded by the enumeration, and treat conditional grants as an incident of it.
- Run the four limits in order and find the first three uncontested. On germaneness, rely on South Dakota's own statement and on the highway program's safety purpose, plus the commission's finding about border-crossing drinkers.
- Reject the petitioner's reading of the fourth limit by showing what the cases actually did: spending limits are "less exacting" than regulatory limits ([[United States v. Butler|Butler]]), and a Tenth Amendment objection to regulating state employees' politics did not carry over to grant conditions ([[Oklahoma v. Civil Service Commission]]).
- Treat coercion as a question of magnitude, answered by the 5% figure, and refuse to infer coercion from the statute's success.
- Borrow [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]'s framing to keep temptation and compulsion apart, on the ground that equating them would "plunge the law in endless difficulties."

### Dissent / concurrence

**(O'Connor, J., dissenting)**

- She accepts the framework and disputes only its application: "it is a disagreement about the application of a principle rather than a disagreement on the principle itself." She agrees the spending power "is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution," that there are "four separate types of limitations," that § 158 serves the general welfare and is unambiguous, and she assumes arguendo that the Twenty-first Amendment is no independent bar.
- Germaneness is where she breaks: "establishment of a minimum drinking age of 21 is not sufficiently related to interstate highway construction to justify so conditioning funds appropriated for that purpose."
- The supposed concession proves nothing. South Dakota's point was that the Twenty-first Amendment is what removes the subject from federal power; conceding a "national concern" in the Amendment's absence "does not . . . amount to a concession that the condition imposed by § 158 is reasonably related to highway construction," and counsel's statements at argument were "at best ambiguous."
- If the object is deterring drunk driving, § 158 "is far too over and under-inclusive" — over-inclusive because "it stops teenagers from drinking even when they are not about to drive on interstate highways," under-inclusive because "teenagers pose only a small part of the drunken driving problem," with eighty-four percent of alcohol-related highway fatalities occurring among those over 21.
- The limiting principle, from the National Conference of State Legislatures' brief: "Congress has no power under the Spending Clause to impose requirements on a grant that go beyond specifying how the money should be spent. A requirement that is not such a specification is not a condition, but a regulation, which is valid only if it falls within one of Congress' delegated regulatory powers." Applied here: "Rather than a condition determining how federal highway money shall be expended, it is a regulation determining who shall be able to drink liquor."
- "When Congress appropriates money to build a highway, it is entitled to insist that the highway be a safe one. But it is not entitled to insist as a condition of the use of highway funds that the State impose or change regulations in other areas of the State's social and economic life because of an attenuated or tangential relationship to highway use or safety."
- The consequence of the majority's rule: "the Congress could effectively regulate almost any area of a State's social, political, or economic life on the theory that use of the interstate transportation system is somehow enhanced." Her example is conditioning highway money on moving the state capital — "hardly more attenuated than the one which the Court finds supports § 158" — set against the United States' concession at argument that conditioning a grant on adoption of a unicameral legislature would fail germaneness.
- She rehabilitates [[United States v. Butler|Butler]]: its error "was not the Court's conclusion that the act was essentially regulatory, but rather its crabbed view of the extent of Congress' regulatory power under the Commerce Clause." The Agricultural Adjustment Act was regulation, and regulation that [[Wickard v. Filburn]] and [[Katzenbach v. McClung]] would now sustain. Butler's spending-power analysis "remain[s] sound."
- The stakes, quoting Butler: an unlimited spending power gives "power to the Congress to tear down the barriers, to invade the states' jurisdiction, and to become a parliament of the whole people, subject to no restrictions save such as are self-imposed."
- On the merits of the Twenty-first Amendment she would hold that regulating "the age of the purchasers of liquor, just as the regulation of the price at which liquor may be sold, falls squarely within the scope of those powers reserved to the States," so the commerce power supplies no alternative basis. She closes on enumeration: "It remains a Government of enumerated powers," citing [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].

**(Brennan, J., dissenting)**

- One paragraph, and a different ground: "regulation of the minimum age of purchasers of liquor falls squarely within the ambit of those powers reserved to the States by the Twenty-first Amendment. Since States possess this constitutional power, Congress cannot condition a federal grant in a manner that abridges this right."
- He is the only Justice who treats the Twenty-first Amendment as an independent constitutional bar — that is, he accepts the argument the majority defines away: "The Amendment, itself, strikes the proper balance between federal and state authority."

### Cold-call notes

- **What does the case actually hold, and what does it carefully not hold?** It holds that § 158 is a valid conditional exercise of the spending power. It does not hold that Congress could enact a national drinking age directly, and it does not decide the Twenty-first Amendment question. Note 1's instruction is to put the Amendment aside and ask the real question: whether Congress may coerce States into passing laws it could not require them to pass.
- **The four limits, in order, with the source of each.** General welfare — from the text of clause 1, and "courts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress." Unambiguous notice — from [[Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman|Pennhurst]], on a contract theory: the State must know what it is agreeing to. Germaneness — from [[Massachusetts v. United States]], and the Court concedes it was suggested "without significant elaboration." Independent constitutional bar — from the general principle that Congress cannot buy a constitutional violation. Coercion sits outside the four as a caveat, drawn from [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]. Note 2 asks which are constraining: only germaneness and coercion could ever be, and Dole applies both so loosely that neither is.
- **Raise the 5%.** This is the hypo lever. At 5%, coercion is "more rhetoric than fact." Ask what happens at 20%, at 50%, at all federal highway funds, at all federal funds of any kind. The answer the course gives is [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]: conditioning a State's entire existing Medicaid grant on accepting the expansion crossed the line, because the State faced loss of a program it had built its budget around rather than a marginal inducement. Dole supplies the standard; NFIB supplies the first application that found a violation. Be ready to say what distinguishes them — the size of the sum, and that Dole's condition attached to *new* money while the Medicaid condition put existing money at risk.
- **Is there a principled line on coercion at all?** Note 5 doubts it. Rehnquist gives a percentage without a theory of why any percentage matters; Steward Machine warns that treating temptation as coercion leads to "a philosophical determinism by which choice becomes impossible." The alternative is O'Connor's: abandon degree and decide by category, asking whether the requirement specifies how the money is spent. That is administrable but forbids a great deal of ordinary conditional spending.
- **What exactly divides Rehnquist and O'Connor on germaneness?** Note 5 states it precisely: the majority treats the condition as germane because highway appropriations and drinking-age laws serve the same general purpose, highway safety; O'Connor requires that the condition be relevant to making the funded program achieve its purposes, and "the efficacy, the marginal impact, of the highway spending is the same whether or not there is a 21-year-old drinking age." Her test asks about the money; his asks about the goal.
- **The objection to O'Connor's test.** Congress can redefine the purpose of its spending. If the program is relabeled as funding "safe interstate travel" rather than highway construction, a drinking age plausibly makes the program more effective. Ask whether her rule survives a well-drafted preamble — and whether any germaneness test can, given that Congress writes the findings.
- **Is germaneness just the Necessary and Proper Clause?** Note 3. Both ask whether a means is sufficiently connected to a legitimate end. If it is a necessary-and-proper question, then the standard should be [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]'s — a means plainly adapted to a legitimate end and consistent with the letter and spirit of the Constitution — and the majority's shared-purpose test is more permissive than even that. Also worth noticing: on the Madisonian view the spending power *is* an incident of the other powers, so the whole conditional-spending doctrine would collapse into Necessary and Proper.
- **Why is conditional spending the escape from anti-commandeering?** Under [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] and [[New York v. United States]] Congress may not conscript state legislatures or executives. Dole lets it pay for the same result. Ask what remains of the anti-commandeering rule: a rule that Congress must use money rather than commands, which matters only when Congress has no money to offer or the State is rich enough to refuse. Then ask whether [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]]'s reliance on the political process is a better fit for Dole than for Printz — the States that hate the condition are represented in the Congress that attached it.
- **Why does the Twenty-first Amendment argument fail, and is that right?** Rehnquist redefines the fourth limit so that it asks whether the State's *compliance* would be unconstitutional, not whether Congress's direct regulation would be. Raising a drinking age violates nobody's rights, so no bar. Brennan's answer is that the Amendment reserves the subject to the States, so a condition that removes the State's choice abridges a right the Constitution gave the State itself. The test case: if the Twenty-first Amendment gives every State an indefeasible right to be dry, could Congress condition highway funds on a State's *repealing* Prohibition? Under the majority's rule the answer appears to be yes, which is hard to square with the Amendment.
- **Note 4's converse question.** The Amendment plainly lets a State stay dry, so Congress cannot force it to allow sales. Does it also strip Congress of commerce power to ban alcohol in a State that wants it? The text — "transportation or importation into any State . . . in violation of the laws thereof, is hereby prohibited" — protects state prohibition laws and says nothing about state permission. The better reading is that the Amendment repealed the Eighteenth Amendment's grant but left the ordinary commerce power intact, which is the Secretary's position in Dole and which would have made the case easy.
- **The casebook's own hypotheticals are the exam version.** No Social Security checks for Arkansans unless the legislature repeals its religious freedom law; none for Coloradans until the State repeals recreational marijuana. Run each through the four limits: general welfare (satisfied), unambiguous (satisfied by drafting), independent bar (probably satisfied, since repealing a state statute violates no one's rights), germaneness (fails — retirement benefits have nothing to do with either subject), coercion (Social Security is far past 5%). Both hypotheticals fail on limits three and five, which shows where all the work is done.
- **What survives of [[United States v. Butler|Butler]]?** Its Hamiltonian holding on the scope of the spending power, cited by both Rehnquist and O'Connor. Its restrictive half — that Congress may not "indirectly accomplish" by taxing and spending what it cannot regulate directly — is exactly what Dole repudiates. Stone's dissent, that "[C]ourts are concerned only with the power to enact statutes, not with their wisdom" and that the sole limit is "that public funds shall be spent for a defined purpose, the promotion of the general welfare," is closer to current law than Roberts's majority.
- **Does Hamilton win completely?** Not quite. Hamilton's own qualification was that the object "be General and not local," and [[United States v. Butler|Butler]] reaffirmed that "the purpose" of the spending "must be general, and not local." [[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering]] admits that "[t]he line must still be drawn between one welfare and another, between particular and general," then hands the drawing to Congress: "The discretion, however, is not confided to the courts." The limit exists and is unenforceable — which is what [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill|Madison]] predicted in 1817.
- **Memorable lines.** Rehnquist: "pressure turns into compulsion"; "more rhetoric than fact"; "relatively mild encouragement"; the "simple expedient" of not yielding. O'Connor: "it is a regulation determining who shall be able to drink liquor"; "a parliament of the whole people, subject to no restrictions save such as are self-imposed"; "It remains a Government of enumerated powers."
