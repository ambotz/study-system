## What it is

Article VI of the Constitution, three short unnumbered clauses at the end of the operative text: the assumption of the Confederation's debts, the Supremacy Clause, and the oath-and-no-religious-test clause. It is the shortest article that does real work, and it is the hinge between the federal government's powers and the States' obligations.

The article is assigned twice in the course. Class 13 uses the oath as the textual basis for departmentalism, in [[Cooper v. Aaron]] and the Lincoln materials. Class 27 returns to it as its own subject, with [[In re Neagle]] as the case: a federal officer immunized from state criminal law for executing a duty no statute imposed, which is the Supremacy Clause operating on executive action rather than on a statute.

## What to notice

- **The Debts Clause is not dead letter, and it is the reason the article opens where it does.** "All Debts contracted and Engagements entered into, before the Adoption of this Constitution, shall be as valid against the United States under this Constitution, as under the Confederation." The new government succeeds to the old one's obligations, which answers in one sentence the objection that ratification would repudiate the Revolutionary debt. Note what it implies about the nature of the change: the Constitution replaced a government, and a replacement that carries the debts forward is not a new compact among the States but a reorganization of one continuing people. Set it against the compact theory in [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]] and against [[Texas v. White]].
- **The Supremacy Clause names three kinds of supreme law, and qualifies them differently.** "This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties made, or which shall be made, under the Authority of the United States, shall be the supreme Law of the Land." The Constitution is supreme without qualification; statutes are supreme only if "made in Pursuance thereof"; treaties are supreme if made "under the Authority of the United States," which was drafted to cover treaties predating the Constitution and so cannot mean "in pursuance of" it.
- **"[I]n Pursuance thereof" is the best textual argument for judicial review.** A statute that is not made in pursuance of the Constitution is not supreme law, so a court applying the supreme law must decide whether a statute qualifies. That is Marshall's structural point in [[Marbury v. Madison]], and it is stronger than the Arising Under Clause because it speaks to what the judges must apply rather than to what cases they may hear. The counterargument is that the clause is addressed to state judges, not federal ones, and settles a conflict-of-laws question rather than conferring a power of review.
- **The clause binds judges, and only judges, by name.** "[A]nd the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding." State judges must apply federal law over their own State's constitution — the founding-era answer to the Confederation's failure, where federal law reached the States as sovereigns rather than their courts. [[Printz v. United States]] builds a whole doctrine on the omission: Article VI names state judges and says nothing about state executives, which Scalia reads as confirming that Congress may not commandeer state executive officers even though it may direct state courts.
- **The "any Thing . . . to the Contrary notwithstanding" language is a rule of priority, not of preemption doctrine.** It tells a state judge which law wins in a conflict; it does not say how large the field of federal law is. Every preemption fight is therefore really a fight about the scope of the federal statute or of the enumerated power behind it, which is why [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] spends its energy on the Necessary and Proper Clause and only then reaches Maryland's tax.
- **Neagle extends supremacy from statutes to executive action.** [[In re Neagle]] holds that "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible" from the Constitution is a "law of the United States," so a marshal protecting a Justice under no statute is protected from a state murder prosecution. Notice the strain: the Supremacy Clause's own vocabulary makes "Laws of the United States" the things "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution through a procedure, which is closer to Justice Lamar's reading of "law" than to Justice Miller's.
- **The oath binds state officers as well as federal ones, and executive as well as judicial.** "The Senators and Representatives before mentioned, and the Members of the several State Legislatures, and all executive and judicial Officers, both of the United States and of the several States, shall be bound by Oath or Affirmation, to support this Constitution." Every officer in the country, state or federal, is personally obligated to the federal Constitution. The clause is what makes the federal system run through the same people who run the States, and it is the reason the Constitution needed no federal enforcement machinery in every county.
- **The President's oath is not this one.** Article II, § 1 prescribes the President's oath separately, in its own words ("preserve, protect and defend"). Article VI's list — senators, representatives, state legislators, executive and judicial officers of both governments — conspicuously omits him, because his oath is already provided for. That the Constitution bothers to swear everyone twice over is the strongest evidence that the oath was meant to do independent work.
- **The oath is the textual seed of departmentalism.** The officer swears to "support this Constitution," not to obey the Supreme Court. [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] makes the argument in its sharpest form: each officer swears to support the Constitution "as he understands it," so the President may veto a bank bill the Court has upheld. Lincoln makes the same move against [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]. [[Cooper v. Aaron]] is the answer, treating the oath as binding officials to the Court's interpretation and citing this clause to do it. Be ready to say that Cooper's use of Article VI is a move, not a reading: the clause names the Constitution as the object of the oath and says nothing about who expounds it.
- **Departmentalism is not nullification, and the distinction is in the oath.** An officer's independent constitutional judgment governs what he does in the exercise of his own powers — vetoing, prosecuting, pardoning, refusing to defend a statute. It does not authorize defying a judgment in a case. The Class 13 materials draw the line; [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] is where it lives doctrinally.
- **No Religious Test, and what it tells you about the rest of the document.** "[B]ut no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States." It applies only to federal office, it applies to every office and "public Trust," and it is one of the few individual-rights provisions in the original Constitution, four years before the First Amendment. The "ever" is unusual drafting: the clause is written as a permanent prohibition rather than as an ordinary limit. Note the pairing with the oath in the same sentence — the Framers required a promise of fidelity to the Constitution in the same breath in which they forbade a promise about God, which is the clearest statement in the document that the government's authority is not religious in origin.
- **What the article does not say.** It does not say who decides whether a statute is "in Pursuance thereof"; it does not bind state executive officers by name; it does not say what happens when two officers sworn to the same Constitution read it differently. Each silence is a question the course answers elsewhere — the first in [[Marbury v. Madison]], the second in [[Printz v. United States]], the third in [[Cooper v. Aaron]] and [[In re Neagle]].
