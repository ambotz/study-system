## What it is

The House debate of December 28, 1796 (6 Annals of Congress 1712–1727) on William Smith's resolution to grant federal relief to the victims of the fire that destroyed Savannah, Georgia. "Two hundred and twenty-nine houses were destroyed, only one hundred and seventy-nine left standing; four hundred families were left quite destitute." The proposed sum was $15,000. The casebook prints it (4th ed., pp. 614–618) as the founding-era test case for federal disaster relief — the moment when the evasions Madison had used for the codfish subsidy and the St. Domingo refugees ran out and Congress had to vote on the spending power itself. Relief was defeated, 55–34. Congress had earlier refused relief to a smaller inland town in Virginia, which supplies the objectors with their strongest precedent.

## What to notice

- **Macon states the objection in the form the course will use all year.** "But he wished gentlemen to put their finger upon that part of the Constitution which gave that House power to afford them relief." Not that relief is unwise, but that no clause authorizes it.
- **Macon's second point is the line-drawing problem, and it is why the vote matters.** "If the United States were to become underwriters to the whole Union, where must the line be drawn when their assistance might be claimed? Was it when three-fourths or four-fifths of a town was destroyed, or what other proportion?" He adds the market answer: "Insurance offices were the proper securities against fire." And he has a comparison case ready — Lexington, Virginia, "as every house in the place was burnt" — which exposes the vote for Savannah as a choice among sufferers, not a principle.
- **Kitchell adds the moral-hazard and crowding-out argument.** "[I]f the General Government were only to give this sum, the State Legislatures would proportion their donations accordingly, and probably give much less than they would otherwise have done." Federal relief substitutes for state relief rather than adding to it, so "while we attempt to serve, we shall eventually injure."
- **Kitchell's aside is the best line in the debate on constitutional method.** He "thought the Constitution did not authorize them to make such a use of public money; however he thought it might be a very flexible instrument; it would bend to every situation, and every situation to that." A written constitution that bends to every case is not a constraint — said by a member watching his colleagues bend it.
- **Giles identifies what is actually being voted on.** "The small sum of $15,000 was not of any consequence when compared with the establishment of a principle of that House acting upon generosity. He believed that neither the money nor humanity, but the establishment of the principle, was the thing aimed at." And on oath: "he did not think they ought to attend to what 'the affairs of men' or what generosity and humanity required, but what the Constitution and their duty required." His prediction — "if the present resolution passed it would make them answerable for all future losses by fire" — is precisely what happened.
- **Nicholas defends the objectors against the charge of coldness.** "[A]n attempt had been made to ridicule the opinion which he and others held of the sacredness of the Constitution." He concedes the narrower point that "he had never heard it said that no individual instance could occur which might be an exception to a Constitutional rule," which gives up more than he seems to notice.
- **Smith's opening argument is the argument from prior practice, and it is a good one.** Congress "had granted assistance to the sufferers by fire at St. Domingo; and surely if it were justifiable to grant relief to foreigners in distress, it was at least equally so when the objects were our own citizens."
- **Smith's second run at the precedents shows every earlier grant was a characterization, not a principle.** The St. Domingo money was nominally "charged to the French Republic," but "[i]t was provisional, and the fact was they had not admitted it, and the United States paid it." The grant to the daughters of Count de Grasse "was said to be for services. Did the daughters perform any services? No, but the father did. But did the Constitution of the United States acknowledge any hereditary claim of this sort? He believed not. This was a mere pretence. It was an act of generosity." Add the recompense to those who suffered in the Western insurrection, the widow of Major Forsyth and the orphan children of Major Trueman. His offer to insert the words "in consideration of services performed," &c. is a dare: if the label is what makes it constitutional, here is the label.
- **Smith reframes the vote to avoid the line-drawing problem.** "He wished gentlemen would say whether no case of calamity could exist in which the United States ought to grant relief? He believed every one admitted such a case might occur. The question was, whether this was the case?" Once the objectors concede the extreme case, the disagreement becomes one of degree — Macon's point exactly, run in reverse.
- **Harper makes the Hamiltonian argument expressly.** "The present case might justly be included under the head of promoting the general welfare of the country." He then treats the constitutional objection as foreclosed by repetition: "since their doubts had been so frequently overruled, he hoped they should hear no more of them." That is liquidation by attrition, and it is the weakest form of the argument from practice — compare the demand in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] that practice be systematic, unbroken and unchallenged.
- **Harper's fallback is the revenue argument, and it is the one that could satisfy Madison.** Savannah was "the only considerable port except Charleston" in that quarter, at the mouth of a river watering a thousand square miles, and "[t]he average revenue of this city was $76,000." Rebuilding a customs port is spending in aid of the commerce and revenue powers, not benevolence. Claiborne runs the same argument — the money will come back "from the quick return the revenue would gain," and trading-houses in the back country were built "for the general welfare; for the support of trade, and the increase of the revenue."
- **Baldwin, the Georgian, argues against mechanical construction — and cites the Appropriations Clause to do it.** The use of a written constitution "and of that provision in it which declared that no money should be drawn from the Treasury but under appropriations made by law, was very manifest from the caution which it gave in the expenditure of public money"; "yet he believed it impossible to obtain absolute directions from it in every case." His list of unlisted-but-accepted spending — lighthouses, "the improvement of harbors, beacons, buoys, and public piers, establishing trading-houses with the Indians" — supports the conclusion that the Constitution "was not capable of being administered under so rigorous and mechanical a construction as had been sometimes contended for."
- **Baldwin's earthquake hypothetical is the founding-era version of the modern coercion hypo.** Suppose a state mostly or wholly on an island, thrown by "tremendous convulsions and earthquakes" into a condition where "not only the whole property of the wretched survivors destroyed, but their place no longer habitable" — "would the Federal Government think they had no powers even to grant them some of their new land as a place of refuge?" He concedes it is deliberately extreme: "He only wished to establish the principle that there were possible instances in which it would be the duty of the Federal Government to interpose relief." Note the escape hatch built into the hypothetical: relief in *land* rests on Article IV, § 3's power over federal property, not on the "general Welfare" clause at all.
- **Rutherford and Claiborne argue from sentiment, and say so.** Rutherford: telling the sufferers to wait "is pouring cold water upon their distresses; it is as though you were to say to a man that is drowning, stay awhile and we will come and assist you." Claiborne: "It was a sharp conflict between humanity to that suffering country and the Constitution," and Georgia "was a slaughter-pen during the war." Note 4 asks whether that candor is "inconsistent with the congressional oath of office" or "refreshing candor and decency." The sharper question is Giles's: if humanity decides, the enumeration decides nothing, and the member has voted his conscience with someone else's money and against his oath.
- **How would Madison and Hamilton have voted?** Note 1. Madison against, unless it could be recharacterized — and his own workarounds show he would have looked hard for one, most plausibly the customs-revenue theory Harper and Claiborne offered. Hamilton is the harder case: his own qualification requires that the object "be General and not local; its operation extending in fact, or by possibility, throughout the Union, and not being confined to a particular spot." Relief to one burned city is the paradigm of local. A Hamiltonian could get there only by generalizing the object — relief for national calamities wherever they occur, or protection of the customs revenue — which is the same aggregation move that made the lighthouse bill "general."
- **Note 2's question about military widows has a real answer.** Payments to the families of those who served are traceable to the war powers and to the government's own obligations — the pay of the army and debts incurred in the national service — so they need no free-standing benevolence power. Smith's rejoinder is that the de Grasse grant was not that, and that the sufferers at Savannah had rendered services too.
- **The sectional pattern makes the vote evidence of principle, not interest.** Northern representatives tended to be the Hamiltonians and Southern ones the opponents of federal spending, yet Savannah was a Southern city, and its champions here were Smith of South Carolina and Baldwin of Georgia. Currie's reading: "One has the sense the wily Federalists were hoping to slip this one by on sympathy grounds, only to employ it mercilessly as a precedent later on." Southern members voting against relief for a Southern city were voting against their constituents' interest, which makes the 55–34 result stronger evidence of the original understanding than a party-line vote would have been.
- **Note 5 turns the debate into a live question: earmarks.** Congress banned earmarking for a decade and has just begun lifting the ban. The Speedway gas station and pizza parlor secured by Rep. David Hobson in 2001 was defended as "vitally important" for a place "with hundreds of college students and no pizza delivery or nearby fast food options." On the Madisonian view, ask which enumerated power covers it; commerce is the only candidate and it is a stretch. On the Hamiltonian view, the object is the paradigm of local — and if earmarks flow to swing districts, the distribution is not general in any sense either. "[W]herever the line is between 'general' and 'local,' who should enforce it?" is Madison's justiciability point returning, and the answer after [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]] and [[Helvering v. Davis]] is: nobody.
