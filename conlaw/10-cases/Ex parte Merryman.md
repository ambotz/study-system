## Rule

Chief Justice Taney, sitting alone, held that only Congress may suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, so a President cannot suspend it himself or delegate that discretion to a military officer. The case is taught less for that ruling than for what followed: the President did not comply, and Taney conceded that his power had been "resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome," which poses the question whether the executive must obey a specific judicial judgment.

## Facts

- **Lincoln authorized General Scott, by private order, to suspend the writ in Maryland; no proclamation announced it.** *Suspension by the President, not Congress, is the whole merits question; Taney stresses that "No official notice has been given."*
- **Congress was not in session and could not safely meet in Washington.** *Necessity when Congress cannot act is Lincoln's best argument; Taney's opinion gives it no weight.*
- **Merryman, a civilian, was arrested at night on a military order "upon vague and indefinite charges, without any proof," and held at Fort McHenry.** *Detaining a civilian without process implicates rights Taney says even Congress "could not suspend."*
- **General Cadwalader refused to produce Merryman, citing the President's authorization, and service of the contempt attachment was refused at the fort.** *Refusal by the executive is what turns a habeas case into the test of judgment supremacy.*
- **Taney issued no further order; he filed the proceedings and sent them to the President.** *The remedy was left to the officer whose conduct was challenged, under the Take Care Clause.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Taney, C.J., as Circuit Justice, sitting alone)

- **Only Congress may suspend the writ.** Taney "had supposed it to be one of those points of constitutional law upon which there was no difference of opinion, and that it was admitted on all hands, that the privilege of the writ could not be suspended, except by act of congress."
- **The President cannot delegate suspension to the military.** The President claims "not only ... the right to suspend the writ of habeas corpus himself, at his discretion, but to delegate that discretionary power to a military officer, and to leave it to him to determine whether he will or will not obey judicial process."
- **The detention is unlawful.** Due process, the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement and the right to a speedy trial, "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend, have been disregarded and suspended, like the writ of habeas corpus, by a military order, supported by force of arms."
- **The court's power is exhausted.** Taney files the proceedings in the circuit court and sends a copy to the President, whose "constitutional obligation to 'take care that the laws be faithfully executed'" now requires him "to determine what measures he will take to cause the civil process of the United States to be respected and enforced."

**Rejected**

- *The Suspension Clause does not say who may suspend; the power is an emergency power; and when Congress cannot meet, only the President can use it to execute the laws against a rebellion.* (Lincoln's position, as Note 1 summarizes it.) Taney's answer, as the casebook summarizes it: the Clause sits in Article I, § 9, among limits on Congress, so suspension is a legislative power; and the writ historically existed to check the executive, so letting the executive suspend it would be absurd.
- *The officer was acting under the President's lawful orders and was entitled to await instructions.* Taney: the military cannot usurp "the authority which the constitution has confided to the judiciary department" upon "any pretext or under any circumstances," or citizens hold "life, liberty and property at the will and pleasure of the army officer in whose military district he may happen to be found." Taney allows only that the officer "may have misunderstood his instructions."

## Context

Merryman is the third of the Class 13 positions. [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] contested whether a Supreme Court opinion binds the political branches as a "political rule"; [[Cooper v. Aaron]] said it binds state officials. Merryman asks the narrowest and hardest question: must the President carry out a court's judgment in a specific case? The casebook's heading is "Ex parte Merryman and Specific Judgments."

Lincoln answered in his July 4, 1861 message to Congress without mentioning Taney or the writ. His defense was necessity ("are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?") and, more quietly, that "It was not believed that any law was violated." The formal legal defense came the next day in [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Attorney General Bates's opinion]], which denies that a judge at chambers may command the President at all.

Merryman is the Suspension Clause's application to the question Jackson flagged in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]: the Constitution's only express emergency provision is in Article I. In Youngstown terms, Lincoln acted with Congress absent (category two at best); Congress later authorized presidential suspension in 1863, moving later suspensions into category one. The same setting, with Congress not in session, frames [[The Prize Cases]] (Class 9), where the Court upheld Lincoln's blockade.

Taney is also the author of [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] (Class 12). Lincoln's refusal to comply was made against a Chief Justice he had already publicly declined to follow as a "political rule." [[Hamdi v. Rumsfeld]] (2004) and [[Boumediene v. Bush]] (2008) are the modern Suspension Clause cases.

The final Notes set out the debate between two casebook co-authors: Paulsen's "Merryman Power" of executive non-execution of judgments, and Baude's "judgment power," under which the President must obey judgments of a court that had jurisdiction. Baude reads Bates's opinion as resting on the lack of jurisdiction. The debate is set out in [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Petition for a writ of habeas corpus presented to Chief Justice Taney; the casebook reports the decision as 17 F. Cas. 144 (D. Md., April Term 1861) with the caption "Taney, Circuit Justice." Taney ordered the proceedings "filed and recorded in the circuit court of the United States for the district of Maryland." Bates treats the proceeding as one before "a judge, at chambers."
- Taney issued the writ to General Cadwalader, then an attachment for contempt; after both were resisted, he delivered this opinion from the bench on May 28, 1861, and transmitted it to the President.

### Facts

After Fort Sumter, Lincoln called for troops and blockaded Southern ports, and Virginia seceded. Maryland seemed poised to follow, leaving Washington surrounded, and a Baltimore mob attacked federal troops moving through the city. On April 27, 1861, Lincoln privately authorized General Winfield Scott to suspend the writ for the "public safety" if needed to move troops through Maryland or suppress secessionist violence; Congress could not safely be convened in Washington. Army officers arrested John Merryman, a farmer and county commissioner suspected of sabotaging bridges and railway lines, and held him at Fort McHenry. His counsel petitioned Taney, who ordered General Cadwalader to produce Merryman. Cadwalader sent a subordinate to explain the President's order and ask for a postponement; Taney issued an attachment for contempt, and service was refused at the fort.

### Issue

- May the President suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and delegate that discretion to a military officer?
- Is Merryman's detention by military order, without judicial process, lawful?
- What may the court do when its writ is refused by an officer acting on presidential authority?

### Holding

- No. Suspension is for Congress alone; the President possesses no such power under the Constitution, still less a power to leave it to a military officer whether to obey judicial process.
- No. The military arrest and detention violate due process, the Fourth Amendment and the speedy-trial right, which even Congress could not suspend.
- Nothing further by force. Taney has exercised "all the power which the constitution and laws confer upon me"; he transmits the record to the President and leaves it to him, under the Take Care Clause, to enforce the civil process.

### Rule

- The privilege of the writ "could not be suspended, except by act of congress."
- A President may not delegate to a military officer the choice whether to obey judicial process.
- Military arrest of a civilian without judicial process, where the courts are open, violates the Fourth and Fifth Amendments and the right to a speedy trial.

### Reasoning

**(Taney, Circuit Justice)**

- The return discloses a claim of presidential power never officially announced, "by proclamation or otherwise."
- Congress's exclusive power to suspend is treated as settled, "admitted on all hands." (The fuller textual and historical argument appears in the casebook only in Note 1's summary: the Clause's placement in Article I, § 9, and the writ's history as a check on the Crown.)
- Taney writes "plainly and fully" out of "a proper respect for the high office he fills," to show he has "not ventured to question the legality of his act, without a careful and deliberate examination."
- The Bill of Rights guarantees are "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend"; their suspension by military order means "the people of the United States are no longer living under a government of laws."
- "[M]y duty was too plain to be mistaken." Having been met with superior force, the court's role ends with a record and an appeal to the President's Take Care duty.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. Taney sat alone.

### Cold-call notes

- **What did Taney order, and what did Lincoln disobey?** Taney issued a writ and an attachment, both refused; his final opinion orders only that the record be filed and sent to the President. He did not issue a further order to release Merryman. The disobedience was of the writ and the attachment. Precision matters here because the judgment-supremacy question turns on what the court actually adjudged.
- **Who is right on the merits (Note 1)?** Taney's text-and-structure argument is strong: the Clause is in Article I, § 9, a list of limits on Congress, and the English writ was a check on the Crown. Lincoln's argument: the Clause is written in the passive voice and names no branch, the power is designed for emergencies, and only the President can act when Congress cannot meet. The better answer is Taney's on the ordinary case; Lincoln's strongest version is a temporary suspension until Congress convenes and can decide, which is the posture he was in when he reported to Congress on July 4, 1861.
- **Youngstown mapping.** Jackson in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] said the Suspension Clause is the Constitution's only emergency provision and that it sits in Article I. Lincoln acted with Congress absent (twilight at best); Congress's 1863 statute authorizing presidential suspension moved later suspensions into category one. Vary the fact: had Congress been in session and declined to act, Lincoln would have been at the lowest ebb.
- **Must the President obey a judgment he thinks wrong?** Three positions. Cooper-style supremacy: yes, and he must also follow the Court's reasoning. Judgment supremacy (Baude): yes if the court had jurisdiction, but the President need not adopt its reasoning beyond the case. Executive coordinacy (Paulsen's "Merryman Power"; Lincoln and Bates): no, if he concludes in good faith that the judgment is unconstitutional. Lincoln in 1858 held the second position toward Dred Scott; Lincoln in 1861 acted on the third.
- **Is Lincoln 1858 consistent with Lincoln 1861?** In 1858 he disclaimed resisting the judgment as to Dred Scott himself. In 1861 his officer refused a writ in a specific case. The distinction he could offer is Bates's: a judge at chambers lacks jurisdiction to command the President, and the matter is "political and not judicial." If that is right, 1861 is not disobedience of a valid judgment.
- **Would jurisdiction matter (Note 7)?** Bates stresses reasons Taney lacked jurisdiction: a judge at chambers cannot hear what is in substance "an appeal ... from a decision of the President," and the subject is political. If the President may refuse only judgments issued without jurisdiction, that is a narrower principle than general non-execution. Paulsen replies that "jurisdiction" is just another place to fight about the merits.
- **Hypotheticals from Note 2.** An order to stop fighting the war, to surrender Maryland, or to return people freed by [[The Emancipation Proclamation]]. These are designed to test whether judgment supremacy is tolerable at the extreme. The judgment-supremacy answer: the President must comply if the court had jurisdiction, and the remedies are appeal, appointments, and amendment. The coordinacy answer: the President's oath to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution" forbids executing an unconstitutional judgment.
- **Could Congress impeach either man (Note 3)?** Impeachment is the political branches' check in both directions: the House could treat Lincoln's non-compliance as a Take Care violation, or Taney's ruling as an abuse. Using impeachment against a judge for an interpretation, rather than misconduct, returns in [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (Class 15).
- **Federalist No. 78 (Note 5).** Hamilton said the judiciary "must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments." Read as a description, that is Merryman. Read as a design feature, it makes executive non-enforcement a deliberate check, which is Paulsen's reading; Baude reads it as consistent with a duty to enforce judgments within jurisdiction.
- **Memorable detail.** Taney sat as a circuit justice in Baltimore; the opinion says his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome." Lincoln's message never names him.
- **Forward link.** [[Trump v. United States]] (2024) treats the President's conclusive and preclusive powers as absolutely immune from criminal prosecution; Bates's claim that the President answers only to "the high court of impeachment" is the nineteenth-century ancestor of that position.
