> [!caution] Source check — The casebook cites the case as "D. Md." and says Taney ruled "from the bench," while Bates calls it "a judge, at chambers." The standard citation (C.C.D. Md.) is used here.

## Snapshot

Lincoln authorized a general by private order to suspend the writ in Maryland, soldiers arrested a secessionist sympathizer, and Taney, sitting alone as Circuit Justice, held on habeas that only Congress may suspend. The military refused his writ and his contempt attachment, so he filed the record and sent it to the President under the Take Care Clause. Article I, § 9, cl. 2 is in play, with the Fourth and Fifth Amendments behind it.

## Issue

The casebook's introduction asks: "Do the courts have the power to bind the president? Was President Lincoln required to release Merryman?"

- May the President suspend the privilege of the writ, and delegate that discretion to a military officer?
- Is Merryman's detention by military order, without judicial process, lawful?
- What may the court do when its writ is refused by an officer acting on presidential orders?

## Rule

Only Congress may suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, so a President cannot suspend it himself or delegate that discretion to a military officer, and a civilian may not be arrested and held on military order without judicial process. When the executive refuses the court's process, the court's power is exhausted: it records what happened and leaves enforcement to the President's Take Care duty.

- The privilege of the writ "could not be suspended, except by act of congress" — a point Taney "had supposed" was "admitted on all hands."
- A President may not "delegate that discretionary power to a military officer, and . . . leave it to him to determine whether he will or will not obey judicial process."
- Due process, the warrant requirement and the speedy-trial right are "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend."

## Operative facts

- **April 1861: after Fort Sumter, Lincoln called for troops and blockaded Southern ports; Virginia seceded, Maryland seemed poised to follow, and a Baltimore mob attacked federal troops in the city.** *The emergency that makes necessity an argument rather than a pretext.*
- **Congress could not safely be convened in Washington, and meeting elsewhere "would have been humiliating to the national government and might have strengthened the rebel cause."** *Necessity when Congress cannot act is Lincoln's best argument; Taney's opinion gives it no weight.*
- **April 27, 1861: Lincoln authorized General Scott, by private order, to suspend the writ for the "public safety" if he deemed it necessary to move troops through Maryland or suppress secessionist violence.** *Suspension by the President rather than Congress is the merits question, and Taney stresses that "No official notice has been given."*
- **Merryman, a farmer and county commissioner suspected of sabotaging bridges and railway lines, was arrested at night on a military order "upon vague and indefinite charges, without any proof," and held at Fort McHenry.** *Detaining a civilian without process implicates rights even Congress "could not suspend."*
- **His counsel petitioned Taney, who issued a writ to General Cadwalader to produce Merryman in Baltimore the next day.** *A specific judicial command to a military officer — the narrow question the unit is built around.*
- **Cadwalader declined, sending a subordinate to explain the President's order and request a postponement pending further instructions.** *Refusal by the executive is what turns a habeas case into the test of judgment supremacy.*
- **Taney issued an attachment for contempt against Cadwalader; service was refused at the fort.** *What is exhausted here is the court's coercive process, not its reasoning.*
- **May 28, 1861: Taney delivered the opinion, ordered the proceedings "filed and recorded in the circuit court," and sent a copy to the President.** *The remedy was left to the officer whose conduct was challenged.*
- **July 4, 1861: Lincoln defended the suspension to Congress without naming Taney or the writ, and [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates's opinion]] followed the next day.** *The legal defense of non-compliance is made elsewhere, which is why the case is taught with Bates.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (Taney, C.J., as Circuit Justice, sitting alone; no other judge participated)

- **Suspension is for Congress alone.** Taney "had supposed it to be one of those points of constitutional law upon which there was no difference of opinion, and that it was admitted on all hands, that the privilege of the writ could not be suspended, except by act of congress."
- **No delegation to the military.** The President claims "not only ... the right to suspend the writ of habeas corpus himself, at his discretion, but to delegate that discretionary power to a military officer, and to leave it to him to determine whether he will or will not obey judicial process."
- **The detention is unlawful.** Due process, the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement and the speedy-trial right, "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend, have been disregarded and suspended, like the writ of habeas corpus, by a military order, supported by force of arms."
- **The court's power is exhausted.** Taney files the proceedings and sends a copy to the President, whose "constitutional obligation to 'take care that the laws be faithfully executed'" now requires him "to determine what measures he will take to cause the civil process of the United States to be respected and enforced."

**Taney, Circuit Justice**

- The return discloses a claim of presidential power never officially announced, "by proclamation or otherwise."
- Congress's exclusive power to suspend is treated as settled rather than argued. The textual and historical case — the Clause's placement in Article I, § 9, and the writ's history as a check on the Crown — reaches the casebook only through Note 1's summary.
- He writes "plainly and fully" out of "a proper respect for the high office he fills," to show he has "not ventured to question the legality of his act, without a careful and deliberate examination."
- Suspension of the Bill of Rights guarantees by military order means "the people of the United States are no longer living under a government of laws."
- "[M]y duty was too plain to be mistaken." Met with superior force — his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome" — the court's role ends with a record and an appeal to the Take Care duty.

## Arguments rejected

- *The Suspension Clause does not say who may suspend; the power is an emergency power; and when Congress cannot meet, only the President can use it to execute the laws against a rebellion.* Lincoln's position, as Note 1 summarizes it. Taney's answer, as the casebook summarizes it: the Clause sits in Article I, § 9, among limits on Congress, so suspension is a legislative power, and the writ historically existed to check the executive, so letting the executive suspend it would be absurd.
- *The officer was acting under the President's lawful orders and was entitled to await instructions.* Taney: the military cannot usurp "the authority which the constitution has confided to the judiciary department" upon "any pretext or under any circumstances," or citizens hold "life, liberty and property at the will and pleasure of the army officer in whose military district he may happen to be found." He allows only that the officer "may have misunderstood his instructions."

## Where it sits

**Specific judgments.** Under the casebook heading "Ex parte Merryman and Specific Judgments," this is the third Class 13 position: [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] on whether an opinion binds the political branches, [[Cooper v. Aaron]] on state officials, Merryman on a specific judgment against the President.

**Lincoln's answer, and Bates's.** His July 4 message rested on necessity — "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" — and on the claim that no law was violated. The formal defense is [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates's]]: a judge at chambers may not command the President.

**The only express emergency clause.** Suspension is what Jackson in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] called the Constitution's sole express provision for "extraordinary authority because of a crisis," though the Article I placement is Taney's. Lincoln acted with Congress absent; the 1863 statute authorizing presidential suspension moved later ones into category one. The same setting frames [[The Prize Cases]].

**Taney and the scholarship.** The Chief Justice wrote [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], whom Lincoln had declined to follow as a "political rule." *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld* and *Boumediene v. Bush* are the modern Suspension Clause cases; the co-authors divide, Paulsen's "Merryman Power" against Baude's judgment power.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

These notes follow [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates's opinion]] and cover both readings; the extract runs out partway through Note 2.

**Note 1. "First, consider the merits. Does the president have the power to suspend habeas? . . . Who is right? Is it clear, or is it fairly debatable?"**

- Taney has the better reading: placement in a list of limits on Congress, plus the writ's function against the Crown, makes executive self-suspension close to absurd.
- Lincoln's best version is narrower than the one he argued — suspension only until Congress can convene and decide, the posture he reported in on July 4, 1861.
- Fairly debatable, though, which is the question to answer: the Clause is passive and addresses an emergency Congress may be unable to meet.

**Note 2. "In thinking about whether the president must follow judicial judgments, does it matter if the question on the merits is fairly debatable? Would it matter if the judiciary's judgment in a case was flagrantly and horribly wrong? . . . What if the Supreme Court ordered Lincoln to leave the White House, turn Maryland over to secessionists . . . ? Or . . . what if the Supreme Court . . . had declared the Emancipation Proclamation unconstitutional? Would Lincoln have been obliged to return the newly freed slaves?"**

- The hypotheticals are built to break judgment supremacy at the extreme, and it holds: comply if the court had jurisdiction, and the remedies are appeal, appointments and amendment.
- The coordinacy answer is that the oath to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution" forbids executing an unconstitutional judgment — Paulsen's "Merryman Power," and Lincoln's and Bates's position.
- Debatability cuts against non-compliance: if reasonable lawyers differ, the President's contrary view is not the clear usurpation the argument needs.
- The [[The Emancipation Proclamation|Emancipation Proclamation]] version is sharpest, because compliance would mean re-enslaving people — which is why Baude's limit is jurisdictional, not substantive.

### Further drilling

- **What did Taney order, and what did Lincoln disobey?** A writ and an attachment, both refused; the opinion orders only that the record be filed and sent to the President. There was never an order to release Merryman.
- **Three positions on obedience.** Cooper-style supremacy: obey the judgment and the reasoning. Judgment supremacy (Baude): obey if the court had jurisdiction. Executive coordinacy (Paulsen, Lincoln, Bates): no, if the President in good faith thinks it unconstitutional. Lincoln held the second toward [[Dred Scott v. Sandford|Dred Scott]] in 1858 and acted on the third in 1861.
- **Would jurisdiction matter?** Bates's ground is that a judge at chambers cannot entertain what is in substance "an appeal ... from a decision of the President." Far narrower than general non-execution — but someone must decide what is jurisdictional, so it may only relocate the problem.
- **Vary the fact.** Had Congress been in session and declined to authorize suspension, Lincoln would have been at the lowest ebb rather than in the twilight zone.
- **Impeachment, and forward links.** The House could treat non-compliance as a Take Care violation, or the ruling as an abuse — the question in [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]. Bates's claim that the President answers only to "the high court of impeachment" is the ancestor of [[Trump v. United States]].
