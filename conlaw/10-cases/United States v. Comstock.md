## Rule

A federal law is "necessary and proper" if it is "rationally related to the implementation of a constitutionally enumerated power," and the chain of implied powers may run several steps from the enumerated power: from federal crimes, to federal prisons, to civil commitment of sexually dangerous federal prisoners after their sentences end.

## Facts

- **18 U.S.C. § 4248 reaches only a person already "in the custody of the [Federal] Bureau of Prisons" who is mentally ill and, as a result, "sexually dangerous to others."** *Limitation to people already in federal custody is the fact [[NFIB v. Sebelius|NFIB]] later used to distinguish the case: Congress did not create the predicate for its own power.*
- **Commitment continues past the end of the criminal sentence, on proof by "clear and convincing evidence."** *Earlier federal commitment statutes ended with the sentence, so they did not raise the question.*
- **The Attorney General must "make all reasonable efforts" to have the State of domicile or trial take custody, and must release the person to any State that agrees.** *Breyer counts this as accommodation of state interests.*
- **Since 1949, § 4246 had authorized post-sentence detention of federal prisoners whose mental illness made them dangerous.** *Breyer: history does not prove constitutionality, but § 4248 becomes a "modest addition" to a framework in place since 1855.*
- **Section 4248 had been applied to 105 of over 188,000 federal inmates.** *The narrow reach answers the objection that the holding creates a federal police power.*
- **The Court assumed, without deciding, that the Due Process Clause and other provisions permit this kind of civil commitment.** *Only the Article I question was decided.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Breyer for the Court; Alito concurring in the judgment; Thomas, joined by Scalia in part, dissenting)

- The test is whether the statute is "a means that is rationally related to the implementation of a constitutionally enumerated power," with the choice of means left "primarily . . . to the judgment of Congress."
- First consideration: the Necessary and Proper Clause grants broad authority, including the unenumerated powers to create federal crimes, to imprison offenders, and to run federal prisons.
- Second consideration: § 4248 is a modest addition to federal prison mental-health statutes that go back to 1855.
- Third consideration: as custodian of its prisoners, the Federal Government may protect the public from dangers its prisoners pose, as it could refuse to release a prisoner with a communicable disease.
- Fourth consideration: the statute accounts for state interests, and powers "delegated to the United States" are "[v]irtually by definition" not "reserved to the States."
- Fifth consideration: the link to an enumerated power is not too attenuated, and the statute is not too sweeping.
- (Alito, concurring in the judgment) The Clause executes only enumerated powers, and § 4248 executes the powers behind the prisoners' statutes of conviction. The Clause is not "carte blanche"; it requires an "appropriate" link, and courts must enforce that limit.

**Rejected**

- *Congress's authority under the Clause can be no more than one step removed from an enumerated power, because [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] forbids piling "inference upon inference."* That argument is "irreconcilable with our precedents": [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] itself traced the post office power to carrying the mail, to punishing mail theft; later cases inferred the power to imprison, and *Greenwood v. United States* (1956) inferred the civil-commitment power.
- *§ 4248 "Execut[es]" no enumerated power; the end is protecting society from sexual violence, a state police-power end, and under McCulloch the fit of the means is irrelevant unless the end is legitimate (Thomas).* The end is the enumerated power behind each federal criminal statute; the dissent concedes the chain from crime to prison to prison management, and the same power justifies § 4248.
- *The five considerations are a "novel five-factor test," and the Court never says which are required (Thomas).* The excerpt gives no direct answer; the Court says only that the considerations are "taken together."
- *§ 4248 invades the province of state sovereignty in civil commitment, an area traditionally left to the States ([[New York v. United States]]).* The Tenth Amendment reserves only powers not delegated, and the statute requires the Attorney General to invite the States to take custody.
- *The holding gives Congress a general police power.* § 4248 is narrow in scope and reaches only people already in federal custody.

## Context

Comstock is the modern restatement of [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s "Let the end be legitimate" test, softened into a rational-basis inquiry and borrowing the "reasonably adapted" formula from Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich]]. Note 4 asks whether Scalia's votes in Raich (for federal power) and Comstock (for the dissent) are consistent. Note 3 presses Breyer's premise that States would not detain released prisoners; Professor Rick Hills called that assumption a legal fiction. The founding-era version of the debate is [[The Bank Debate]]: Hamilton's "natural relation" test sustains the statute, and Madison's chain-of-implications objection is the dissent's argument. See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].

[[NFIB v. Sebelius]] cabined the case two years later. Roberts cited Comstock for the Court's deference on "necessary," but held that the prior cases, including continued confinement "of those already in federal custody," were "derivative of, and in service to, a granted power," whereas the individual mandate would let Congress "create the necessary predicate" for its own power. Thomas repeated his Comstock view in his dissent in *Trump v. Mazars* (2020): the Clause "made explicit what was already implicit in the grant of each enumerated power."
