## Snapshot

Jefferson's Secretary of State withheld a justice-of-the-peace commission Adams had signed and Marshall had sealed. Marbury sought mandamus in the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction. The Court held that his right had vested, that the laws afforded a remedy and that mandamus was proper — but that § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, read to confer that jurisdiction, was repugnant to Article III. In play: Article II, §§ 2–3; Article III, § 2, cl. 2; the Article VI oath.

## Issue

The casebook asks how the Court "see[s] its role in the constitutional structure." Marbury answers by refusing effect to a statute.

- Has Marbury a right to the commission he demands?
- If that right has been violated, do the laws afford him a remedy?
- Is the remedy a mandamus from this Court under § 13?
- If § 13 conflicts with Article III, must the Court apply it?

## Rule

A court deciding a case must treat the Constitution as "a superior, paramount law," so it may not give effect to a statute "repugnant to the constitution." Article III's list of original jurisdiction is exclusive; Congress cannot enlarge it. An officer appointed to a fixed term holds a right the executive cannot resume, and a withheld ministerial duty on which individual rights depend is judicially examinable.

- "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
- "The constitution is either a superior, paramount law . . . or it is on a level with ordinary legislative acts . . . . Between these alternatives there is no middle ground."
- "[I]n one class of cases its jurisdiction is original, and not appellate; in the other it is appellate, and not original."

## Operative facts

- **The Organic Act of February 27, 1801 created more than forty District of Columbia justices of the peace, "to continue in office for five years."** *The fixed term makes the right vested; for an officer removable at will "the circumstance which completes his appointment is of no concern."*
- **On March 3, 1801 the lame-duck Federalist Senate confirmed Adams's nominees and Adams signed their commissions before going to bed.** *The signature is the President's last act, so the appointment was complete then — nothing "inchoate and incomplete" remained.*
- **John Marshall, still Secretary of State though already confirmed as Chief Justice, affixed the Great Seal that night.** *The seal is "conclusive testimony" of the signature and of "the completion of the appointment."*
- **Marbury's commission was never delivered; Marshall was short-handed and thought delivery "legally inconsequential."** *Marshall was right in law — transmission "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law" — and the non-delivery is what made the case.*
- **Jefferson took office the next day and directed Madison not to deliver any commissions still held at the State Department.** *Sealing and recording are prescribed "by law, and not to be guided by the will of the President," so withholding is "violative of a vested legal right," not executive discretion.*
- **On December 16, 1801 Charles Lee moved in the Supreme Court for a rule requiring Madison to show cause why mandamus should not issue.** *Filed in original rather than appellate jurisdiction — fatal. He wins on right and remedy and loses on forum.*
- **Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 empowered the Court to issue mandamus "to any courts appointed, or persons holding office, under the authority of the United States."** *Marshall reads the clause as a grant of original jurisdiction, which manufactures the clash with Article III; read as a remedy where jurisdiction already exists, it raises no constitutional question.*
- **The Jeffersonians then repealed the 1801 Act, abolished the new circuit courts, restored circuit riding, cancelled the 1802 Term and began impeaching Federalist judges, so the case waited until 1803.** *The Court decided under threat, with the Repeal Act's real test case, [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]], right behind it.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (unanimous; Marshall, C.J., for the Court; no dissent or concurrence)

- **Right.** Because the office ran five years "independent of the executive," the appointment "conferred on him a legal right to the office," and withholding the commission is "violative of a vested legal right."
- **Remedy.** The laws afford one. "The very essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws, whenever he receives an injury."
- **Mandamus is the proper writ.** It lies for a duty "not depending on executive discretion," whatever the respondent's "exalted station." "This, then, is a plain case for a mandamus."
- **But not from this Court.** Mandamus "to an officer for the delivery of a paper" is original, not appellate, so § 13 is "not . . . warranted by the constitution."
- **An unconstitutional statute cannot be applied.** "[A] law repugnant to the constitution is void; and . . . courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument." "The rule must be discharged."

**Marshall, C.J., for the Court**

- **Order of questions.** Right, remedy, then whether the writ may issue "from this court" — condemning the executive's conduct before announcing that the Court cannot correct it.
- **Right.** Appointing (§ 2) and commissioning (§ 3) sit in "two separate and distinct sections of the constitution"; after the signature the Secretary's duties are fixed by law, and sealing and recording are "a ministerial act."
- **Remedy.** "The government of the United States has been emphatically termed a government of laws, and not of men." Examinability "must always depend on the nature of that act," and "[t]he question whether a right has vested or not, is, on its nature, judicial."
- **Section 13 and Article III.** The Secretary of State "is precisely within the letter of the description," so if the writ cannot issue "it must be because the law is unconstitutional." Free reapportionment would leave the clause "mere surplusage" and "form without substance."
- **Judicial review.** The legislature's powers "are defined, and limited; and that those limits may not be mistaken, or forgotten, the constitution is written." Four texts: the judicial power over cases "arising under the constitution"; the two-witness rule, "addressed especially to the courts"; the judicial oath, "worse than solemn mockery" otherwise; and the Supremacy Clause's "in pursuance" language.

## Arguments rejected

- *The appointment was incomplete until the commission was delivered and accepted.* Transmission "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law."
- *Delivering or withholding a commission is a political act for the executive alone.* An officer charged by law with a duty on which rights depend "cannot at his discretion sport away the vested rights of others."
- *An order to a cabinet officer intrudes into the cabinet and intermeddles "with the prerogatives of the executive."* The Court disclaims such jurisdiction as "[a]n extravagance, so absurd and excessive," but this case concerns "a paper, which, according to law, is upon record."
- *Article III's original-jurisdiction clause has no negative words, so Congress may add cases within the judicial power* (argued "at the bar"). "[A]ffirmative words are often, in their operation, negative of other objects than those affirmed." Nor is the writ appellate: appellate jurisdiction "revises and corrects the proceedings in a cause already instituted."
- *A statute beyond Congress's power still binds the courts, which must "close their eyes on the constitution, and see only the law."* That reduces to nothing "the greatest improvement on political institutions—a written constitution."

## Where it sits

**The power was not new.** The casebook calls Marbury "the first time that the entire Court wrote an opinion holding an act of Congress unconstitutional," but review was assumed in [[Brutus No. 11]] and [[The Federalist No. 78]], the Justices had refused advisory opinions ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]), and [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]] upheld a federal tax.

**Politics all the way down.** The Judiciary Act of 1801, the Repeal Act and the impeachment campaign ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]) are the setting; the expected confrontation came a week later in [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]. McConnell's verdict: Marbury is "the product of a defeated and demoralized Court."

**Review, not yet supremacy.** It holds only that a court may decline to apply an unconstitutional statute in its own case; whether its reading binds the other branches is Class 13 — Jefferson, Jackson and [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates|Lincoln]] against [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]]. No act of Congress fell again until [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]].

**The forgotten first half.** The ministerial–political line is the root of the political question doctrine ([[A Map of Article III]]) and the mandamus cases ([[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]], [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]]); the fixed-term premise runs to [[The Decision of 1789]] and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor v. United States]]; high office exempts no one ([[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]], [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]).

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "What are the Court's arguments for judicial review? . . . Are its arguments closer to the views expressed by Brutus, or by Hamilton?"**

- Three strands: structure, judicial duty and four textual hooks. Hamilton's, and modest with it: review attaches to deciding "particular cases." The oath is the weak link — it binds legislators and executive officers too.

**Note 2. "What does Marbury actually hold, and which parts of the opinion are dicta? . . . Marshall went out of his way . . . to provoke President Jefferson, and . . . to decline to issue any order that Jefferson could resist. Is that a fair characterization?"**

- Narrowly: no original jurisdiction to mandamus the Secretary of State, § 13 void so far as it confers it. Right and remedy are dicta unless the order of analysis was necessary — arguably it was, since § 13 reaches mandamus only "in cases warranted by the principles and usages of law." Fair as to effect: Jefferson was left nothing to defy.

**Note 3. "Did the Act purport to confer 'original jurisdiction' . . . whenever someone seeks a writ of mandamus? Or . . . [is] mandamus a writ the Court may grant as a remedy?"**

- The second is better: the clause sits in § 13's appellate sentence, after the remedial grant of "writs of prohibition to the district courts," so Marbury loses on the statute and judicial review never arises.

**Note 4. "Is he right that Congress may not increase the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction? Is there any other plausible reading?"**

- Exclusive both ways, or the distribution is "form without substance." The rival reading is a floor: those cases are guaranteed a forum, not a ceiling.

**Note 5. "Is Marbury's argument one of judicial supremacy over the other branches, or of the Constitution's supremacy over all of them?"**

- The live one; subparts (a)–(e) run it against the President, Congress and the states. "[C]ourts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument" is the departmentalist reading, and Marbury's first half is itself review of executive action.
- The logic extends to Jackson on the Bank ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]) and [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]; a defied judgment is Class 13, [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] against [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]].

**Note 6, with the notes after [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]], covering both readings.** "Was the Court's decision a capitulation? . . . should the Court consider the risk to its institutional standing and authority?"

- Stuart is text plus acquiescence: no words "prohibit or restrain" transferring a cause between tribunals, and circuit riding is fixed by "practice and acquiescence." Marbury asserts review where it costs nothing; Stuart withholds it where the cost would have been real.

### Further drilling

- **Vary the forum.** On appeal there would have been jurisdiction, no constitutional question, and an order Jefferson would have ignored.
- **Vary the office.** A justice of the peace serving at pleasure could simply have been removed — compare *Myers v. United States*.
- **Vary the act.** Had Adams not signed, the act would be discretionary and "only politically examinable."
- **The conflict of interest.** Marshall sealed the commissions, failed to deliver them, then decided the case.
