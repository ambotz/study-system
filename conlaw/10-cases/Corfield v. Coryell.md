## Rule

Article IV § 2 entitles a citizen of one state, while in another state, only to those privileges and immunities that are "in their nature, fundamental," and it is a rule of equal treatment in those rights rather than a grant of them. It gives the visitor no share in property the state holds in common for its own citizens, so New Jersey may reserve its oyster beds to residents.

## Facts

- New Jersey's act of 1820 made it unlawful for anyone "not, at the time, an actual inhabitant and resident of this state" to gather oysters from a vessel not wholly owned by a resident, forfeiting the vessel. *The residency line in the sixth section is the only feature of the act that raises Article IV § 2.*
- The second section barred dredging by "[n]o person residing in, or out of this state." *A prohibition falling on residents and outsiders alike cannot be attacked as discrimination.*
- The Hiram, owned by a Philadelphian, was "found engaged in taking oysters in Maurice river cove by means of dredges," and was condemned and sold. *Nonresident use of a state resource is the claim Washington held the clause does not reach.*
- Oysters are stationary and lie in soil and waters within the state. *This lets Washington treat the fishery as property held in common, unlike a trade, a lawsuit or a tax.*
- Oyster beds "might be totally exhausted and destroyed" if the state could not exclude outsiders. *The consequence supplies the affirmative reason for the common-property exception.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Washington, Circuit Justice, sitting on circuit; judgment for the defendant)

- **The clause reaches only fundamental rights.** "We feel no hesitation in confining these expressions to those privileges and immunities which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments; and which have, at all times, been enjoyed by the citizens of the several states which compose this Union, from the time of their becoming free, independent, and sovereign."
- **The catalogue.** "Protection by the government; the enjoyment of life and liberty, with the right to acquire and possess property of every kind, and to pursue and obtain happiness and safety; subject nevertheless to such restraints as the government may justly prescribe for the general good of the whole. The right of a citizen of one state to pass through, or to reside in any other state, for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits, or otherwise; to claim the benefit of the writ of habeas corpus; to institute and maintain actions of any kind in the courts of the state; to take, hold and dispose of property, either real or personal; and an exemption from higher taxes or impositions than are paid by the other citizens of the state."
- **The franchise is added, and immediately qualified.** "to which may be added, the elective franchise, as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state in which it is to be exercised."
- **The purpose is comity.** Enjoyment of these rights by the citizens of each state in every other was "manifestly calculated" (in the words of the corresponding article of confederation preamble) "the better to secure and perpetuate mutual friendship and intercourse among the people of the different states of the Union."
- **No participation in exclusively local rights.** "But we cannot accede to the proposition which was insisted on by the counsel, that, under this provision of the constitution, the citizens of the several states are permitted to participate in all the rights which belong exclusively to the citizens of any other particular state, merely upon the ground that they are enjoyed by those citizens; much less, that in regulating the use of the common property of the citizens of such state, the legislature is bound to extend to the citizens of all the other states the same advantages as are secured to their own citizens."
- **The common-property holding.** Where private several fisheries do not exist, the right of fishing "belongs to all the citizens or subjects of the state. It is the property of all." The citizens "may be considered as tenants in common of this property; and they are so exclusively entitled to the use of it, that it cannot be enjoyed by others without the tacit consent, or the express permission of the sovereign who has the power to regulate its use."
- **Reading the clause otherwise would be "a grant of a cotenancy."** Since the right to take oysters "is a right of property, vested either in certain individuals, or in the state, for the use of the citizens thereof, it would, in our opinion, be going quite too far to construe the grant of privileges and immunities of citizens, as amounting to a grant of a cotenancy in the common property of the state, to the citizens of all the other states."
- **Consequences confirm the result.** Such a construction "would, in many instances, be productive of the most serious public inconvenience and injury, particularly, in regard to those kinds of fish, which, by being exposed to too general use, may be exhausted."
- **The other two objections fail as well.** The court held the act no infringement of Congress's commerce power, citing [[Gibbons v. Ogden]], and no violation of Article III § 2's grant of admiralty jurisdiction, and ordered judgment for the defendant — both disposed of by the casebook's editors rather than printed.

**Rejected**

- *New Jersey lets its own citizens take oysters; "privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states" must therefore mean that a Pennsylvanian gets whatever a New Jerseyman gets, and the sixth section denies exactly that.* Washington refuses the premise that the clause transfers the content of one state's citizenship to visitors: it secures equality only in the fundamental rights that belong "to the citizens of all free governments," and the use of a state's common property is not one of them.
- *Oysters lying in their beds have never been appropriated; they remain part of the common stock to which all men are equally entitled, so New Jersey has nothing to reserve and is simply excluding strangers from what nobody owns.* Rutherfoth, following Grotius, answers that where "the exclusive right in the water and soil which a person has occasion to use in taking them is vested in others, no other persons can claim the liberty of hunting, fishing, or fowling, on lands, or waters, which are so appropriated." Grotius: "The sovereign . . . who has dominion over the land, or waters, in which the fish are, may prohibit foreigners . . . from taking them." New Jersey's exclusive right "has never been ceded by that state, in express terms, to the United States," which counsel admitted.
- *The seizure of a Pennsylvania vessel engaged in the coasting trade burdens commerce among the states and invades the power granted by Article I § 8.* Rejected; the extract prints only the editors' note that the court so held and their cross-reference to [[Gibbons v. Ogden]].
- *Condemnation and sale of a vessel by two county justices usurps the admiralty and maritime jurisdiction that Article III § 2 extends to the courts of the United States.* Rejected, again only in the editors' summary.

## Context

Corfield is the hinge between two constitutional provisions. Article IV § 2 is a **comity rule**: Note 1 records that the clause "is sometimes known as the Comity Clause because it requires a certain form of non-discrimination between states," and that "out-of-staters do not carry their state's rights with them into another state: A Minnesota fishing license is not good in Wisconsin." The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges **or** Immunities Clause, drafted four decades later, borrowed Washington's phrase for an entirely different job — securing rights against a citizen's own state — and its framers quoted the Corfield catalogue as their gloss. [[Slaughter-House Cases]] (1873) then read the Fourteenth Amendment clause down to rights of national citizenship and left Article IV doing the work it had always done. The casebook's warning is worth heeding: do not confuse the two clauses, and do not assume that a right on Washington's list is therefore a right against one's own state.

The gap in Corfield is between its **dicta and its holding**. The famous paragraph is an enumeration of fundamental rights offered in answer to a question the court did not have to answer, since the court went on to hold that the very activity before it fell outside the clause. Washington lists nothing that resembles oyster-raking. The operative rule of the case is the **common-property exception**, and it is narrow: a state that owns a resource in trust for its citizens may allocate it to them.

Modern doctrine kept the structure and tightened the exception. [[Toomer v. Witsell]] (1948) rejected the idea that a state owns its fisheries in any sense that lets it hoard them from out-of-staters, and put commercial shrimping inside the clause. The modern test is two steps: the right must be **fundamental to the promotion of interstate harmony**, and if it is, the state must show a **substantial reason** for the discrimination and a **close relation** between the discrimination and that reason. [[Supreme Court of New Hampshire v. Piper|Piper]] (1985) found no such reason for a bar-residency rule, over a Vermont applicant living 400 yards from the line. [[United Building & Construction Trades Council v. Mayor and Council of Camden|United Building & Construction Trades Council]] (1984) applied the clause to a municipal 40% hiring ordinance but held, quoting earlier cases, that "like many other constitutional provisions, the privileges and immunities clause is not an absolute" and that a "state should have considerable leeway in analyzing local evils and in prescribing appropriate cures," and remanded because it was "impossible to evaluate Camden's justification on the record as it now stands." [[Hicklin v. Orbeck]] (1978) struck down "Alaska Hire." [[Baldwin v. Fish and Game Commission of Montana|Baldwin v. Montana Fish & Game]] (1978) went the other way for recreational elk hunting, which is Corfield's oyster bed in modern dress. [[Saenz v. Roe]] (1999) drew on the clause for "the right of the newly arrived citizen to the same privileges and immunities enjoyed by other citizens of the same State."

The other doctrine policing state discrimination is the **dormant Commerce Clause**, and Note 3 says so: "Many of these cases are decided under the so-called 'dormant Commerce Clause.' . . . But not all." Corfield itself carried a dormant commerce claim, rejected in 1823 without the elaboration the doctrine later acquired. [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] is that branch two centuries on, and the comparison is the point of pairing them: Article IV § 2 protects **citizens** against discrimination in fundamental rights; the dormant Commerce Clause protects the **interstate market** against protectionism, reaches corporations (which Article IV does not, since [[Paul v. Virginia]]), and asks about purpose and effect rather than about citizenship.

Where it sits in the course: Article IV is the horizontal half of federalism, following the vertical material of Classes 16–23 and the Union's structure in [[Texas v. White]]. The casebook's Article IV § 2 sequence runs from interstate comity in slavery cases through Corfield to [[Prigg v. Pennsylvania]] and the Fugitive Slave Clause, which is the same section put to the opposite use — a duty running between states that overrode a state's own law. Article IV comity is also the machinery [[Dred Scott v. Sandford|Dred Scott]] made unbearable: Taney's argument that Black citizenship in one state would carry entry and full privileges into every other assumed a stronger reading of Article IV than Corfield gives.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Action of trespass in the federal circuit court at Philadelphia by Corfield, the Pennsylvania owner of the sloop Hiram, against Coryell, who had seized the vessel; the defendant justified under the New Jersey act and the sentence of the Cumberland county magistrates.
- "The points reserved present for the consideration of the court, many interesting and difficult questions, which will be examined in the shape of objections made by the plaintiff's counsel to the seizure of the Hiram." This is a circuit court sitting with a Supreme Court Justice, not the Supreme Court; nothing in it binds anyone, which is one reason the opinion is remembered for its dicta.

### Facts

New Jersey's act of June 9, 1820 regulated its oyster fishery in layers. The first section closed the beds from May to September to everyone on a $10 penalty; the second forbade dredging at any time by any person "residing in, or out of this state," on a $50 penalty; the sixth made it unlawful for a person who was not an actual inhabitant and resident to gather oysters from a vessel not wholly owned by a resident, and forfeited the vessel, oysters and rakes along with $10. The seventh let any person seize such a vessel and bring it before two county justices, who were to try the case and order a sale on condemnation. Some Pennsylvanians chartered the Hiram out of Philadelphia and went dredging in Maurice River Cove; she was seized, condemned and sold. Her owner sued in trespass, and Justice Bushrod Washington heard the constitutional objections on circuit.

### Issue

- Does the New Jersey act infringe the power granted to Congress by Article I § 8 to regulate commerce among the several states?
- Does the act's exclusion of nonresidents from gathering oysters violate Article IV § 2's guarantee that "the citizens of each state shall be entitled to all privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states"?
- Does condemnation of the vessel by county justices trench on the admiralty and maritime jurisdiction that Article III § 2 extends to the federal courts?

### Holding

- No. The court held the act no infringement of the commerce power; the extract carries only the editors' statement of the holding and their cross-reference to [[Gibbons v. Ogden]].
- No. The clause secures only privileges and immunities "which are, in their nature, fundamental"; a share in the state's common property is not among them, and the legislature is not "bound to extend to the citizens of all the other states the same advantages as are secured to their own citizens" in regulating that property.
- No. The court held the statute consistent with Article III's grant of admiralty jurisdiction, again in the editors' summary only, and gave judgment for the defendant.

### Rule

- Article IV § 2 guarantees equal treatment in a defined class of rights, not the transfer of one state's whole body of citizenship rights to visitors.
- The class is limited to privileges and immunities "which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments," and which the citizens of the several states have enjoyed "from the time of their becoming free, independent, and sovereign."
- The illustrative list: protection by the government; life and liberty; acquiring and possessing property of every kind; pursuing and obtaining happiness and safety; passage through and residence in another state for trade, agriculture or professional pursuits; habeas corpus; access to the state's courts; taking, holding and disposing of property; exemption from higher taxes than other citizens pay; and the elective franchise "as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state in which it is to be exercised."
- Each right is held "subject nevertheless to such restraints as the government may justly prescribe for the general good of the whole."
- A state's common property — here, an unappropriated oyster fishery in state waters — is owned by its citizens as "tenants in common," and the clause is not "a grant of a cotenancy in the common property of the state, to the citizens of all the other states."
- Where a several fishery is privately owned, it "is as much the property of the individual to whom it belongs, as dry land, or land covered by water," and is protected against "citizens or strangers" alike.

### Reasoning

**(Washington, Circuit Justice)**

- Begins by narrowing the question: "The inquiry is, what are the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states?" The answer is a category, not a list of whatever any given state happens to confer.
- Concedes that enumeration is possible but unnecessary — "it would perhaps be more tedious than difficult to enumerate" — then enumerates anyway, under "general heads."
- Grounds the clause in comity, quoting the preamble of the corresponding article of confederation provision: the object was "the better to secure and perpetuate mutual friendship and intercourse among the people of the different states of the Union."
- Distinguishes two kinds of fishery. A several fishery is private property, protected against everyone. A common fishery is the property of the citizens collectively, who "are so exclusively entitled to the use of it, that it cannot be enjoyed by others without the tacit consent, or the express permission of the sovereign."
- Answers the public-law argument with authority rather than principle: Rutherfoth and Grotius establish that dominion over the soil and waters carries the power to exclude non-subjects from taking the fish in them.
- Adds a sovereignty point: the exclusive right "has never been ceded by that state, in express terms, to the United States," a concession by the plaintiff's own counsel.
- Closes with consequences — exhaustion of the beds — which is the only place the opinion argues rather than classifies.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. A single Justice sitting on circuit; there is no vote and no separate opinion.

### Cold-call notes

- **What did Corfield hold?** That New Jersey could exclude nonresidents from its oyster beds, because a state's common property is outside Article IV § 2. Do not answer with the catalogue: the catalogue is dictum and it is the part of the case the holding does not use.
- **Why is the famous passage dictum?** Washington had to decide whether oyster-raking was a protected privilege. Defining the whole category was one route to "no," but he could have said simply that use of state property is not covered. Everything he says about habeas corpus, the courts and taxation decides nothing in the case.
- **Where does this list come from? Does it have a common theme?** Note 2 asks. The best answer: these are the rights a person needs in order to function as a member of civil society anywhere — security, property, movement, access to courts, and equal fiscal burdens — which is why Washington ties them to "the citizens of all free governments" rather than to American constitutional text. The theme is **civil** rights, in Note 1's sense: "The word civil is derived from the same Latin root as the words citizen and city."
- **Then why is the elective franchise on the list?** It is the one item that breaks the theme, since voting is the paradigm political right that Note 1 says the clause does not equalize. Washington guards it with "as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state in which it is to be exercised," which drains it of content: you get the franchise on whatever terms the state sets, and residence is one of those terms. Be ready to say the item is either a mistake or a nullity.
- **Vary the resource.** Suppose New Jersey reserved to residents not oyster beds but the right to *sell* oysters at market. The common-property rationale disappears: selling is trade, and "[t]he right of a citizen of one state to pass through, or to reside in any other state, for purposes of trade" is on Washington's own list. Suppose instead the state charges nonresidents a higher oyster licence fee: that begins to look like the "higher taxes or impositions" the list forbids, and the state would have to justify the differential as a charge for a resource its taxpayers maintain — which is roughly what [[Toomer v. Witsell|Toomer]] later required.
- **Vary the ownership.** If the beds had been a *several* fishery owned by a New Jersey individual, the Pennsylvanians lose faster and for a duller reason: private property excludes "citizens or strangers" alike, and there is no discrimination at all.
- **Vary the claimant.** If a Pennsylvania *corporation* had owned the Hiram, Article IV § 2 drops out entirely, because the clause speaks of citizens and corporations are not citizens for its purposes ([[Paul v. Virginia]]). The claim would have to be made under the Commerce Clause — which is the doctrinal reason [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross|Pork Producers]] is a dormant Commerce Clause case and not this one.
- **How would the case come out today?** The residency requirement probably survives, but not on Washington's reasoning. A modern court asks whether taking oysters recreationally is fundamental to interstate harmony (probably not, on the [[Baldwin v. Fish and Game Commission of Montana|elk-hunting]] analogy) and, if it is commercial, whether New Jersey has a substantial reason closely related to a conservation problem the nonresidents cause. The state's "ownership" of the fishery is no longer a trump.
- **What does Article IV § 2 *not* do?** It creates no substantive rights. It does not stop New Jersey from closing the beds to everyone, which the first and second sections of this very act did. That is the cleanest way to see it as an antidiscrimination rule: the state's power over the resource is unlimited so long as it treats outsiders as it treats its own.
- **Why did the Fourteenth Amendment's drafters quote this case?** Because Washington's paragraph was the only judicial catalogue of "privileges and immunities" available, and they wanted the phrase to carry that content against the states. [[Slaughter-House Cases|Slaughter-House]] refused the transfer. Note the irony for the exam: the passage they borrowed was dictum in a case that *upheld* a discriminatory state law.
- **Which types of constitutional argument is Washington using?** The Notes close by asking. Text, barely — he quotes the clause and then leaves it. Structure and history, through the Articles of Confederation preamble. Precedent, not at all. Natural law and the law of nations (Grotius, Rutherfoth) do most of the work, which is unusual enough to be worth naming. Consequences close the argument. See [[Modes of constitutional argument]] and [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Memorable details.** The vessel is the Hiram; the waters are Maurice River Cove; the penalty is $10 plus the boat, the oysters and the rakes. Washington's quotation of the clause quotes the clause as "all the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states," inserting a word the Constitution does not contain, which is a small reminder to read the provision, not the opinion's version of it.
