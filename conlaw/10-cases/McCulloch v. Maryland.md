## Rule

Congress has implied powers: under the Necessary and Proper Clause it may choose any means "appropriate" and "plainly adapted" to a legitimate end, and how necessary the means is belongs to Congress, not the courts. A State may not tax, or "in any manner control," the operations of a federal instrumentality, because the Constitution makes federal law supreme and one State cannot tax what all the people created.

## Facts

- **No enumerated power mentions a bank or a corporation.** *Congress had to rest the charter on implied powers, so the case had to settle how far they reach.*
- **Congress chartered the First Bank in 1791 after full debate, let it lapse in 1811, and chartered the Second Bank in 1816 after wartime financial distress.** *Marshall gives the practice "a considerable impression" but says the charter would stand "were the question entirely new."*
- **The Bank itself, not the charter, chose to put a branch in Baltimore.** *This forced a sub-holding that Congress may leave the siting of branches to the Bank.*
- **Maryland's tax fell only on banks the State had not chartered, which in practice meant the Bank of the United States.** *The tax singled out the Bank, but Marshall does not rest on discrimination. Note 6 asks whether he should have.*
- **The tax fell on the Bank's operations (its notes), not its real estate or Marylanders' shares.** *Marshall expressly preserves general taxes on both, so the case can be won without exempting federal property from all state tax.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (unanimous; Marshall, C.J.)

- **Congress may incorporate a bank.** The Constitution comes from the people, not the States; the federal government, "though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action." Nothing excludes implied powers, and the Necessary and Proper Clause enlarges, or at least does not restrain, Congress's choice of means: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."
- **The branches are valid too.** They are "conducive to the complete accomplishment of the object," and the Bank "may, we think, be safely trusted with the selection of places where those branches shall be fixed."
- **Maryland's tax is "unconstitutional and void."** "[T]he States have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress to carry into execution the powers vested in the general government." That is "the unavoidable consequence of that supremacy which the constitution has declared."
- **The limit.** The holding "does not extend to a tax paid by the real property of the bank, in common with the other real property within the State," nor to a tax on Marylanders' interests in the Bank "in common with other property of the same description throughout the State."

**Rejected**

- *The Constitution is the act of sovereign and independent States; federal powers are delegated by the States, which alone are sovereign, and must be exercised in subordination to them.* The Constitution was ratified by the people in state conventions. The people met in their States because "No political dreamer was wild enough to think of breaking down the lines which separate the States," but their act remained "the measures of the people themselves." The government "is, emphatically, and truly, a government of the people."
- *Creating a corporation is a sovereign power not expressly granted, so it cannot be implied.* All legislative power is sovereign. Chartering a corporation is not "a great substantive and independent power" like war or taxation; "It is never the end for which other powers are exercised, but a means by which other objects are accomplished."
- *"Necessary" means indispensable, so the Clause limits Congress to means without which a power "would be nugatory," the most direct and simple.* In ordinary usage "necessary" often means "convenient, or useful, or essential." Article I, § 10 says "absolutely necessary" when it means that. A strict reading would forbid oaths of office beyond the constitutional oath, most of the federal criminal code, and punishment for robbing the mail.
- *The Clause does no more than confirm that Congress may legislate at all.* "That a legislature, endowed with legislative powers, can legislate, is a proposition too self-evident to have been questioned."
- *State banks can do the job, so a national bank is unnecessary.* The Constitution does not make the Union depend on means "which another government may furnish or withhold"; in any case "Congress alone can make the election."
- *Taxation is an absolute sovereign power, limited only by the express limits of Article I, § 10, and the States can be trusted not to use it to destroy.* "[T]he power to tax involves the power to destroy," and "all inconsistencies are to be reconciled by the magic of the word Confidence" will not do: the people of one State would not trust another State to control their own government, and "In the legislature of the Union alone, are all represented."
- *Maryland claims only a power to tax property, not the mail or the mint.* The principle Maryland asserts gives no reason for that line. If States may tax one federal instrument, "they may tax the mail; they may tax the mint; they may tax patent rights."
- *The Federalist treats state taxing power as concurrent and unlimited.* Those numbers answered the fear of federal monopoly over taxation, not the question whether States may tax federal means. And while the Federalist deserves "great respect," "a right to judge of their correctness must be retained."
- *Taxing power is concurrent, so if Congress may tax state banks, the States may tax the national bank.* The difference is "the action of the whole on a part, and the action of a part on the whole." When Congress taxes state banks, the people of all the States are represented; when Maryland taxes the Bank, it taxes "institutions created, not by their own constituents."

## Context

McCulloch is the judicial resolution of the 1791 argument in [[The Bank Debate]] (Class 16). Marshall adopts Hamilton's framework: incidental powers, the ordinary meaning of "necessary," a corporation as a means. He answers Jefferson's reading of "necessary" as indispensable and turns Madison's own later retreat against Maryland ("statesmen of the first class ... have yielded those opinions to the exigencies of the nation"). [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] gives Madison's own account of that retreat, and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] rejects the decision's authority over the President thirteen years later.

The first holding is the foundation of [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]]. The modern cases still cite "Let the end be legitimate." Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] builds on it. [[Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] give "proper" independent force, so that a law that violates state sovereignty, or that creates a power rather than carrying one into execution, is not "proper" even if it is convenient. [[United States v. Comstock]] (2010) applies McCulloch's rational-relation reading to civil commitment. The "pretext" passage is the ancestor of the limits enforced in [[United States v. Lopez]] and NFIB.

The second holding is the root of **intergovernmental immunity**, which belongs with [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]] as its mirror image. [[Osborn v. Bank of the United States]] (1824) reaffirmed it against Ohio's raid on the Bank. [[Collector v. Day]] (1871) extended the immunity the other way, exempting state judges' salaries from federal income tax. [[Graves v. New York ex rel. O'Keefe]] (1939) abandoned the broad immunity for nondiscriminatory taxes on government employees, and [[South Carolina v. Baker]] (1988) confirmed that retreat. What survives is a ban on taxing or regulating the United States directly and a ban on discriminating against it. [[United States v. Washington]] (2022) applied the discrimination branch unanimously, striking down a Washington workers' compensation law aimed only at federal contract workers at the Hanford site. [[Johnson v. Maryland]] (1920), in Note 6, extends the principle from taxes to regulation.

The Bank returns in the removal unit. [[Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) reserved "the Federal Reserve, to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States," and [[Trump v. Cook]] (2026) upheld the Governors' for-cause protection on that tradition. Cook quotes McCulloch's account of why Congress revived the Bank.

Next in the casebook is [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] (1824), where Marshall again rejects "strict construction" of the enumerated powers.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Maryland sued James McCulloch, cashier of the Baltimore branch, for statutory penalties after he issued notes without paying the State's tax. The state trial court gave judgment for Maryland, and the Maryland Court of Appeals affirmed.
- On writ of error, the Supreme Court reversed.

### Facts

Hamilton's First Bank, chartered in 1791 after the debates of Class 16, expired in 1811 when a recharter bill failed. The War of 1812 brought financial crisis. Madison vetoed a bank bill in 1815 on policy grounds, conceding the constitutional question, then signed the charter of the Second Bank in 1816. Opposition in the States followed. Maryland taxed banks it had not chartered, and five other States moved to tax the Bank, Ohio and Kentucky with heavy annual levies on each branch. The Bank opened a Baltimore branch, its cashier refused to pay, and Maryland sued. The casebook prints only Marshall's opinion, which takes two questions in turn: whether Congress could charter the Bank, and whether Maryland could tax its branch.

### Issue

- Does Congress have power to incorporate a national bank (and to let the bank establish branches in the States)?
- May Maryland tax the operations of a branch of the Bank of the United States?

### Holding

- Yes. The Constitution does not exclude implied powers, and the Necessary and Proper Clause lets Congress choose any appropriate means plainly adapted to a legitimate end. A bank is an appropriate means of carrying out the fiscal powers (taxing, borrowing, and supporting armies), and the Bank may choose where to put its branches.
- No. The Constitution and federal laws are supreme. A State's taxing power reaches only what its own people can subject to it, and a tax on a federal instrumentality's operations would let one State control what the people of all the States created. The tax is void.

### Rule

- "[T]he government of the Union, though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action."
- "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."
- If a law "is really calculated to effect any of the objects entrusted to the government," the courts will not "inquire into the degree of its necessity." But the Court will strike a law passed "under the pretext of executing its powers" for "objects not entrusted to the government."
- "[T]he States have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress to carry into execution the powers vested in the general government."
- The immunity does not cover nondiscriminatory state taxes on the Bank's real property or on residents' shares in it.

### Reasoning

**(Marshall, C.J., for the Court) Preliminaries**

- Only the Court can decide the question "peacefully": "by this tribunal alone can the decision be made."
- **Practice.** The question is not "entirely unprejudiced by the former proceedings of the nation." "[A] bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long acquiesced in. But "a doubtful question," one "in the decision of which the great principles of liberty are not concerned, but the respective powers of those who are equally the representatives of the people, are to be adjusted," "ought to receive a considerable impression from that practice." The First Bank "did not steal upon an unsuspecting legislature," and experience without the Bank "convinced those who were most prejudiced against the measure of its necessity." Marshall then disclaims reliance: the arguments from practice "are not made under the impression, that, were the question entirely new, the law would be found irreconcilable with the constitution."
- **The people, not the States.** Ratification by conventions means the Constitution "derives its whole authority" from the people. "The assent of the States, in their sovereign capacity, is implied, in calling a Convention," but the people's act "required not the affirmance, and could not be negatived, by the State governments."
- **Enumeration and supremacy.** The government "is acknowledged by all to be one of enumerated powers," but the Supremacy Clause and the state officers' oath make it "supreme within its sphere of action."

**(Marshall, C.J.) Implied powers without the Clause**

- The Articles of Confederation said "expressly"; the Tenth Amendment "omits the word," so whether a power was delegated turns on "a fair construction of the whole instrument."
- A constitution that listed every means "would partake of the prolixity of a legal code"; its nature requires "that only its great outlines should be marked." The restrictions in Article I, § 9 confirm this: they would be pointless if nothing were implied. "[W]e must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding."
- The great powers (taxing, borrowing, commerce, war, armies) imply "ample means for their execution." Treasure raised in the north may have to go south. The burden is on those who claim that "one particular mode of effecting the object is excepted."
- Incorporation is "never used for its own sake." No charity, college, or city exists in order to be incorporated.

**(Marshall, C.J.) The Necessary and Proper Clause**

- Maryland's first reading (the Clause merely authorizes legislation) is self-refuting.
- **Meaning of "necessary."** "[N]o word conveys to the mind, in all situations, one single definite idea." A thing "may be necessary, very necessary, absolutely or indispensably necessary." Article I, § 10's "absolutely necessary" shows the Convention knew the difference.
- **Purpose.** The provision is "made in a constitution intended to endure for ages to come, and, consequently, to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs." Fixing the means forever would deny the legislature "the capacity to avail itself of experience."
- **Examples.** A strict reading would bar oaths of office beyond the constitutional oath, the general criminal code, the carrying of mail and punishment of mail robbers, and punishment of perjury in federal courts. If "necessary" means "conducive to" in order to allow punishment, it means the same for other means.
- **"Proper."** Adding a word "the only possible effect of which is to qualify that strict and rigorous meaning" shows that some choice of means was intended.
- **Placement and form.** "1st. The clause is placed among the powers of Congress, not among the limitations on those powers." "2d. Its terms purport to enlarge, not to diminish." A limitation would have been written as one: "no laws shall be passed but such as are necessary and proper."
- **Conclusion.** "[I]f it does not enlarge, it cannot be construed to restrain the powers of Congress." The Clause at least removes doubt about "that vast mass of incidental powers which must be involved in the constitution, if that instrument be not a splendid bauble."
- **Application.** A corporation is an ordinary means; territorial governments, created under a comparable "needful rules" clause in Article IV, are corporations too. A bank is "a convenient, a useful, and essential instrument" of fiscal operations.
- **Judicial role.** "[T]he degree of its necessity, as has been very justly observed, is to be discussed in another place." The Court will strike measures that are prohibited or passed "under the pretext" of executing powers, but inquiring into the degree of necessity "would be to pass the line which circumscribes the judicial department, and to tread on legislative ground."

**(Marshall, C.J.) The tax**

- The States' taxing power is concurrent and vital, but the Constitution can "withdraw any subject" from it, as the Import-Export Clause shows.
- No express provision exempts the Bank. The exemption rests on a principle "so interwoven with its web, so blended with its texture, as to be incapable of being separated from it." From supremacy follow three corollaries: "a power to create implies a power to preserve"; a power to destroy in another hand is "hostile to" the power to create; and "that authority which is supreme must control."
- **Theory of the taxing power.** The political check on state taxation is that "the legislature acts upon its constituents." Federal means have no such check, because they are "given by all, for the benefit of all." A State's sovereignty "extends to every thing which exists by its own authority, or is introduced by its permission," not to federal means. The principle is "safe for the States, and safe for the Union," and spares courts "the perplexing inquiry, so unfit for the judicial department, what degree of taxation is the legitimate use, and what degree may amount to the abuse of the power."
- **Construction.** Even setting that theory aside, "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and "Confidence" is no answer. Maryland's principle would let States tax "all the means employed by the government," and the Supremacy Clause would become "empty and unmeaning declamation."
- **Federalist and concurrent power.** The cited numbers of the Federalist answered the fear that federal taxation would swallow the States' revenues, not the question of state taxes on federal means. Concurrent taxing power is not symmetrical: Congress taxing state banks is "the action of the whole on a part"; Maryland taxing the Bank is "the action of a part on the whole."

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. Marshall twice stresses unanimity: "the unanimous and decided opinion of this Court" on the charter; "We are unanimously of opinion" on the tax.

### Cold-call notes

- **What are the two holdings, and which matters more today?** Implied powers (Congress may charter the Bank) and intergovernmental immunity (Maryland may not tax it). The first is cited constantly in enumerated-powers cases; the second survives in narrowed form, as a ban on direct taxation and on discrimination against the United States.
- **Which positions does Marshall reject, and which does he adopt?** (Casebook intro.) He is clearer about what he rejects: "necessary" as indispensable, the Clause as a restriction, and incorporation as a separate sovereign power. What he adopts is the "Let the end be legitimate" test. It has three parts: a legitimate end within the enumerated powers; means that are appropriate and plainly adapted; and means that are not prohibited and are consistent with "the letter and spirit" of the Constitution.
- **Does the Clause add power, restrict it, or confirm it?** (Note 3.) Marshall says only that the Clause cannot restrict: "if it does not enlarge, it cannot be construed to restrain." As "A Friend of the Constitution" he agreed with Hampden that the Clause adds nothing beyond the incidental powers Congress would have anyway. The best reading: the Clause declares the incidental-powers principle and puts the question of degree in Congress's hands.
- **What does "proper" do?** (Note 4.) Marshall treats "necessary and proper" as a unit, with "proper" softening "necessary." Bray's hendiadys reading takes the same view. Modern opinions ([[Printz v. United States|Printz]], [[NFIB v. Sebelius|NFIB]]) treat "proper" as an independent limit tied to state sovereignty and to the requirement that the law carry an enumerated power into execution. Be ready to say which reading "letter and spirit" supports. The better answer: Marshall's "not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution" is where a "proper" limit lives.
- **Which enumerated powers does the Bank execute?** (Note 5.) Marshall never ties the Bank to a single power. He lists taxing, borrowing, commerce, war, and armies and navies, and calls the Bank an instrument of "fiscal operations." Hamilton likewise relied on the powers in combination. A critic's point: a means that serves many powers at once looks like a freestanding power.
- **What can practice settle?** (Note 2.) Marshall: practice gets "a considerable impression" on a "doubtful question" about the division of power between representatives of the people. It cannot legitimate a "bold and daring usurpation" or a question on which "the great principles of liberty" turn. He then says he would reach the same result anyway, so practice is a makeweight. Compare [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette|Madison]], for whom practice was decisive, and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]], for whom it was split.
- **Compact versus people: why does it matter here?** If the States made the Constitution, federal powers are delegated by the States and should be construed strictly in their favor. If the people made it, there is no presumption for the States. The same premise powers the tax holding: the Bank is a creature of "the people of all the States," so one State's people "cannot confer a sovereignty which will extend over them." The premise returns in [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]] and in the secession readings of Class 24 ([[Texas v. White]]).
- **"It is a constitution we are expounding": what is the argument?** It is an argument from the kind of document, not a license to update. A constitution sets "great outlines" and leaves means to be deduced, so the absence of "bank" from the text proves nothing. Critics read it as living constitutionalism. In context it is a rule for construing grants of means, and it is paired with the "intended to endure for ages to come" passage.
- **The pretext passage: link forward.** Marshall reserves review where Congress, "under the pretext of executing its powers, pass[es] laws for the accomplishment of objects not entrusted to the government." [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] (Class 19) enforces an outer limit on the Commerce Clause. NFIB (Class 23) holds that the individual mandate is not "proper" because it creates, rather than executes, a commerce power. Neither case really asks about motive. Be ready for the question whether a pretext inquiry is judicially manageable, since Marshall in the same paragraph forbids inquiry into "the degree of its necessity."
- **"Degree of necessity" is a legislative question: how deferential is that?** Very. Once the means is "appropriate" and "really calculated" to a legitimate end, the courts stop. Jackson turned this against the Court: if the degree of necessity is for the legislature, the President in his legislative capacity (the veto) must judge it himself. That argument is internally consistent with McCulloch.
- **Hypothetical: Congress charters a corporation to run a national lottery to raise revenue.** The end (revenue) is legitimate, and the means is arguably adapted to it. But is it "proper," or a pretext for regulating gambling? Under McCulloch the degree of necessity is not for the Court; the argument has to be pretext or a violation of "the spirit" of the Constitution.
- **Hypothetical: Maryland imposes a general 1% tax on all banks in the State, including the national bank.** The tax no longer singles out the Bank, but it still falls on the Bank's "operations," so Marshall's reasoning condemns it: the State "cannot confer a sovereignty" over federal means. Modern doctrine would still bar a tax whose legal incidence falls on the United States, but it permits nondiscriminatory taxes on contractors and employees ([[Graves v. New York ex rel. O'Keefe|Graves]]; [[United States v. Washington]] shows the discrimination rule is still enforced).
- **What was wrong with the tax?** (Note 6.) There are three candidate answers: States cannot tax federal operations at all (Marshall's holding); Maryland singled out the Bank (Marshall's facts, and the modern rule); or Congress would have disapproved (a preemption theory). Marshall adopts the first and disclaims any inquiry into "what degree of taxation is the legitimate use." Modern law has moved toward the second.
- **The mail-carrier extension.** [[Johnson v. Maryland]] (1920): a federal mail driver need not get a state license, but may have to obey rules on "the mode of turning at the corners of streets." Line: a State may not condition the performance of federal functions on its own permission, but generally applicable rules that only incidentally touch federal employees apply. The note asks whether courts should decide such questions or leave them to Congress. Marshall's representation argument ("In the legislature of the Union alone, are all represented") suggests Congress is the better forum. That is the same political-process logic [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]] (Class 20) applies in the other direction.
- **The asymmetry: is it principled?** Congress may tax state banks, but the States may not tax the national bank. Marshall's answer is representation: the whole acting on a part versus a part acting on the whole. The Tenth Amendment cases supply the reverse immunity (States protected from federal commandeering), with a different rationale: sovereignty rather than representation. See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- **Types of argument.** (Note 2.) Text ("necessary," "expressly," the Clause's placement, § 10's "absolutely"); structure (supremacy, representation, enumeration); purpose (a constitution "intended to endure"); consequences (treasure moved north to south; taxing the mail and the mint); practice (1791 through 1816). Marshall's structural arguments carry the tax holding, which has no text at all. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Marshall on Jefferson, Hamilton and Madison.** (Note 1.) Jefferson's "indispensable" reading of "necessary" is rejected. Hamilton's ordinary-meaning reading and his theory of incorporation as a means are adopted almost verbatim. Madison's 1791 reading is rejected, while his 1815–16 conversion is used as evidence ("statesmen of the first class ... have yielded those opinions").
- **Trump v. Slaughter and Cook.** The Banks now do Article II work. Slaughter reserved the Fed "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks," and Cook upheld the Governors' protection on that basis. The tension: McCulloch treats the Bank as an instrument for carrying federal powers into execution, which suggests it should answer to the executive. Cook treats it as the model of insulation from the President.
- **Memorable lines.** "[I]t is a constitution we are expounding." "Let the end be legitimate ..." "[T]he power to tax involves the power to destroy." "[A] splendid bauble." "[T]o hold a lighted taper to the sun." "[T]he magic of the word Confidence."
