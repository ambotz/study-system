## Rule

Congress may regulate purely local, noncommercial activity that is part of an economic "class of activities" when it rationally concludes that leaving the class unregulated would undercut a comprehensive scheme regulating an interstate market in a fungible commodity; the as-applied subclass a litigant carves out (medical, state-authorized, homegrown) does not escape if Congress rationally declined to exempt it. Lopez's economic-activity line survives, but "economic" means "the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities."

## Facts

- **Raich and Monson grew or obtained marijuana solely for their own medical use, never bought or sold, never crossing state lines.** *Thomas's whole dissent rests on this; the majority treats it as irrelevant once the class is defined broadly.*
- **California's Compassionate Use Act authorized that use on a physician's recommendation.** *Held irrelevant under the Supremacy Clause: state action "cannot circumscribe Congress' plenary commerce power."*
- **The Controlled Substances Act is a comprehensive "closed regulatory system" banning manufacture, distribution and possession of Schedule I drugs outside its terms.** *The larger scheme is what distinguishes Lopez's freestanding § 922(q).*
- **Marijuana is a fungible commodity with an established, if illegal, interstate market.** *Homegrown supply can be diverted and is indistinguishable from trafficked supply; take away the market and the Wickard analogy fails.*
- **Respondents challenged the CSA only as applied, conceding its validity on its face.** *The case turns on how narrowly the "class of activities" may be drawn.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (6–3; Stevens, J., for the Court; Scalia, J., concurring in the judgment; O'Connor, J., and Thomas, J., dissenting)

- Congress may "regulate purely local activities that are part of an economic 'class of activities' that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce."
- Under [[Wickard v. Filburn]], Congress "can regulate purely intrastate activity that is not itself 'commercial,' in that it is not produced for sale, if it concludes that failure to regulate that class of activity would undercut the regulation of the interstate market in that commodity."
- The Court asks only whether "a 'rational basis' exists" for concluding that the activities, aggregated, substantially affect interstate commerce; given enforcement difficulties and diversion, Congress rationally concluded that exempting homegrown marijuana "would leave a gaping hole in the CSA."
- The Court of Appeals' judgment (which had ordered a preliminary injunction) is vacated; the substantive due process and medical necessity claims are left open.

**Rejected**

- *Wickard is distinguishable: the Agricultural Adjustment Act exempted small farms, Filburn ran a commercial farm, and the Wickard record proved the market effect.* Stevens: the small-farm exemption did not limit the Secretary's power; Filburn's home-consumption wheat "was not treated by the Court as part of his commercial farming operation"; and congressional findings supply what the Wickard stipulation supplied.
- *Lopez and Morrison control: respondents' conduct is noneconomic and local.* Stevens: "the activities regulated by the CSA are quintessentially economic," since economics is "the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities."
- *The relevant class is intrastate, noncommercial, medical use on a physician's advice in compliance with state law, "different in kind from drug trafficking" (Ninth Circuit).* The narrower class might justify a policy exemption, but Congress rationally chose to include it; the subclass "was an essential part of the larger regulatory scheme." If medical use were beyond the outer limits, recreational home use would be too.
- *State authorization takes the conduct out of federal reach.* Supremacy Clause; [[United States v. Darby|Darby]]: the commerce power "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or non-exercise of state power."

## Context

Raich is the limit of [[United States v. Lopez]]. Lopez left open that noneconomic conduct can be reached as "an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity"; Raich walks through that door. The Lopez majority splits: Kennedy joins Stevens, Scalia concurs in the judgment on a separate theory, and only Rehnquist, O'Connor and Thomas dissent.

The two federalism signals of the decade point different ways. [[United States v. Morrison]] (2000, not assigned) extended Lopez to gender-motivated violence despite congressional findings; Raich shows that once the activity is the production or consumption of a traded commodity inside a comprehensive scheme, Lopez offers little. O'Connor's charge that Lopez is now "nothing more than a drafting guide" is the standing critique.

Scalia's concurrence relocates the substantial-effects category to the Necessary and Proper Clause and reads it through [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]: means must be "appropriate," "plainly adapted," and consistent "with the letter and spirit of the constitution," and a law that violates state sovereignty, as in [[Printz v. United States]] (Class 20), is not "proper." Thomas uses the same McCulloch text for the opposite result, invoking Marshall's warning against using the Clause as a "pretext." The Necessary and Proper route and the "proper" limit become central in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] (Class 23), where the question is whether a mandate to buy insurance is a regulation of commerce or a necessary and proper means to the ACA's insurance reforms. [[United States v. Comstock]] (2010, not assigned; next in the casebook) upheld federal civil commitment of sexually dangerous prisoners under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Thomas's dissent restates his [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] originalism ("commerce" as buying, selling and transporting) and adds an as-applied Necessary and Proper analysis. After the casebook, Thomas wrote in 2021 (statement respecting denial of certiorari in [[Standing Akimbo, LLC v. United States]]) that federal tolerance of state marijuana markets had undermined the premise of Raich's "gaping hole" reasoning.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Raich and Monson sued the Attorney General and the DEA Administrator for declaratory and injunctive relief barring enforcement of the CSA against their personal medical cultivation, possession and use.
- The Ninth Circuit held the CSA unconstitutional as applied and directed a preliminary injunction; the Supreme Court vacated, leaving the due process and medical necessity claims for remand.

### Facts

California voters passed Proposition 215, the Compassionate Use Act of 1996, which exempts from state prosecution patients and caregivers who possess or cultivate marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation. Angel Raich and Diane Monson, Californians with serious medical conditions, used marijuana under the Act. In 2002 county deputies and DEA agents came to Monson's home; the county officials concluded her use was lawful under state law, but after a three-hour standoff the federal agents seized and destroyed her six cannabis plants. The federal Controlled Substances Act, Title II of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, had consolidated federal drug law into a closed system aimed at the "international and interstate traffic in illicit drugs" and at diversion from legitimate channels, listing marijuana in Schedule I.

### Issue

- Does the power to regulate interstate commerce, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, authorize Congress to prohibit the local cultivation and possession of marijuana for personal medical use in compliance with state law?

### Holding

- Yes. Homegrown marijuana is part of an economic class of activities (production and consumption of a fungible commodity with an interstate market), and Congress rationally concluded that exempting it would undercut the CSA's comprehensive regulation of that market; the narrower medical, state-authorized subclass does not have to be carved out.

### Rule

- Congress may regulate local activities that are part of an economic "class of activities" with a substantial effect on interstate commerce; "the de minimis character of individual instances arising under that statute is of no consequence" (Stevens, quoting Lopez).
- Review is for a rational basis, not proof of effects "in fact" (Stevens).
- Congress need not exempt a subclass if including it in "the larger regulatory scheme" is rational; the Court "refuse[s] to excise individual components of that larger scheme" (Stevens).
- State law cannot shrink the commerce power (Supremacy Clause; Darby).
- Congress may regulate even noneconomic local activity "if that regulation is a necessary part of a more general regulation of interstate commerce," so long as the means are "reasonably adapted" to a legitimate end (Scalia).

### Reasoning

**(Stevens, J., for the Court)**

- The challenge is narrow: respondents concede the CSA is valid and attack only its application to them.
- Wickard is "of particular relevance," and "[t]he similarities between this case and Wickard are striking": home cultivation of a fungible commodity, a statute controlling supply and demand in a national market, and the risk that high prices draw home supply into that market.
- The difference in aims does not matter: Wickard's statute stabilized prices, the CSA seeks to eliminate the market "in their entirety," but in both "production of the commodity meant for home consumption . . . has a substantial effect on supply and demand in the national market for that commodity."
- The task is "a modest one": rational basis. Enforcement problems in distinguishing local from interstate marijuana and diversion risk make a "gaping hole" rational.
- Lopez and Morrison involved noneconomic activity; the CSA "regulates the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities for which there is an established, and lucrative, interstate market."
- Relief lies in reclassification under the statute and in "the democratic process, in which the voices of voters allied with these respondents may one day be heard in the halls of Congress."

**(Scalia, J., concurring in the judgment)**

- The third Lopez category is "misleading and incomplete": activities that substantially affect commerce "are not themselves part of interstate commerce, and thus the power to regulate them cannot come from the Commerce Clause alone" but from the Necessary and Proper Clause.
- Two routes: regulating activities with substantial effects, and regulating intrastate activity (even noneconomic) needed to make an interstate regulation effective ([[United States v. Wrightwood Dairy Co|Wrightwood Dairy]]: Congress "possesses every power needed to make that regulation effective").
- The second route does not reduce Lopez to a drafting guide: it "can only be exercised in conjunction with congressional regulation of an interstate market, and it extends only to those measures necessary to make the interstate regulation effective."
- McCulloch's limits are "not merely hortatory": means must be "appropriate" and "plainly adapted," not "prohibited," and consistent with "the letter and spirit of the constitution"; Printz shows a law violating state sovereignty is not "proper."
- Applied: Congress may seek to "extinguish the interstate market in Schedule I controlled substances"; "[t]hat simple possession is a noneconomic activity is immaterial"; marijuana grown at home "is never more than an instant from the interstate market."

### Dissent / concurrence

**(O'Connor, J., with Rehnquist, C.J., and Thomas, J., as to all but Part III, dissenting)**

- Federalism's virtue is state experimentation: "a single courageous State may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory" (Brandeis in [[New State Ice Co. v. Liebmann]]); California has made its own judgment on medical marijuana.
- Letting Congress immunize local regulation by "packaging regulation of local activity in broader schemes, is tantamount to removing meaningful limits on the Commerce Clause"; Lopez becomes "nothing more than a drafting guide."
- The class must be drawn by "objective markers": federal and state law already treat medical and recreational use as distinct, so the relevant conduct is personal medical cultivation, possession and use.
- "The Court's definition of economic activity is breathtaking": home care substitutes for daycare, charades for movie tickets, gardening for the supermarket; "To draw the line wherever private activity affects the demand for market goods is to draw no line at all."
- No evidence: the government never showed that homegrown medical users are a class large enough to affect the national market or threaten the CSA; "Piling assertion upon assertion does not, in my view, satisfy the substantiality test of Lopez and Morrison." Recasting the case under the Necessary and Proper Clause "does not change the analysis significantly."

**(Thomas, J., dissenting)**

- The marijuana "has never been bought or sold, that has never crossed state lines, and that has had no demonstrable effect on the national market"; if Congress can reach it, "it can regulate virtually anything."
- Commerce is "selling, buying, and bartering, as well as transporting for these purposes"; respondents' conduct is outside the Commerce Clause itself.
- Necessary: the ban must be "plainly adapted," with "an 'obvious, simple, and direct relation'" to regulating interstate trafficking, not merely "a reasonable means." Facially the intrastate ban may qualify, but as applied, state-authorized medical users are "a distinct and separable subclass" that does not undermine the interstate ban.
- Proper: even a necessary means may not subvert "basic principles of federalism and dual sovereignty"; the government's theory (any commodity with an interstate market) would make the Clause "a 'pretext . . . for the accomplishment of objects not intrusted to the government'" (McCulloch).

### Cold-call notes

- **Why does Raich come out the other way from Lopez?** Three differences, each a lever. (1) Economic activity: growing and consuming a commodity is "production . . . and consumption of commodities"; possessing a gun is not. (2) Comprehensive scheme: the CSA regulates a whole interstate market, and local activity could undercut it; § 922(q) stood alone. (3) Fungible commodity with an interstate market: homegrown marijuana is interchangeable with, and divertible into, the trafficked supply; a school-zone gun is not part of any market the statute regulates. Aggregation ([[Wickard v. Filburn|Wickard]]) then does the rest.
- **Remove the scheme.** Hypo: Congress passes a freestanding statute banning only homegrown medical marijuana. No "larger regulation" to protect, so the Scalia route closes; Stevens's route depends on calling home cultivation "economic" and aggregating it on its own. Much closer to Lopez.
- **Remove the market.** Hypo: Congress regulates a homemade product with no interstate market (a family recipe). There is nothing to divert and no supply-demand effect; the Wickard analogy fails at its premise.
- **Re-draft Lopez.** O'Connor's point: re-enact § 922(q) as part of a national firearms scheme covering "transfer or possession of a firearm anywhere in the nation." Under Stevens, does gun possession near schools become regulable as part of the class? Under Scalia, only if exempting it "could . . . undercut" the interstate regulation. Is that plausible for guns near schools?
- **Draw the class.** The whole case is a fight over the "class of activities." Stevens: all intrastate marijuana, because Congress chose not to exempt. O'Connor: personal medical use, by "objective markers" in law. Thomas: state-authorized medical users as a "distinct and separable subclass." Who decides the class, Congress or the Court? O'Connor's framing: "more than nothing" but "less than everything."
- **Does state law matter?** Stevens: no; Supremacy Clause and Darby. O'Connor: state regulation marks an objective line and shows the states' laboratory role. Thomas: state controls (ID cards, affirmative defenses) show medical marijuana is separable from trafficking, so exempting it would not undercut enforcement. Note 4 asks this directly.
- **Scalia versus Thomas on Necessary and Proper.** Both read McCulloch. Scalia asks whether the means are "reasonably adapted" to a legitimate end and emphasizes the market-regulation predicate; Thomas demands an "obvious, simple, and direct relation," tests the application to the subclass, and gives "proper" independent force. Scalia's "proper" limit points to Printz-style state sovereignty; Thomas's points to the police power reserved to the states. Which is closer to Marshall? McCulloch rejected "absolutely necessary," which cuts toward Scalia on "necessary"; Marshall's "pretext" warning cuts toward Thomas on "proper."
- **Is Raich a Commerce Clause case or a Necessary and Proper case (Note 1)?** Stevens cites both clauses together; Scalia says the third Lopez category always was a Necessary and Proper power. It matters because the Necessary and Proper route has its own limits (derivative of an interstate regulation; "proper") and because the same framing returns in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]].
- **Note 3's patchwork problem.** If the dissenters are right, can in-state-only farmers opt out of federal food-safety law, or hobbyist pilots out of federal aviation rules? If the majority is right, can Congress reach anything local so long as the scheme is broad enough ("too big to fail")? Scalia's limit is the answer to the first half; O'Connor's "drafting guide" is the objection to the second.
- **The no-limiting-principle argument.** Lopez: "hard pressed to posit any activity." Raich dissents: the definition of economics is "breathtaking" (O'Connor); Congress "can regulate virtually anything" (Thomas). In NFIB the argument returns against a mandate to buy insurance; be ready to say which limit Raich left standing (activity, a market, a scheme) and which one NFIB adds.
- **Originalism versus doctrine.** Thomas is consistent from Lopez to Raich. Scalia, who joined Lopez, reaches the result through the Necessary and Proper Clause rather than the effects test; ask whether his route narrows the doctrine or broadens it (he allows regulation of activity that does not itself "substantially affect" commerce).
- **Memorable details and lines.** Six plants seized after a three-hour standoff at Monson's home. Stevens: "a gaping hole in the CSA"; "quintessentially economic." Scalia: "never more than an instant from the interstate market." O'Connor: "laboratory"; "drafting guide"; charades substitute for movie tickets.
