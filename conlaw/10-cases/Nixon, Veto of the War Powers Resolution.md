## What it is

President Nixon's message of October 24, 1973 returning House Joint Resolution 542, the War
Powers Resolution, without his approval. Congress repassed the resolution over the veto in
November 1973. The casebook follows the message with notes on the WPR's constitutionality and two
post-WPR case studies: the 1990–91 Gulf War and the 1999 Kosovo bombing.

The message is the executive branch's founding statement of its position on the WPR, and every
later President has held some version of it. Read it against the statute's text (pp. 316–318),
which gets no separate reading file here.

## What to notice

- **The statute Nixon is vetoing, in four provisions.**
  - § 2(c): the President's power to introduce forces into hostilities is exercised "only pursuant
    to (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency
    created by attack upon the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed
    forces." Category (3) is the Convention's repel-attacks carve-out and the Prize Cases rule.
  - § 5(b): the 60-day clock. The President "shall terminate" the use of forces within sixty days
    of the § 4 report unless Congress declares war, specifically authorizes, extends the period by
    law, or cannot meet; plus up to thirty days for safe withdrawal.
  - § 5(c): forces "shall be removed by the President if the Congress so directs by concurrent
    resolution," a resolution not presented to the President.
  - § 8(a): authority "shall not be inferred" from any law, "including any provision contained in
    an appropriation Act," or any treaty, unless it specifically says it is authorization within
    the meaning of the WPR. § 8(c) disclaims any intent "to alter the constitutional authority of
    the Congress or of the President."
- **Nixon's constitutional objection.** The resolution would "take away, by a mere legislative
  act, authorities which the President has properly exercised under the Constitution for almost
  200 years." "The only way in which the constitutional powers of a branch of the Government can
  be altered is by amending the Constitution."
- **The objection begs the question on the 60-day clock.** It assumes the President has
  constitutional power to continue hostilities indefinitely without Congress. If the Declare War
  Clause means what the Convention debate suggests, the President never had that power, and
  § 5(b) takes nothing away; it only sets a default rule for how long the repel-attacks and
  emergency power lasts. In Youngstown terms the clock turns congressional silence into a
  standing denial, converting Jackson's category two into category three after sixty days. That is
  valid unless the power is conclusive and preclusive in the President, beyond Congress's
  reach, and the power to continue a war is the least plausible candidate.
- **The objection is right on the concurrent resolution.** A concurrent resolution "does not
  normally have the force of law, since it denies the President his constitutional role in
  approving legislation." [[INS v. Chadha]] (1983) vindicated that point ten years later. The
  casebook's 2020 Iran episode shows the consequence: a resolution under the WPR was presented to
  the President and vetoed (see [[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).
- **The practical objections.** An adversary would "postpone serious negotiations until the 60
  days were up"; a deadline "could lead to an escalation of hostilities"; the resolution would
  strip "quiet diplomacy backed by subtle shifts in our military deployments"; it would cast doubt
  on rescues, protection of fishing boats and responses to hijackings; § 8 would bar "fulfilling
  our obligations under the NATO treaty"; and "the bill is somewhat vague as to when the 60 day
  rule would apply." The last point proved the most durable: the trigger is "hostilities," and
  much later practice is a fight about whether limited air operations are "hostilities."
- **The accountability argument.** Congress should act "through a positive action, with full
  debate on the merits . . . and with each member taking the responsibility of casting a yes or
  no vote." The resolution "would give every future Congress the ability to handcuff every future
  President merely by doing nothing and sitting still." This is the strongest part of the message
  and it turns the Framers' logic around: Nixon says a Congress that does nothing should not be
  able to stop a war; the Convention's premise was that a war nobody in Congress votes for should
  not begin. Mason was "for clogging rather than facilitating war."
- **What Nixon accepts.** § 3 consultation is "consistent with the desire of this Administration."
  He proposes a nonpartisan commission on the constitutional roles of the branches in foreign
  affairs, including war powers, international agreements "and the question of Executive
  privilege," a phrase that looks different a year later (see [[United States v. Nixon]]).
- **Note 1: is the WPR constitutional, and does it authorize anything?**:
  - § 2(c) as a statement of constitutional law is not binding on the President; as a
    description it is too narrow even by [[The Prize Cases]], which do not limit defensive force
    to attacks on U.S. territory or forces, and too narrow by the executive's practice of rescues.
  - § 8(c)(2) disclaims any grant of authority, so the WPR cannot be read as authorizing sixty
    days of war, though in practice it has been treated as a de facto license.
  - § 8(a)'s clear-statement rule binds only the interpretation of past statutes; a later
    Congress can override it in any authorization, and the 2001 AUMF expressly complies with it.
  - § 5(c) is unconstitutional after [[INS v. Chadha]]; the question is severability.
- **The executive's response in practice.** Presidents "construed it narrowly, refused to concede
  its constitutionality, and sometimes have asserted it to be unconstitutional," while reporting
  "in a manner consistent with the Act's requirements, seeking to avoid confrontation" (p. 316).
  Reporting "consistent with" rather than "pursuant to" keeps the constitutional objection alive.
  That matters for liquidation: a practice can settle meaning only if both branches accept it, and
  here each branch has deliberately preserved its position.
- **The Gulf War (Note 2).** Desert Shield (defending Saudi Arabia) versus the planned offensive
  to expel Iraq from Kuwait after U.N. authorization. Two district courts in D.C. dismissed on
  different grounds:
  - [[Dellums v. Bush]] (1990): not ripe, because Congress had not acted and an attack was not
    certain; but not a political question, because the Constitution supplies a clear standard
    requiring congressional authorization.
  - [[Ange v. Bush]] (1990): a political question, because the Constitution gives no basis for
    resolving a dispute between the branches' war powers.
  - Congress then authorized force, mooting the question. Dellums is the better view on
    justiciability: the allocation of a power is a legal question, as [[The Prize Cases]] show.
    Ripeness is the real barrier, since a court cannot decide whether Congress has refused before
    Congress votes.
- **Kosovo (Note 3).** In one day the House failed on a tie to support the bombing, rejected a
  declaration of war, defeated a § 5(c) removal resolution, and required authorization for ground
  troops; Congress then passed a supplemental appropriation. Under Youngstown that record defies
  classification. The appropriation looks like category one; the rejected declaration and the
  ground-troops resolution look like category three; § 8(a) says the appropriation cannot count.
  The best reading is that Congress declined to authorize and declined to stop, which is category
  two for air operations and category three for ground troops.
- **Did the WPR's clock bind in Kosovo?** If the bombing ran past sixty days without a "specific
  authorization," either the WPR was violated or the supplemental appropriation counted despite
  § 8(a). The casebook leaves the question open ("Did it need to be?"): the executive answer is
  that a statute cannot cut off a constitutional power, the congressional answer is § 5(b).
- **Note 4 sets up the AUMF.** "With the attacks on September 11, 2001, the war powers pendulum
  swung once again." Is the AUMF "the functional equivalent of a declaration of war"? See
  [[Modern Applications of the War Power]].
