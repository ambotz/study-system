## Rule

A court will not strike down a tax that is otherwise within the taxing power because of the motive Congress had in laying the tax; the remedy for an abuse of the taxing power lies with the people, not the judiciary.

## Facts

- Congress laid an excise tax on oleomargarine, a butter substitute made from other fats and oils.
- The oleomargarine tax was "almost certainly enacted to benefit butter producers." *The Court refused to treat that motive as a ground of invalidity.*
- The challengers did not deny that Congress could tax oleomargarine; they argued that a lawful power had been used for an unlawful purpose. *The Court held that argument asks the judiciary to review motive, which is not its function.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The oleomargarine tax is valid.
- The judicial duty to uphold the Constitution does not include "the responsibility of correcting every possible abuse arising from the exercise by the other departments of their conceded authority."
- Striking down a tax for its motive would "overthrow the entire distinction between the legislative, judicial, and executive departments of the government" and "would be a mere act of judicial usurpation."
- The remedy for an abuse of the taxing power "lies, not in the abuse by the judicial authority of its functions, but in the people."

**Rejected**

- *If a lawful power may be exerted for an unlawful purpose, "all limitations of power must disappear," and the judiciary's function of confining each department to its authority "will be of no avail."* The Court answered that the argument reduces to the claim that "the abuse by one department of the government of its lawful powers is to be corrected by the abuse of its powers by another department."

## Context

McCray is the third case in the survey in [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]], after the *License Tax Cases* (Congress may tax what it cannot regulate) and *Veazie Bank v. Fenno* (the size of a tax is for Congress). McCray adds that the motive for a tax is also off limits. The casebook asks whether any limit on the taxing power survives once motive is ignored. [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] (1922) struck down the child labor tax by distinguishing McCray: the oleomargarine tax did not involve "the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern and business with a heavy exaction to promote the efficacy of such regulation." [[United States v. Kahriger]] (1953) then cited McCray among the cases upholding taxes whose "intent to curtail and hinder, as well as tax," was "manifest." See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].

The motive rule outlived the taxing context. In *United States v. O'Brien* (1968), the draft-card-burning case, the Court quoted McCray for the "familiar principle" that it "will not strike down an otherwise constitutional statute on the basis of an alleged illicit legislative motive."
