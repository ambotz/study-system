## What it is

The casebook's account (pp. 196–199, under "Foundations of the Removal Power: The Decision of 1789") of the First Congress's debate over whether the President could remove the heads of the new executive departments, with the later Jackson and Johnson removal fights. It opens Part C, "Supervising the Executive Branch," and leads into the doctrinal history of [[Myers v. United States|Myers]], [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]]. The debate is the leading example of constitutional meaning fixed by early practice rather than by text or judicial decision.

## What to notice

- **The silence.** Article II "makes rules for the appointment of executive branch officers. But it says nothing expressly about their removal (except via impeachment)." The editors ask whether the Vesting Clause, the Take Care Clause, or the Senate's role in appointment fills the gap, or whether "all of these decisions [should] be up to Congress." They also ask a larger question: whether removal is the President's only supervisory tool, or whether he may "simply tell other members of the executive branch how to do their jobs."
- **Four positions in the House, each with a textual hook.**
  - *Impeachment only* (William Smith): an officer "could only be removed by an impeachment before the senate, and that being once in office, he must remain there until convicted upon impeachment." Hook: the only removal provision in the text.
  - *President alone, by constitutional right* (Madison, Ames): removal is part of "the executive Power." Hook: the Vesting Clause and the Take Care Clause.
  - *President with Senate consent* (Theodorick Bland): "the power which appointed should remove." Otherwise the Senate's role in appointment "would be rendered almost nugatory." Hook: the Appointments Clause.
  - *Whatever Congress decides* (John Laurance): because the Constitution is silent on tenure, it "depended upon the will of the legislature" to set the terms of office: a term of years, good behavior, removal for cause, or removal "for any cause he thinks proper." Hook: Congress's power to establish offices "by Law" and the Necessary and Proper Clause.
- **Madison's answer to Smith.** Impeachment is "a supplemental security for the good behavior of the public officers." Removal makes the President "in a peculiar manner, responsible for their conduct," and exposes him to impeachment if he "neglects to superintend their conduct." This is [[The Federalist No. 70]]'s responsibility argument turned into a power over subordinates.
- **Madison's Vesting Clause argument.** "The constitution affirms, that the executive power shall be vested in the president: Are there exceptions to this proposition? Yes there are." The Senate's role in appointment is one; "Have we a right to extend this exception? I believe not." And: "if any power whatsoever is in its nature executive it is the power of appointing, overseeing, and controling those who execute the laws." The structure of the argument matters: the Vesting Clause is a grant of power, and the specific clauses are exceptions to it, to be read narrowly. That is the reading Jackson rejected for domestic seizures in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]].
- **Ames's two arguments.**
  - Structure and necessity: the President cannot "personally execute all the laws," so he "must therefore have assistants," and "in order that he may be responsible to his country, he must have a choice in selecting his assistants, a control over them, with power to remove them."
  - Take Care: the President "cannot do this without he has a control over officers appointed to aid him." Without removal "you virtually destroy his responsibility, the great security which this constitution holds out to the people of America."
- **What was actually decided.** "[T]he final statute did indeed contain language about presidential removal, so Madison's side won the immediate debate." The editors are careful about what the vote shows:
  - It is "not so clear" whether the vote endorsed Madison's constitutional theory "or a mixed coalition of those who believed in executive power and those who thought Congress could decide."
  - "[I]t is clear that the result was a rejection of the other two positions—that the Senate must be involved in removal, and that impeachment is the only method of removal."
- **The three readings of the Decision.** Each one has a later Justice behind it.
  - *Constitutional recognition.* Congress acknowledged a removal power the Vesting Clause already gives the President. Prakash's 2006 article defends this reading, and Taft's opinion in [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] (1926) adopts it. The drafting point usually cited is that the final bill spoke of the officer's removal by the President as something that might happen, rather than conferring a power to remove.
  - *Legislative grant.* A majority believed only that Congress could give the President the power, and chose to. On this reading Laurance's position won, and Congress may condition the power when it creates other offices. This is the reading most congenial to [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]].
  - *Limited to its subject.* Whatever the House decided, it decided for a "purely executive" officer responsible to the President alone. Sutherland takes this line in Humphrey's: the debates did not consider "other than executive officers," and Madison himself thought the Comptroller of the Treasury, whose duties "partook of the judiciary quality," might be different.
  - The one thing all three readings share is the rejection of Bland's and Smith's positions. Senate consent to removal, which the Tenure of Office Act and the Myers statute later demanded, was the position the First Congress voted down.
- **The Decision as "liquidation."** Madison predicted that "[t]he decision that is at this time made will become the permanent exposition of the constitution." That is Madison's idea of liquidation: where the text is indeterminate, a deliberate course of practice by the political branches can settle its meaning, and the settlement then binds. Baude's own scholarship (Constitutional Liquidation, 2019) develops the idea and asks three things of it: an indeterminate text, a deliberate course of practice, and a settlement accepted by the public. Apply the three to the Decision of 1789:
  - Indeterminacy: real. The text is silent on removal, and four positions each had a textual argument.
  - Deliberate practice: strong. The debate "went on for weeks," and the House was debating the constitutional question, not only policy.
  - Settlement: contested. The Senate divided closely on the bill, Jackson was censured for a removal in the 1830s, and Congress passed the Tenure of Office Act in 1867. The settlement may have been only about the negative propositions (no Senate role, not impeachment only), which is what the editors say is "clear."
  - The difference between liquidation and ordinary precedent: a liquidated meaning claims to be what the Constitution means, not merely a practice the Court defers to. Compare Frankfurter's historical gloss in Youngstown, which needs long acquiescence but no single deliberate decision.
- **Jackson's "Protest."** Jackson fired a Treasury Secretary who would not remove federal deposits from the Bank of the United States. The Senate censured him; he argued the Vesting Clause gave him "unlimited power to remove executive branch subordinates"; when his party won, the Senate expunged the censure. Practice after 1789 adds a political ratification of the executive reading.
- **Johnson and the Tenure of Office Act.** Congress required Senate consent before removals became permanent. Johnson fired Stanton in violation of it, was impeached, and "the Senate came within one vote of removing him." The Act was diluted under Grant and repealed under Cleveland. The repeal is evidence of settlement against Bland's position; the impeachment is evidence that the settlement was not complete in 1868.
- **Hypotheticals.**
  - A statute requires Senate consent to remove a cabinet secretary: invalid under every reading of 1789, and under [[Myers v. United States|Myers]].
  - A statute lets the President remove a cabinet secretary only for inefficiency, neglect or malfeasance: invalid on the constitutional-recognition reading; valid on the legislative-grant reading; and on the limited-to-its-subject reading invalid, because a secretary is the paradigm purely executive officer.
  - The same for-cause limit on a multimember commission: this is where the three readings diverge most, and it is [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] and [[Trump v. Slaughter]].
