## What it is

Optional pre-quarter background, pp. 35–40: six themes the casebook says recur throughout the book. It follows The Federalist No. 39, whose conclusion ("neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both") appears at the top of p. 35.

## What to notice

- **Federalist 39's scorecard**, the lead-in. In its foundation federal; in the sources of its powers partly federal and partly national; in their operation national; in their extent federal (enumerated objects only, with a "residuary and inviolable sovereignty" in the states); in the amendment process neither wholly one nor the other.
- **1. A written constitution.** Supreme law (Art. VI, the Supremacy Clause), which is the foundation of judicial review and of the principle that all government is constrained by law. St. George Tucker: "the constitution is not an 'ideal thing,' but a real existence: it can be produced in a visible form."
- **2. Republicanism and popular sovereignty.** "We the People": neither the states nor the national government is sovereign; the people are. The principle is present but imperfect in the original scheme, which was tainted by accommodation of slavery, and was extended by the voting amendments (XV, XVII, XIX, XXIII, XXIV, XXVI).
- **3. Separation of powers.** The division is **functional** (legislative, executive, judicial), not the English "mixed regime" of king, lords and commons. Montesquieu is the source. The Anti-Federalists objected that checks and balances intermingled the powers Montesquieu said to keep apart; John Adams preferred an elected mixed regime. Madison defended the design in The Federalist Nos. 47–51: different electorates, staggered terms (six years to replace everyone), and a "will" of its own for each branch.
- **4. Federalism.** Supremacy for valid national action, but limited, enumerated national powers. The case for a central government: collective-action and free-rider problems (the navy example), economies of scale, protection of minorities against states. The case for local government: varied preferences, competition, experimentation, and closer popular control. Federalism "tends to lead to some form of judicial review," because someone must umpire.
- **5. Rights.** A few in the original (Art. I, §§ 9 and 10); most in the amendments. The Bill of Rights against the national government; the Reconstruction Amendments against the states, a "Second Founding." The amendments also steadily expanded national power: enforcement clauses in XIII–XV, the income tax (XVI) and the direct election of senators (XVII).
- **6. The imperfect congruence of the Constitution with justice.** The Constitution does not forbid all bad things or require all good ones, and it permitted slavery. Nearly a third of the Convention's delegates left or refused to sign. Washington: "the best that could be obtained at this time," with "a constitutional door . . . op[e]ned for amendment." Spencer Roane warned that treating the Constitution as perfect makes it "a nose of wax."
- **The interpretive risk in the sixth theme.** If the Constitution is assumed perfect, the interpreter will make it match whatever he thinks perfection is. The question left for you: who should fix the imperfections, and how?
