## Rule

A federal officer acts "in pursuance of a law of the United States" for habeas purposes even when no statute authorizes what he did, because the President's duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed reaches "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself"; an act within that authority cannot be punished by a State.

## Facts

- **Neagle was a deputy marshal assigned to guard Justice Field on circuit, at the Attorney General’s direction and under no statute.** *The authority is executive, not legislative, which is what makes "law" the question.*
- **Terry had drawn a bowie knife in Field’s courtroom in 1888, and he and his wife had since threatened Field’s life.** *Without a credible threat the protective order is unreasonable and the whole chain fails.*
- **At Lathrop on August 14, 1889, Terry struck Field twice from behind and "had his arm drawn back and his fist doubled up, apparently to strike a third blow."** *Gives Neagle a defense-of-another justification needing no federal authority.*
- **Told "Stop! stop! I am an officer!", Terry thrust his hand toward his bosom, "as Neagle felt sure, with the purpose of drawing a bowie-knife"; Neagle fired twice.** *Necessity rests on Neagle’s belief, not on what Terry carried.*
- **California charged Neagle with murder, and the federal circuit court discharged him before any state trial.** *Decisive for the dissent: the writ withdrew the case from the state courts rather than reviewing them.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (6–2; Miller, J., for the Court; Lamar, J., dissenting, joined by Fuller, C.J.; Field, J., took no part)

- Rev. Stat. § 753 reaches a prisoner held "for an act done or omitted in pursuance of a law of the United States, or of an order, process or decree of a court or judge thereof," and to read "law" as meaning only a statute would be "an unwarranted restriction of the meaning of a law designed to extend in a liberal manner the benefit of the writ of habeas corpus."
- "In the view we take of the Constitution of the United States, any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument, or any duty of the marshal to be derived from the general scope of his duties under the laws of the United States, is a 'law,' within the meaning of this phrase."
- The Take Care Clause is not confined to enacted text: "Is this duty limited to the enforcement of acts of congress or of treaties of the United States according to their express terms; or does it include the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself, our international relations, and all the protection implied by the nature of the government under the constitution?"
- "We cannot doubt the power of the President to take measures for the protection of a judge . . . who, while in the discharge of the duties of his office, is threatened with a personal attack," and the same authority would let him guard the mails against robbery without waiting for Congress.
- There is a federal peace to keep: "That there is a peace of the United States; that a man assaulting a judge of the United States while in the discharge of his duties violates that peace." Rev. Stat. § 788 gives marshals "the same powers in executing the laws of the United States as the sheriffs and their deputies in such state may have, by law, in executing the laws thereof."
- On the merits of the killing, Neagle "did justifiably interfere by shooting Terry while in the act of assaulting Mr. Justice Field, whom he had already struck two or three times," and "it became necessary for the protection of Judge Field . . . to kill Terry."
- Neagle "was in custody for an act done in pursuance of a law of the United States, and was imprisoned in violation of the Constitution and laws of the United States." "We therefore affirm the judgment of the circuit court authorizing his discharge from the custody of the sheriff of San Joaquin county."

**Rejected**

- *"Law of the United States" means an enacted law; Congress has legislated in great detail about marshals and has never authorized bodyguards for judges, so the Attorney General's instruction is an executive order and nothing more, and a statute that discharges prisoners held under "a law" cannot be satisfied by the absence of one.* Miller: the phrase covers "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible" from the Constitution, and the narrow reading would be "an unwarranted restriction" on a remedial statute; the executive's duty to protect the courts is itself the law being executed.
- *A State's power to punish a homicide committed on its soil is the core of its reserved authority, and if federal officers may be lifted out of state criminal process on the executive's own say-so the States lose control of the crimes committed within them.* Miller: the officer was doing what federal law required, and the Supremacy Clause leaves no room for a State to punish the performance of a federal duty; the marshal's relation to "the peace of the United States" is the sheriff's to the peace of the State.
- *Whether Neagle was justified is a question of California law for a California jury, and he may raise his federal defense at trial and, if it is rejected, on writ of error.* Miller: the evidence taken at the habeas hearing showed the killing was necessary, and a federal officer held for doing his federal duty need not stand trial to establish it.
- *Even accepting a federal protective power, it must come from somewhere in Article II's enumeration, and guarding a Justice on a train is not "executing" anything Congress enacted.* Miller: the government would be "without the means of self-protection" on that view; the protection implied "by the nature of the government under the constitution" supplies the power.

## Context

Neagle is the executive-power half of a pair with [[In re Debs]] (1895), where the Court sustained the Attorney General's suit for an injunction against the Pullman strike on a similarly implied authority to protect federal instrumentalities; read together they are the nineteenth-century authority for the proposition that the executive may act to protect the government's own functions without a statute. The jurisdictional half of Neagle belongs with [[Tennessee v. Davis]] (1880), which let a federal revenue officer remove a state murder prosecution to federal court, and with the Supremacy Clause line running back to [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]: a State may not tax, obstruct or punish the operations of the federal government.

The case sits against [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]. Justice Jackson's concurrence there treats Neagle-style protective power as the real residue of inherent executive authority, and Justice Black's opinion for the Court treats the absence of a statute as fatal. Which of those is right about Neagle is the question the class turns on. It also sits against the Take Care materials of Class 8: [[United States v. Cox]] and [[Adams v. Richardson]] draw the line between executive discretion not to enforce and executive obligation, and Neagle is the same clause read as a source of power rather than as a constraint.

For Article VI, Neagle is the Supremacy Clause applied to executive action rather than to statutes, and it forces the question of what "in Pursuance thereof" does. The oath clause is the other Article VI provision in play: the President swears to the Constitution, not to Congress, which is the textual seed of departmentalism in [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] and the target of [[Cooper v. Aaron]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Sheriff Cunningham of San Joaquin County held Neagle on a state murder warrant issued by Justice Swain of Stockton; Neagle petitioned the Circuit Court for the Northern District of California for habeas corpus on August 16, 1889, alleging he was imprisoned "for an act done in pursuance of a law of the United States."
- The circuit court (Sawyer, Circuit Judge, with Sabin, District Judge) took testimony and discharged him; the sheriff appealed, and the Supreme Court decided the case on April 14, 1890, with Field taking no part. The case is reported both as In re Neagle and as Cunningham v. Neagle.

### Facts

The quarrel began in the Sharon litigation, where a federal circuit court on which Justice Field sat declared Sarah Althea Hill's purported marriage contract with William Sharon a forgery. She married David S. Terry, a former Chief Justice of California. On September 3, 1888, when Field ruled against her on a bill of revivor, she accused him from the courtroom of having been bought and refused to leave; Terry struck the marshal in the face and reached for a bowie knife, and both Terrys were sentenced for contempt. Afterward each made threats against Field, Terry saying of the danger, "Well, that's as good a thing as I want to get." On April 27, 1889, Attorney General W. H. H. Miller directed the marshal to employ special deputies so that the judges would "feel entirely safe and free from anxiety." Neagle was appointed and travelled with Field on circuit.

### Issue

- Does "an act done . . . in pursuance of a law of the United States" in Rev. Stat. § 753 reach an act done under executive authority that no statute confers?
- Was Neagle's killing of Terry within that authority and necessary to it, so that California may not hold him for trial?

### Holding

- Yes. "Law" includes any obligation "fairly and properly inferrible" from the Constitution, including the President's duty to protect the judges of the United States; the narrower reading would be "an unwarranted restriction" on a remedial habeas statute.
- Yes. On the evidence taken below the killing was necessary to protect Field from an assault in progress, so Neagle was imprisoned "in violation of the Constitution and laws of the United States" and was rightly discharged.

### Rule

- The habeas remedy for federal officers turns on whether the act was federally authorized, not on whether a statute commanded it.
- The Take Care Clause obliges the President to execute "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself," not only acts of Congress and treaties according to their express terms.
- Protection of the courts, the mails and other federal instrumentalities is within that duty, and the President may order it without waiting for Congress.
- A federal officer who does what federal law authorizes is beyond state criminal punishment for the act.
- Rev. Stat. § 788 gives marshals, in executing federal law, the powers sheriffs have in executing state law, which includes the common-law power to keep the peace.

### Reasoning

**(Miller, J., for the Court)**

- **The statutory question comes first, and it is a question about the word "law."** Section 753 reaches a prisoner held for an act done "in pursuance of a law of the United States." Reading "law" as "statute" would be "an unwarranted restriction of the meaning of a law designed to extend in a liberal manner the benefit of the writ of habeas corpus."
- **The answer is stated as a definition, not as a balance.** "[A]ny obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument, or any duty of the marshal to be derived from the general scope of his duties under the laws of the United States, is a 'law,' within the meaning of this phrase." Everything else in the opinion is an effort to locate such an obligation.
- **The obligation is located in Article II, § 3.** Miller poses the question rhetorically: "Is this duty limited to the enforcement of acts of congress or of treaties of the United States according to their express terms; or does it include the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself, our international relations, and all the protection implied by the nature of the government under the constitution?" The question answers itself in the opinion's structure; no separate argument for the broader reading is offered.
- **The concession that there is no statute is made openly.** "It is not supposed that any special act of Congress exists which authorizes the marshals or deputy marshals of the United States in express terms to accompany the judges." The absence of a statute is treated as the premise of the holding rather than as an obstacle to it.
- **Necessity from consequences.** If the President may not act without express authority, the government lacks the means of protecting the persons who carry on its work. "We cannot doubt the power of the President to take measures for the protection of a judge . . . who, while in the discharge of the duties of his office, is threatened with a personal attack." The mails supply the parallel: if the President or the Postmaster General learns that the mails "are liable to be robbed," no one can doubt his authority "to make an order for the protection."
- **A federal peace, and a federal peace officer.** "That there is a peace of the United States; that a man assaulting a judge of the United States while in the discharge of his duties violates that peace." Rev. Stat. § 788 then supplies the officer: marshals have "the same powers in executing the laws of the United States as the sheriffs and their deputies in such state may have, by law, in executing the laws thereof," so the marshal stands to the peace of the United States as the sheriff stands to the peace of the State. This is the one statutory link in the chain, and it borrows state law to supply its content.
- **Justification on the merits, decided on the habeas record.** Neagle "did justifiably interfere by shooting Terry while in the act of assaulting Mr. Justice Field, whom he had already struck two or three times," and "it became necessary for the protection of Judge Field . . . to kill Terry." The Court resolves the necessity question itself rather than leaving it to a jury.
- **Disposition.** Neagle "was in custody for an act done in pursuance of a law of the United States, and was imprisoned in violation of the Constitution and laws of the United States"; the circuit court's discharge is affirmed.

### Dissent / concurrence

- **Lamar, J., dissenting, joined by Fuller, C.J.** "The chief justice and myself are unable to assent to the conclusion reached by the majority of the court." The dissent disclaims any view of the killing: "Our dissent is not based on any conviction as to the guilt or innocence of the appellee."
- **What the case is really about.** Lamar restates the question as whether Neagle "shall in this ex parte proceeding be discharged and delivered from any trial or further inquiry in any court, state or federal, for what he has been accused of in the forms prescribed by the constitution and laws of the state in which the act in question was committed." Framed that way the case is about who decides, and the majority's answer is that nobody tries the question at all.
- **The word "law."** "[A] wholly inadmissible construction is placed on the word 'law,' as used in that statute." Habeas itself is a creature of enacted law — "[t]he power to award the writ . . . by any of the courts of the United States must be given by written law" — so a statute that frees a prisoner held under "a law of the United States" cannot be satisfied by an inference from the Constitution's general structure.
- **Who answers for the killing.** "We maintain that for the exercise of that right or duty he is answerable to the courts of the state of California, and to them alone." A federal defense is a defense, not an exemption: "Whether his defense is sufficient or not is for the court which tries him to determine," and "[a]ll questions which may arise in the orderly course of the proceeding against him are to be determined by the court to whose jurisdiction he has been subjected."
- **The principle underneath.** "No officer or employe of the United States is placed by his position, or the services he is called to perform, above responsibility to the legal tribunals of the country." The dissent would have remanded Neagle "to the custody of the sheriff," leaving the federal question to reach the Supreme Court on writ of error after a trial.

### Cold-call notes

- **What was the statute, and what was the law?** The only statutes in the chain are Rev. Stat. § 753 (the habeas remedy) and § 788 (marshals have sheriffs' powers). Neither authorizes protecting a Justice. The "law" Neagle executed is the President's Article II duty, mediated by an Attorney General's letter and a marshal's appointment. Be ready to lay out the chain in that order, because the weak link is different at each stage: § 788 borrows state law, the Attorney General's letter is not law at all, and the constitutional duty is the thing in dispute.
- **Is Neagle a Youngstown category two case?** Jackson's second category is the "zone of twilight" where Congress has neither granted nor denied authority. That is the natural home for Neagle: Congress legislated about marshals and said nothing about bodyguards, and silence there looks like an invitation rather than a prohibition. The argument that it is really category one is that § 788 is a congressional grant, so the President acts with Congress's authority; the argument that it flirts with category three is that Congress had occupied the field of marshals' duties in detail and the omission was deliberate. Miller never reaches for congressional authorization at all, which is why the case reads as a claim of independent Article II power — closer to what Jackson calls the President acting alone.
- **Compare the shape of the claim with Youngstown's.** Truman claimed a power to seize an industry against three statutory schemes Congress had enacted for labor emergencies; the President in Neagle claimed a power to keep a federal court functioning where Congress had said nothing. Vary the facts: if Congress had passed a statute providing that marshals shall not accompany judges, Neagle becomes Jackson's category three and Black's opinion controls. If Congress had appropriated money for judicial security, it becomes category one and the case is easy. The case is hard exactly because the field is empty.
- **What would Black have done with Neagle?** Black's Youngstown opinion says the President's power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself" — which is the Neagle answer verbatim, since Miller grounds the power in the Constitution itself. The difference is that Black treats lawmaking as the thing the President may not do, and Miller does not think an order to protect a judge is lawmaking. Push on that: is the difference the subject matter (property versus personal protection), the scale, or the presence of a regulated private party?
- **Why the backdrop matters.** Field was a sitting Justice, riding circuit under a statute Congress had enacted, attacked over a ruling he had made. The threatened institution was the judiciary, and the protector was the executive; nobody in the case argued that the courts should protect themselves. Note who is absent: Field recused, but the Court he sat on decided that his own protection was constitutionally required. If Terry had attacked a postmaster, or a congressman, or a private litigant, the "peace of the United States" argument gets progressively weaker, and the mails hypothetical is Miller's way of showing it does not stop at judges.
- **Vary the officer and the act.** A deputy marshal who shoots a man threatening a federal courthouse: squarely within Neagle. A Secret Service agent who shoots an assassin: same logic, and the power predates the statute. A federal agent who conducts a warrantless search to protect a judge: Neagle supplies authority to act but says nothing about constitutional limits on the means, and an individual right would cut against the power where a State's criminal law does not. A federal officer who kills a man in a bar fight while off duty: no federal duty, so § 753 gives nothing and the State tries him.
- **The tension with the Take Care cases.** [[United States v. Cox]] holds that the decision to prosecute is executive and the courts cannot compel it; [[Adams v. Richardson]] holds that a general abdication of enforcement is reviewable. Both read "take Care" as describing a duty owed with respect to statutes Congress passed. Neagle reads the same clause as a source of authority to act where Congress has passed nothing. Ask whether one clause can do both jobs: if "the Laws" in Article II, § 3 includes the Constitution's implied obligations, then the executive's duty is defined partly by the executive's own view of what the Constitution obliges, and Cox's unreviewable discretion becomes considerably larger.
- **The tension with anti-commandeering.** [[Printz v. United States]] rests on the observation that Article VI binds "the Judges in every State" and says nothing about state executives, so state judges may be pressed into federal service and state executives may not. Neagle is the mirror image: a federal executive officer immunized from a State's judges. Put the two together and the Supremacy Clause distributes obligations asymmetrically — state judges must apply federal law, state courts may not try federal officers for doing federal duty, and state executives owe nothing at all.
- **[[In re Debs]] as the next step.** Debs (1895) sustained an injunction sought by the Attorney General against a strike obstructing interstate commerce and the mails, on an authority to protect federal instrumentalities that no statute conferred. Neagle is the personal-protection case and Debs the property-and-commerce case; together they mark the high-water line of implied executive authority before Youngstown pulled it back.
- **The oath clause and departmentalism.** Article VI binds "all executive and judicial Officers, both of the United States and of the several States" by oath "to support this Constitution." The oath runs to the Constitution, not to Congress and not to the Supreme Court, which is the textual basis for [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]: each officer swears to his own understanding of the instrument. Neagle is the executive-power payoff of that reading, because it lets the President execute the Constitution directly. [[Cooper v. Aaron]] is the counterweight, treating the oath as an obligation to obey the Court's interpretations. Be ready to say which way the clause cuts: it names officers who swear, and it does not say whose reading they are sworn to.
- **"In Pursuance thereof" and judicial review.** The Supremacy Clause makes supreme "the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof," while treaties are supreme when "made . . . under the Authority of the United States." The asymmetry is the strongest textual argument in [[Marbury v. Madison]]: a statute not made in pursuance of the Constitution is not supreme law, and some organ must say which is which. Note the awkwardness for Neagle: the phrase suggests that "Law of the United States" in the constitutional vocabulary means enacted law made under a procedure, which is closer to Lamar's reading of "law" than to Miller's.
- **Lamar's strongest point, and the answer.** Nobody claimed Neagle would be convicted. The question was whether he must be tried, and a federal defense is ordinarily raised at trial and reviewed on error. The answer is institutional and candid: a federal officer who must stand trial in a hostile state court for doing his duty is deterred from doing it, so the protection has to run to the process and not just to the judgment. That is the same argument that supports removal jurisdiction and, later, official immunity, and it is worth saying that Neagle purchases federal supremacy at the cost of any state adjudication at all.
- **Doctrine against original meaning.** The Take Care Clause is a duty imposed, phrased as a limitation on the executive ("he shall take Care"), and it appears among the President's obligations rather than among his powers. Reading it as a grant of protective power is a structural and consequentialist argument, not a textual one; Miller's own framing is a rhetorical question rather than an exegesis. The originalist defense is that executive power at common law included keeping the peace, which § 788 preserves by reference to sheriffs' powers. Baude's two questions come apart here: what the Court has said is Neagle, and what the Constitution requires may be Lamar.
- **Memorable lines.** "[A]ny obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law'"; "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself"; "[t]hat there is a peace of the United States"; and, from the dissent, "[n]o officer or employe of the United States is placed by his position . . . above responsibility to the legal tribunals of the country."
