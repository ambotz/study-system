## Snapshot

Dillon, held under the National Prohibition Act for transporting liquor, attacked the Eighteenth Amendment itself: the resolution proposing it declared the Amendment inoperative unless ratified within seven years, and he argued Congress had no power to attach such a condition. On habeas, the Court held that Article V implies ratification within a reasonable time, that fixing a definite period is incident to Congress's power to designate the mode of ratification, and that seven years was reasonable. Article V itself says nothing about time.

## Issue

- Does Article V permit Congress to fix a definite period within which a proposed amendment must be ratified, where the Article says nothing about time?
- If so, was the seven-year period attached to the Eighteenth Amendment reasonable?
- When was ratification consummated — on the thirty-sixth State's ratification, or on the Secretary of State's proclamation?

## Rule

Article V implies that ratification must come "within some reasonable time after the proposal," because what is reasonably implied in a written instrument is as much a part of it as what is expressed. Congress may fix that period in advance as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification, and seven years is reasonable.

- "[T]he fair inference or implication from Article V is that the ratification must be within some reasonable time after the proposal."
- "[W]ith the Constitution, as with a statute or other written instrument, what is reasonably implied is as much a part of it as what is expressed."
- Ratification must be "sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of States to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- Whether to fix a definite period "is . . . a matter of detail which Congress may determine as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification."

## Operative facts

- **Article V says nothing about the time for ratification — "neither that it shall be unlimited nor that it shall be fixed by Congress."** *Van Devanter concedes this at the outset, which forces the Court onto implication rather than text and gives the OLC its opening seventy years later.*
- **Four amendments proposed long before 1921 were still pending — two from 1789, one from 1810, one from 1861.** *The consequence that makes the contrary reading unattractive: ratifications "by representatives of generations now largely forgotten" could be completed by a later generation. One of the four is the Congressional Pay Amendment, ratified in 1992.*
- **Congress proposed the Eighteenth Amendment by resolution, 40 Stat. 1050, declaring the Amendment "inoperative unless ratified within seven years."** *The whole case. Without that limit Dillon has no argument that the Amendment is invalid, and the Court has no occasion to ask where a time limit could come from.*
- **The States ratified inside the seven years, the thirty-sixth ratifying on January 16, 1919, and the Secretary of State proclaimed the Amendment on January 29, 1919.** *The thirteen-day gap is what the Amendment's one-year delay is measured from, and it is what decides the case against Dillon.*
- **Congress then passed the National Prohibition Act, § 3 of Title II forbidding the transport of intoxicating liquor and § 26 providing for seizure and custody.** *The enforcement statute is the only reason an Article V question reaches a court at all.*
- **Dillon was arrested and held in custody on a charge of transporting intoxicating liquor.** *A criminal defendant attacking the constitutional amendment his statute enforces; the Court reaches Article V because a habeas petitioner raised it.*
- **He argued that the seven-year condition was beyond Congress's power and so invalidated the Amendment, and in the alternative that the one-year delay ran from the proclamation, so the Act had not yet taken effect when he was arrested.** *Two arguments, both about dates: the first is why the case is in the casebook, the second is why Dillon lost.*
- **The district court denied the petition for habeas corpus and he appealed.** *A custody posture rather than a suit about an amendment's validity, which is why no one had to establish standing.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (unanimous; Van Devanter, J., for the Court; no separate opinions)

- **Congress may fix a definite ratification period.** Whether to do so "is, in our opinion, a matter of detail which Congress may determine as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification."
- **Seven years was reasonable.** It "was reasonable, if power existed to fix a definite time," and that is confirmed by "the periods within which prior amendments were ratified."
- **Ratification was consummated on the thirty-sixth State's ratification,** January 16, 1919, not on the Secretary of State's proclamation, so the one-year delay ended January 16, 1920 and Dillon's conduct fell inside the thirteen-day gap. The order denying habeas corpus is affirmed.

**Van Devanter, J., for the Court**

- Start from silence. The Article says nothing either way — "neither that it shall be unlimited nor that it shall be fixed by Congress" — and neither the federal convention's debates nor the state ratifying conventions "shed any light on the question."
- Implication fills the gap, on the ordinary principle that "with the Constitution, as with a statute or other written instrument, what is reasonably implied is as much a part of it as what is expressed."
- **Single endeavor.** Proposal and ratification are "not treated as unrelated acts but as succeeding steps in a single endeavor."
- **Necessity implies dispatch.** Amendments are proposed only when "deemed to be a necessity," which implies they are "to be considered and disposed of presently."
- **Contemporaneous approbation.** Because ratification "is but the expression of the approbation of the people," it "must be sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of States to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- The consequences confirm it. On the contrary view the four long-dormant proposals could be revived by a later generation, a result to which "few would be able to subscribe."
- Judge Jameson's conclusion is adopted: an amendment not ratified "early while that sentiment may fairly be supposed to exist" should be "regarded as waived, and not again to be voted upon, unless a second time proposed by Congress."
- Fixing the period in advance avoids "speculation on what is a reasonable time" — a practical argument for letting Congress decide beforehand rather than leaving courts to decide after the fact.

## Arguments rejected

- *Article V states its requirements with precision — two-thirds of both Houses, three-fourths of the States, two modes of ratification — so a court may not add a condition the drafters left out; the silence is permission, not a gap.* The Court answers that what is reasonably implied in a written instrument is as much a part of it as what is expressed, and locates the implication in Article V's own structure rather than in policy.
- *A proposed amendment stays alive until three-fourths of the States act, whenever that happens; nothing in the Article makes a proposal lapse.* The Court calls this "quite untenable," pointing to the four proposals pending from 1789, 1810 and 1861 whose old ratifications, "by representatives of generations now largely forgotten," could be completed by a later generation.
- *(Implicitly) Even if ratification must be timely, timeliness is for a court to judge case by case, not for Congress to fix by resolution in advance.* The Court treats the choice as "a matter of detail" incident to the power to designate the mode, and prefers a fixed period because it avoids "speculation on what is a reasonable time."

## Where it sits

**The chapter's only Supreme Court case.** The Article V chapter excerpts Dillon to set up a fight, not to state settled law. [[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment|The 1992 OLC opinion]], printed immediately after, says Dillon's reasoning "is unpersuasive" and that the contemporaneity requirement "is not within the holding." The Twenty-seventh Amendment exists because the executive branch acted on that view.

**Coleman is the hinge.** In *Coleman v. Miller* (1939) Hughes, for three Justices, treated Dillon's three considerations as dictum and held that whether the Child Labor Amendment had "lost its vitality through lapse of time" was for Congress at promulgation. After Coleman, which has no controlling majority, the rule survives without a court to enforce it, which is why the [[The Equal Rights Amendment|ERA]] fight runs through Congress, OLC and the Archivist.

**The fact pattern Dillon called untenable.** [[The Congressional Pay Amendment]] is one of the 1789 proposals, ratified in 1992. *Hollingsworth v. Virginia* (1798), the other early Article V holding: an amendment need not be presented to the President.

**The seam inside the opinion.** A timing requirement derived from structure and purpose, the text conceded silent, the content handed to Congress. Coleman widened that gap and the OLC drove through it. Outside the assigned pages: the consummation holding and the Court's survey of prior ratification periods.

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

- **What exactly did Dillon hold?** That Congress may set a ratification deadline and that seven years is reasonable. The contemporaneity language is reasoning, not a holding that any particular ratification came too late. Getting this right is what lets you follow the OLC's attack on the case.
- **Name the three grounds.** Single endeavor; proposal only on necessity, implying prompt disposition; ratification as a contemporaneous expression of the people's approbation. The OLC answers each: the first assumes its conclusion, the second confuses necessity with emergency, the third assumes consensus must be contemporaneous.
- **Where does the Court get authority to add a term to Article V?** From implication in a written instrument. Press the counter: Article V states its numbers precisely and expressly fixed one deadline — no amendment before 1808 touching the slave-trade and capitation clauses — so the drafters knew how to write a time rule when they wanted one.
- **Is the silence a gap or a choice?** Dillon treats it as a gap to be filled by implication; the OLC treats it as a deliberate choice. Everything else follows from that one step, so be ready to defend it before any of the three grounds matters.
- **Why did Dillon himself lose?** Ratification was consummated when the thirty-sixth State ratified on January 16, 1919, not on the proclamation thirteen days later, so the one-year delay ended January 16, 1920 and his conduct fell inside the gap. He lost on the calendar.
- **Would the Court apply Dillon to the Twenty-seventh Amendment?** Dillon's dictum says no ratification over 203 years; Coleman says it is for Congress; the OLC says the premise is wrong. The honest answer is that the question has never been litigated, because Congress has not tried to violate the Amendment.
- **Who enforces the rule after Coleman?** No one reliably. Hughes gave the question to Congress at promulgation, the OLC says the contemporaneity premise is unsound, and the Archivist acts on the executive's view. Ask whether [[The Equal Rights Amendment|the ERA]] is the same question in reverse.
- **Vary the facts.** A deadline in the proposing resolution rather than the amendment's text (the Eighteenth and the Twentieth through Twenty-second put the seven years in the text; the ERA's was in the resolution). A deadline extended before it expires ([[The Equal Rights Amendment|the ERA]] again). An amendment with no deadline ratified over forty years rather than two centuries — is there a line, and who draws it?
- **Memorable lines.** "[S]ucceeding steps in a single endeavor." "[S]ufficiently contemporaneous . . . to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period." Jameson's "regarded as waived."
