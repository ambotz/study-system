## What it is

Attorney General Edward Bates's formal opinion to Lincoln, "Opinion on the Suspension of the Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus," 10 Op. Att'y Gen. 74, dated July 5, 1861, the day after Lincoln's message to Congress promised that "an opinion, at some length, will probably be presented by the Attorney General." It answers two questions: whether the President, during "a great and dangerous insurrection," may arrest and hold persons suspected of complicity with the insurgents; and whether he may refuse "to obey a writ of habeas corpus issued by a court or a judge."

It is the administration's legal answer to [[Ex parte Merryman]], and the most thorough executive-branch statement of departmentalism in the casebook. The Notes that follow (pp. 413–416) close the unit with the merits of presidential suspension and the debate between casebook co-authors Paulsen and Baude over whether the President must obey specific judgments.

## What to notice

- **The co-ordinacy premise.** The departments "are co-ordinate and coequal—that is, neither being sovereign, each is independent in its sphere." "[I]f we allow one of the three to determine the extent of its own powers, and also the extent of the powers of the other two, that one can control the whole government, and has in fact achieved the sovereignty." The framers "did not even try ... to create an arbiter among them."
- **The judgment/opinion distinction, stated precisely.** "[I]t is quite possible for the same identical question (not case) to come up legitimately before each one of the three departments, and be determined in three different ways, and each decision stand irrevocable, binding upon the parties to each case." The judgment of one department "is not binding upon the other two, as to the arguments and principles involved in the judgment. It binds only the parties to the case decided." This is Lincoln's 1858 position (bound as to the parties, not as a "political rule") in the form of a theory.
- **The reciprocity argument.** If the President is bound by the principles laid down by the judiciary, "so also is the judiciary bound by the principles laid down by the President." "Departments co-ordinate and coequal, and yet reciprocally subordinate to each other! That cannot be." The argument answers [[Cooper v. Aaron]] in advance: Cooper's premise requires a hierarchy the Constitution does not create among federal departments.
- **The judiciary does not bind itself.** The judiciary "can and often does overrule and disregard" its own decisions, "as, in common honesty, it ought to do." If precedent does not bind the Court, it is hard to say it binds the President.
- **The President is a civil magistrate, not a soldier.** Using the army to execute the laws does not make the President "military," any more than a court becomes belligerent "by calling out the power of the country to enforce its decrees." "To call, as is sometimes done, the judiciary the civil power, and the President the military power, seems to me at once a mistake of fact and an abuse of language." This answers Taney's framing of the case as military usurpation of civil authority.
- **Passive judiciary, active executive.** The judiciary "is, for the most part, passive"; "Its great function is judgment." The President "is active"; he alone is required "to 'take care that the laws (all the laws) be faithfully executed.'" Together with the oath to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution," this makes him "in a peculiar manner, and above all other officers, the guardian of the Constitution."
- **Answer to question 1: the arrest power.** In a time "when the very existence of the nation is assailed," the President "has the lawful discretionary power to arrest and hold in custody persons known to have criminal intercourse with the insurgents," or against whom there is probable cause. Bates grounds this in the oath, the Take Care Clause, and the militia and insurrection acts "of 1795 and 1807."
- **Answer to question 2: the writ need not be obeyed.** Bates cannot understand "how it can be legally possible for a judge to issue a command to the President to come before him ad subjiciendum—that is, to submit implicitly to his judgment—and, in case of disobedience, treat him as a criminal." "[T]he whole subject-matter is political and not judicial," and "no court or judge can take cognizance of the political acts of the President." "I deny that he is under any obligation to obey such a writ, issued under such circumstances."
- **Habeas as an appeal from the President.** Habeas "is in the nature of an appeal," and "it will hardly be seriously affirmed, that a judge, at chambers, can entertain an appeal, in any form, from a decision of the President." The argument is jurisdictional: it targets a single judge at chambers, and a political subject. This is why Baude reads Bates as consistent with judgment supremacy.
- **Luther v. Borden and political questions.** Bates relies on [[Luther v. Borden]] (1849) for judicial deference to the President's determination of insurrection: "If ... the judicial power extends so far, the guarantee contained in the Constitution of the United States ... is a guarantee of anarchy and not of order."
- **Impeachment as the only remedy.** The power is "a sacred trust," and "for any breach of that trust he is responsible before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal." Bates offers accountability through Congress, not the courts.
- **What Bates does not argue.** The excerpt barely addresses the Article I location of the Suspension Clause; the editors bracket the claim that suspension "is no part of his ordinary duty in time of peace; it is temporary and exceptional, and was intended only to meet a pressing emergency, when the judiciary is found to be too weak to insure the public safety." Bates's weight is on who decides, not on the Clause's text.
- **Note 1: who may suspend?** Taney (as the casebook summarizes): the Clause's location in Article I, § 9 signals a limit on Congress's power, so suspension is legislative; and the writ historically checked the King, so executive suspension "would therefore be absurd." Lincoln: the Clause does not say who suspends; it is for emergencies; suspension was necessary to executing the laws; and only the President can act when Congress is out of session. The Article I placement is the stronger textual point; the passive voice and the emergency design leave Lincoln's temporary-suspension claim fairly debatable, and Jackson's reliance on the Clause's placement in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] cuts for Taney.
- **Note 2: does the merits' debatability matter?** A judgment-supremacy theory says no: the President obeys a judgment within jurisdiction whether it is right, debatable, or "flagrantly and horribly wrong." The extreme hypotheticals (an order to stop fighting the war, to surrender Maryland, to undo [[The Emancipation Proclamation]], to release "enemy soldiers and saboteurs") test whether anyone can hold that position at the limit.
- **Notes 4 and 6: the stakes on each side.** If the President may decline to execute judgments, "every judicial decision" risks becoming "a mere advisory opinion, for the president to decide at leisure whether to obey." If he may not, Cooper and Merryman imply "that the president and Congress can be forced to take actions that they think are violations of the Constitution," which is hard to square with the Oath and Take Care Clauses.
- **Note 5: Hamilton's "merely judgment."** In [[The Federalist No. 78]] the judiciary "must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments." Paulsen reads that dependency as a deliberate check; Bates, by contrast, points to the courts' own power to call out "the whole power of the county to enforce their judgments," which suggests judicial execution is not wholly at the President's mercy.
- **Note 7: jurisdiction as the intermediate position.** If Taney lacked jurisdiction, non-compliance can be justified without claiming a general power to ignore judgments. The question is whether "jurisdiction" is a principled line or "just one more place for a fight about the merits."
- **Paulsen: "no defensible middle ground."** In his 1993 Merryman Power article Paulsen argued there is "no defensible middle ground" between complete judicial supremacy and complete executive coordinacy, including the "Merryman Power" of non-execution of judicial decrees. If presidential interpretive autonomy exists for pardons and vetoes (Jefferson's Sedition Act pardons), nothing distinguishes law-execution. In 1994 he adopted the executive-autonomy horn, supported, he argues, by the reasoning of [[Marbury v. Madison|Marbury]].
- **Baude: judgment supremacy.** In The Judgment Power (2008), Baude argues the original understanding "distinguished the specific judgments of courts over the parties from broader notions of judicial supremacy," so the executive "must obey judicial judgments, as long as the judiciary properly possessed jurisdiction," even if "largely free to ignore the judiciary's views in most contexts." He reads Hamilton to support it: "the President would fight against judicial oppression by ignoring judgments when the Judiciary lacked power to issue them, that is, lacked jurisdiction." Baude "argues that this is the best explanation of Bates's opinion in Merryman."
- **Paulsen's reply: it "gives away the game."** "Who decides whether a particular judgment falls within the scope of judicial 'jurisdiction'? The courts? If so, we are back to pure, unadulterated judicial supremacy. ... The executive? If so, we are back to the power of executive review of judgments." And a court could write into its "judgment" "that the executive must enforce the judiciary's understanding of the law generally." Conclusion: "The 'judgment supremacy' position, with the slightest push, slides straightaway into complete judicial supremacy."
- **The editors' questions to Paulsen.** Is the slope "really inevitable"? Does it assume that the executive "will dishonestly recharacterize their views in terms of 'jurisdiction'," or that "'jurisdiction' is an empty concept"? And: "isn't all of constitutional law about relocating problems?" The best defense of Baude's position is that jurisdiction is a real and familiar legal category, policed in ordinary litigation, and that a line need not be self-enforcing to be principled.
- **How the three positions map.** Judicial supremacy: Douglas, [[Cooper v. Aaron|Cooper]]. Departmentalism as to opinions, with obedience to judgments: Lincoln in 1858; Baude. Departmentalism reaching judgments themselves: Lincoln's administration in 1861 and Paulsen's "Merryman Power." Bates straddles the last two: "It binds only the parties to the case decided," but he denies the writ's force on jurisdictional and political-question grounds.
