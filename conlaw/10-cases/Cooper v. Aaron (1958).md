## Snapshot

Little Rock's School Board, facing violence stirred up by the Governor and Legislature, asked to suspend its court-approved desegregation plan for two and a half years; the District Court agreed, the Court of Appeals reversed. Affirming, the Court accepted the Board's good faith and the findings of harm but held the State had caused the conditions. In dicta signed by all nine Justices, it read [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] as making the federal judiciary "supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution." Article VI is the provision in play.

## Issue

- May a federal court postpone a school board's court-approved desegregation plan because of violence and disorder at the school, when that disorder is traceable to the official actions of the State's own Governor and Legislature?
- Are state legislators and executive officials bound by this Court's interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment in *Brown*, or may they act on the premise that they are not bound by it?

## Rule

State officials may not delay compliance with a desegregation decree because of violence and disorder that other state officials brought about; a State acts through all its officers, and the Board's good faith is no excuse for conditions the State created. The Court's interpretation of the Constitution is itself supreme law, binding every state officer through the Article VI oath.

- "A State acts by its legislative, its executive, or its judicial authorities. It can act in no other way" (*Ex parte Virginia*), so no "agency of the State, or of the officers or agents by whom its powers are exerted," may deny equal protection.
- Marbury "declared the basic principle that the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution," so the interpretation in *Brown* "is the supreme law of the land."
- "No state legislator or executive or judicial officer can war against the Constitution without violating his undertaking to support it."

## Operative facts

- **After *Brown* (1954), the Little Rock School Board adopted a desegregation plan that the federal courts approved.** *The Board is in court as a party under a decree, so every obligation in the case traces to a judgment rather than an opinion.*
- **On September 4, 1957, Arkansas National Guard units, "acting pursuant to the Governor's order, stood shoulder to shoulder at the school grounds" and forcibly kept the nine students out, as they did every school day for three weeks.** *State force against a federal decree — nullification in fact, whatever the Governor's theory.*
- **The United States obtained a federal injunction forbidding Arkansas officials to resist the desegregation order.** *The officials were thereafter under their own judgment, so the case never required a rule about non-parties.*
- **On September 23 the students entered under police protection but were removed that morning because the officers could not control "a large and demonstrating crowd."** *Disorder, not law, excluded them, and disorder is the ground the Board later relied on.*
- **On September 25 the President dispatched federal troops; regular army held the school until November 27, then federalized Guardsmen for the rest of the year, and eight of the nine students stayed through it.** *The decree was executed by the executive — Note 3's question is what the supremacy claim would be worth without that.*
- **On February 20, 1958, the Board and Superintendent petitioned to withdraw the students to segregated schools and postpone desegregation two and a half years, blaming hostility "engendered largely by the official attitudes and actions of the Governor and the Legislature."** *The petition states the causal fact that defeats it, as the Board's certiorari petition also does.*
- **The District Court granted the postponement, finding "chaos, bedlam and turmoil," repeated violence against the students, a "serious financial burden," harm to education that "will continue to suffer," and a continuing need for "military assistance or its equivalent."** *Accepted in full, so the holding cannot rest on doubting the harm — only on who caused it.*
- **The Governor and Legislature acted throughout "upon the premise that they are not bound by our holding in Brown."** *That premise, not the postponement, provoked the supremacy passage — which the Court wrote after saying the case was already disposed of.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (opinion of the Court jointly signed by all nine Justices; Frankfurter concurring separately, omitted from the casebook; judgment of the Court of Appeals affirmed)

- **The postponement was properly denied.** "The constitutional rights of respondents are not to be sacrificed or yielded to the violence and disorder which have followed upon the actions of the Governor and Legislature."
- **The Board acts for the State.** Local officials "stand in this litigation as the agents of the State," so good faith cannot shelter it from what other state officials did.
- **State officials are bound by *Brown*.** Article VI, Marbury and the oath make the Court's interpretation binding on every state officer.

**The Court, per all nine Justices**

- Good faith and the findings are accepted "without reservation," but their significance changes once the conditions are seen as "directly traceable to the actions of legislators and executive officials of the State of Arkansas."
- Had the school been "under the direct management of the State itself," those in charge could not plead their own good faith to excuse delay caused by other state officials; local status puts the Board "in no different posture."
- The Fourteenth Amendment's command runs to the "State," which can "act in no other way" than through its officers (*Ex parte Virginia*), and rights cannot be nullified "ingeniously or ingenuously" (*Smith v. Texas*).
- "What has been said, in the light of the facts developed, is enough to dispose of the case. However, we should answer the premise" — then: Article VI; Marbury's "province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is"; and the claim that judicial supremacy in exposition "has ever since been respected by this Court and the Country as a permanent and indispensable feature of our constitutional system."
- The oath binds officers against warring on the Constitution, and precedent condemns state resistance: *United States v. Peters* (if legislatures "may, at will, annul the judgments of the courts of the United States . . . the constitution itself becomes a solemn mockery"); *Sterling v. Constantin* (otherwise "the fiat of a state Governor, and not the Constitution of the United States, would be the supreme law of the land"); *Abelman v. Booth* (the oath guards "against resistance to or evasion of its authority, on the part of a State").
- *Brown*'s authority is reinforced by process: unanimous, twice argued, now "unanimously reaffirmed" by a Court including three new Justices.

## Arguments rejected

- *The Board acted in entire good faith, the educational harm is found and real, and equity may give a public body time to restore order before enforcing a right.* Every premise is accepted and the conclusion refused: "regardless of the Board's good faith, the actions of the other state agencies responsible for those conditions compel us to reject the Board's legal position."
- *The School Board is a local body distinct from the Governor and Legislature and should not bear the consequences of their conduct.* "[F]rom the point of view of the Fourteenth Amendment, they stand in this litigation as the agents of the State."
- *Brown binds only its parties; Arkansas officials may act on their own reading of the Fourteenth Amendment and test the principle in fresh litigation.* This is Lincoln's position on [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]]. The Court answers with Article VI, Marbury and the oath: the interpretation itself binds, and a Governor claiming power to nullify a federal order would make his fiat the supreme law.

## Where it sits

**The first clear claim of supremacy.** Cooper is, in the casebook's words, the Court's "first clear-cut claim to judicial supremacy in constitutional interpretation." It adopts Douglas's side of [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]], a century later and for the Fourteenth Amendment rather than slavery.

**What Marbury actually held.** [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] held that a court deciding a case applies the Constitution over a conflicting statute. Cooper converts that into a claim that the Court's exposition binds everyone. Its other authorities speak of judgments — *Peters* of annulling "the judgments of the courts of the United States," *Sterling* of nullifying "a federal court order" — and those sufficed, since the Board was party to a decree.

**Supremacy is neutral about whose rights.** Cooper cites Taney's unanimous opinion in *Abelman v. Booth* (1859), which rejected Wisconsin's attempt to free a prisoner held under the Fugitive Slave Act. The two great precedents against state resistance come from a fugitive-slave case and a desegregation case.

**State defiance versus coordinate branches.** Cooper is a federal-state case; whether its principle binds the other federal branches is the question of [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] and [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]].

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Is Douglas's view the same as the Court's in Cooper? Is Lincoln's view that of Governor Faubus? Is it possible to have a consistent position on the Court's authority . . . through good decisions and bad ones?"**

- Lincoln's is not Faubus's. Lincoln disclaimed resisting the judgment; Faubus used troops to block a federal decree, so a departmentalist who obeys judgments condemns Faubus without adopting Cooper's dictum.
- The consistency test is the point: Cooper's rule entrenches [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] as surely as *Brown*, while Lincoln's criteria — unanimous, reaffirmed, settled — fit *Brown* and not a 5–4 decision.

**Note 2. "Cooper cites some vigorous assertions of judicial authority from . . . Marshall and . . . Hughes. Do their statements go as far as Cooper? Does it matter that Cooper claims authority for 'our holding,' while Marshall is saying that state legislatures may not annul 'the judgments' of federal courts?"**

- No, and yes. *Peters* and *Sterling* support judgment supremacy; Cooper asserts opinion supremacy, and that line is the whole difference.
- Cooper did not need the broader claim: the officials were resisting a decree in a case the Board was party to, and the United States held an injunction against them.

**Note 3. "President Eisenhower sent federal troops to Little Rock . . . . What if he hadn't? Would the Supreme Court have had any power to enforce its rulings on its own? . . ."**

- No power of its own: the judiciary has "merely judgment" and must "depend upon the aid of the executive arm" ([[The Federalist No. 78]]).
- The claim was therefore made from a position of executive support; what happens when the executive declines is [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]].

**Note 4. "In 1956, two years before Cooper, 101 Southern members of Congress published 'The Southern Manifesto' . . . . But . . . Justin Driver argues that debate over the Manifesto also revealed 'widespread adherence to notions of judicial supremacy' well before . . . Cooper. . . . [D]oes that make it less of a power grab?"**

- If Driver is right, Cooper codifies a settled practice rather than inventing one — an argument from practice rather than text.
- The counterpoint is in the case: Arkansas officials expressly denied being bound, so the premise was live.

### Further drilling

- **Was the passage necessary?** No, on the Court's own account, and it is the only part anyone remembers. Know the sentence that concedes it.
- **Hypo: Arkansas enacts segregation in a district party to no suit.** On judgment supremacy, no decree binds those officials and they must be sued; on Cooper, the statute is void and enforcing it violates the oath.
- **Why state defiance differs.** Article VI ranks federal law above state law; the three federal branches are not ranked, which is Bates's co-ordinacy argument. Cooper can be right about Arkansas without settling Merryman.
- **Types of argument.** Text (Article VI, the oath), precedent (Marbury, *Peters*, *Sterling*, *Abelman*), structure (a State acts through all its officers), and practice. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
