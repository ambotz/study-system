## Rule

Congress may use a tax-and-credit scheme to induce states to enact laws it could not require them to enact, because a conditional tax rebate is a temptation, not coercion, unless "pressure turns into compulsion."

## Facts

- Title IX of the Social Security Act of 1935 imposed a payroll tax on employers.
- Employers received a credit of up to 90% of the federal tax for contributions to a state unemployment fund certified by the federal government as complying with prescribed federal standards.
- The tax-and-credit scheme was designed to induce states to adopt unemployment compensation laws that the national government "clearly could not directly require states to enact." *The Court held that inducement was not coercion.*
- The condition, a state unemployment compensation law, was related in subject matter to the national program. *The Court reserved the case of a condition requiring "a statute unrelated in subject matter to activities fairly within the scope of national policy and power."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (5–4)

- The tax-and-credit scheme is within the taxing power, though "plainly intended to achieve social policy goals."
- "The statute does not call for a surrender by the states of powers essential to their quasi-sovereign existence."
- The Child Labor Tax Case ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]) presented different issues and does not control.
- A financial inducement could in some circumstances pass the point at which "pressure turns into compulsion," but the Court did not "fix the outermost line."
- A tax is not necessarily valid "if it is laid upon the condition that a state may escape its operation through the adoption of a statute unrelated in subject matter to activities fairly within the scope of national policy and power."

**Rejected**

- *The tax-and-credit scheme coerces the states: a state whose employers have already been taxed has no genuine choice about taking the money back on Congress's terms.* The Court answered: "[E]very rebate from a tax when conditioned upon conduct is in some measure a temptation. But to hold that motive or temptation is equivalent to coercion is to plunge the law in endless difficulties." The law instead "assumes the freedom of the will as a working hypothesis."

## Context

Steward Machine and [[Helvering v. Davis]] were decided in 1937, the year of [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]] and *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish*, the "Revolution of 1937." In [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]], Steward Machine is the tack back toward broad taxing power after [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]]. The architecture matters: the federal statute prescribes no conduct for employers, the tax falls on everyone, and the credit does the work. If the same device would have saved the child labor tax, the tax/penalty line is a drafting rule. See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].

The case's lasting contribution is the coercion caveat in [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]]. [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|South Dakota v. Dole]] quoted Steward Machine and found the loss of 5% of highway funds "relatively mild encouragement"; O'Connor, dissenting in Dole, relied on the reservation about unrelated conditions as the root of the germaneness requirement. [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] was the first holding that a condition crossed the line: Roberts called the Medicaid expansion "a gun to the head" and tied coercion to the anti-commandeering rule of [[New York v. United States]] and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]. Ginsburg, dissenting on that point, noted that before NFIB the Court "has never ruled that the terms of any grant crossed the indistinct line between temptation and coercion." See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
