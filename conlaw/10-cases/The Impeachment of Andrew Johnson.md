## What it is

The casebook's introduction to Article II, § 4 (Impeachment), followed by an excerpt from David Currie, *The Reconstruction Congress*, 75 U. Chi. L. Rev. 383, 437–452 (2008), narrating the House's two attempts to impeach Andrew Johnson (1867 and 1868) and his acquittal in the Senate. The introduction frames impeachment as the constitutional substitute for the English methods of removing a king (killing him, forcing abdication, exile), and quotes Franklin at the Convention: without impeachment, "recourse was had to assassination in wch. he was not only deprived of his life but of the opportunity of vindicating his character."

Assigned as the first of the Class 10 readings on checking the President. Impeachment is the political remedy; [[United States v. Nixon]] and [[Trump v. United States]] are the judicial ones. The Johnson story is also a removal case: the article that nearly convicted him charged the firing of a Cabinet secretary, which is the [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]] fought out again eighty years later.

## What to notice

- **The casebook's framing questions.** Can impeachment "preserve the balance of power between the branches, by disciplining and constraining the president," or can it be used to allow "Congress to dominate the president"? "Can we have the sweet without the bitter?" Everything in the Johnson story is an answer to that question.
- **Three presidents, four impeachments, no convictions.** Johnson, Clinton, and Trump (twice) were impeached and all were acquitted; Nixon resigned before the House voted. Impeachment has never removed a President, so its constitutional meaning has been made almost entirely by acquittals and by the threat.
- **Why impeachment at all.** Congress disliked Johnson's Reconstruction policy, but "the United States did not have a parliamentary system in which a president could be deposed by a simple majority vote of no confidence." The two-thirds requirement in the Senate is the structural line between impeachment and a no-confidence vote.
- **The first attempt (1867) charged policy as crime.** Ashley's resolution charged that Johnson "corruptly used" the appointing, pardoning and veto powers. The Judiciary Committee majority (5–4) called the crux "usurpation of power, which involves, of course, a violation of law": Johnson had tried to reconstruct the South on his own. The House rejected the recommendation.
- **The indictable-offense argument (the committee dissent).** "[C]rimes" and "[m]isdemeanors" are "terms of art." Three structural points support it: the Impeachment Judgment Clause leaves the convicted party liable to indictment ("[h]ow can this be if his offence be not an indictable crime?"); the Pardon Clause excepts "cases of impeachment"; and Article III's jury-trial clause carries the same exception. Blackstone: impeachment is "a prosecution of the already known and established law."
- **The political-offense argument (the committee majority).** Hamilton in Federalist No. 65: impeachable offenses are those "which proceed from the misconduct of public men, or in other words from the abuse or violation of some public trust." Story: impeachment reaches "political offences, growing out of personal misconduct, or gross neglect, or usurpation, or habitual disregard of the public interests."
- **Madison and "maladministration."** Van Trump reminded the House that the Convention, at Madison's urging, struck "maladministration" as a ground because "[s]o vague a term . . . will be equivalent to a tenure during pleasure of the Senate." This is the best originalist evidence that "high Crimes and Misdemeanors" is narrower than bad government, though it does not show that it requires a statutory crime.
- **Currie's own view.** No indictable crime is required. Text does not settle it; British precedent is "in disarray"; Hamilton and the early commentators point the other way; early House practice never charged statutory violations; and a narrow reading would leave out conduct such as "a president's deliberate usurpation of congressional power." That is a text, history, practice and consequences argument in one paragraph ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).
- **The second attempt (1868) was a removal case.** Johnson fired Secretary of War Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act, which required Senate consent to remove a Cabinet member and made violation a misdemeanor. That gave the House a statutory crime, and it impeached 126–47. Most of the eleven articles rested on the firing.
- **The other articles.** Article IX charged Johnson with telling General Emory that the appropriations rider requiring orders to go through the general of the army was unconstitutional (a Commander in Chief and power-of-the-purse question). Article X charged "intemperate, inflammatory, and scandalous harangues" against Congress, a pure speech count to which Johnson answered with the freedom of speech.
- **Butler for the House.** Removal follows appointment and so requires the Senate's consent; the Vesting Clause does not carry all powers "that could be classified as executive"; even if the President has a removal power, Congress may regulate it under the Necessary and Proper Clause. And the President's authority to judge the Act's validity was "exhausted" when Congress overrode his veto.
- **Curtis for the defense.** The Act did not cover Stanton, whom Lincoln appointed; Congress "in 1789 had recognized the president's constitutional right to remove the secretary of war"; and if the President thinks a law unconstitutional it is his duty to disobey it "in order to provoke a judicial test of its validity."
- **The Senate vote.** Acquittal on Article XI and then on Articles II and III, each 35–19, one vote short of two-thirds. At least seven Republicans voted not guilty, including Trumbull and Fessenden, mainly on statutory grounds (the Act did not reach Stanton) and on the principle that a President should not be convicted "for mere misconstruction of a doubtful law." Reverdy Johnson went further and found the Act unconstitutional.
- **Trumbull's warning.** "Once set the example of impeaching a President for what, when the excitement of the hour has subsided, will be regarded as insufficient causes and no future President will be safe who happens to differ with a majority of the House and two thirds of the Senate . . . ." Fessenden: impeachment is "a power to be exercised with extreme caution [if at all] when you once get beyond the line of specific criminal offenses."
- **Currie's lesson.** On the meaning of "high Crimes and Misdemeanors" the precedent is "equivocal": the House rejected the "bundle of generalities" but later approved a count based only on speeches. The "real lesson" is the result: "no one should be impeached because he disagrees with the congressional will."
- **Note 1: which powers, and should disagreement be enough?** The charges covered the removal, appointment, pardon, veto, war and "directing" powers. The veto and pardon are expressly presidential and can hardly be abused by being used (Ashley charged exactly that). Removal was genuinely contested in 1868 and is the one on which Johnson was later vindicated. A strong answer: good-faith disagreement about the scope of a power is not impeachable, however high the stakes, but using a power one knows one lacks, or using a real power for a corrupt end (bribery is the paradigm), may be.
- **Note 2: if not impeachment, what?** Congress can override vetoes (it overrode Johnson repeatedly), write the Army rider and other conditions into appropriations ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]], [[United States v. Lovett]]), refuse confirmations, investigate, pass statutes that put the President at Jackson's "lowest ebb" ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]), leave the dispute to the courts, and wait for the election. Johnson was not renominated. The Tenure of Office Act dispute was settled by the Court in [[Myers v. United States]] (1926), which said the Act had been invalid.
