## Snapshot

A grand jury investigating Watergate indicted seven of the President's former aides and named the President an unindicted coconspirator. The Special Prosecutor subpoenaed tapes of the President's conversations for their trial, the President moved to quash on executive privilege and lost, and the case came up on certiorari before judgment. The Court held the privilege constitutionally based but qualified: a generalized claim of confidentiality yields to a specific need for criminal evidence. Article II's executive power meets Article III's duty to do justice.

## Issue

- Is the order reviewable, and is a dispute between the President and a Special Prosecutor serving under an Executive regulation justiciable?
- Did the subpoena satisfy Rule 17(c)'s relevancy, admissibility and specificity?
- Is the privilege absolute, so that the President alone decides whether his communications must be produced?
- If qualified, does it yield to a subpoena for evidence in a pending criminal trial?

## Rule

The President has a constitutionally based privilege for confidential communications, and those communications are presumptively privileged, but the privilege is qualified rather than absolute. The courts, not the President, decide its scope, because the judicial power cannot be shared with the Executive. A claim resting on military, diplomatic or national-security secrets draws far greater deference, and an Executive that has delegated authority by regulation is bound by that regulation while it stands.

- Presidential communications are "presumptively privileged," and "[t]he generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."
- "Neither the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances."
- The Executive is bound by its own delegation "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force."

## Operative facts

- **After the 1972 Watergate break-in, the Attorney General appointed a Special Prosecutor under a Justice Department regulation that delegated authority over Watergate offenses and limited his removal.** *The delegation makes this a case rather than an internal argument; a dispute with an ordinary at-will subordinate would have been the President's alone to resolve.*
- **The regulation gave the Special Prosecutor "explicit power to contest the invocation of executive privilege."** *Decisive on justiciability: "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force the Executive Branch is bound by it."*
- **In March 1974 a grand jury indicted seven men, including former senior White House and campaign officials, for conspiracy to defraud the United States and to obstruct justice, and named the President an unindicted coconspirator.** *It tied the President's own conversations to the charged conspiracy, which bears on relevance under Rule 17(c).*
- **The Special Prosecutor obtained a Rule 17(c) subpoena for tapes and documents of specified conversations between the President and his aides.** *The evidence was sought for someone else's criminal trial, which is why the holding is confined to criminal prosecutions.*
- **The President's counsel moved to quash, asserting executive privilege and arguing that the dispute was an internal Executive Branch matter.** *Two theories, both about who decides rather than about what the tapes contained.*
- **The claim of privilege rested on a generalized interest in confidentiality and nothing more.** *Decisive: there was no claim "to protect military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," which the Court says would have drawn much greater deference. Change this fact and the balance changes with it.*
- **On May 20, 1974, the District Court (Sirica, J.) denied the motion to quash and ordered the materials delivered for in camera examination.** *The privilege is protected by a judge's private review rather than defeated by public disclosure, which keeps the intrusion small enough to justify.*
- **On May 24 the Special Prosecutor petitioned for certiorari before judgment, the President cross-petitioned, and the Court heard argument on July 8 and decided on July 24, 1974.** *The Court took the case before the Court of Appeals ruled, on a compressed schedule running alongside the House Judiciary Committee's impeachment inquiry.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (unanimous, 8–0; Burger, C.J., for the Court; Rehnquist, J., who had served in the Nixon Justice Department, took no part)

- **Reviewable and justiciable.** The order was appealable and properly before the Court. "The mere assertion of a claim of an 'intra-branch dispute,' without more, has never operated to defeat federal jurisdiction," and the setting supplies "that concrete adverseness which sharpens the presentation of issues."
- **Rule 17(c) satisfied.** The Special Prosecutor cleared the three "hurdles": "(1) relevancy; (2) admissibility; (3) specificity."
- **No absolute privilege, and the courts decide its scope.** Neither separation of powers nor the need for confidentiality sustains an unqualified immunity from judicial process.
- **The privilege yields here.** A generalized interest in confidentiality gives way to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial, subject to in camera review. Affirmed.

**Burger, C.J., for the Court**

- **Justiciability.** The Attorney General may appoint subordinates by statute and delegated authority by regulation; because the regulation lets the Special Prosecutor contest privilege, a court can resolve the dispute.
- **Who decides.** [[Marbury v. Madison|Marbury]] settles it: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." The Article III power cannot be shared with the Executive.
- **Source of privilege.** The text says nothing of confidentiality, but the privilege follows from the nature of enumerated powers. "The President's need for complete candor and objectivity from advisers calls for great deference from the courts."
- **The balance.** "[W]e must weigh the importance of the general privilege of confidentiality of Presidential communications in performance of the President's responsibilities against the inroads of such a privilege on the fair administration of criminal justice."
- **Criminal justice on the other side.** "The very integrity of the judicial system and public confidence in the system depend on full disclosure of all the facts, within the framework of the rules of evidence." The Fifth and Sixth Amendments guarantee due process and compulsory process, which a blanket privilege would defeat.
- **Minimal harm.** "The President's broad interest in confidentiality of communications will not be vitiated by disclosure of a limited number of conversations preliminarily shown to have some bearing on the pending criminal cases."
- **Limits.** Three balances are reserved: confidentiality against "the need for relevant evidence in civil litigation," confidentiality against "congressional demands for information," and the President's "interest in preserving state secrets."
- **Procedure.** In camera inspection "is always a procedure calling for scrupulous protection against any release or publication" of material the court finds irrelevant or inadmissible.

## Arguments rejected

- *The separation of powers makes each branch the final judge of its own powers, so the President alone decides whether his communications are privileged.* Burger: the judicial power "can no more be shared with the Executive Branch" than the President can share the veto with the courts.
- *This is an internal Executive Branch dispute between the President and his own subordinate, which the President alone may resolve.* Burger: the Attorney General delegated authority by regulation, and "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force the Executive Branch is bound by it."
- *Confidentiality is essential to candid advice, because "those who expect public dissemination of their remarks may well temper candor."* Burger accepts the premise and calls the interest "weighty indeed and entitled to great respect," but "we cannot conclude that advisers will be moved to temper the candor of their remarks by the infrequent occasions of disclosure."
- *Even if the privilege is qualified, the Executive's independence "within its own sphere" insulates the President from a judicial subpoena.* Burger: an absolute privilege would impede "the primary constitutional duty of the Judicial Branch to do justice in criminal prosecutions."

## Where it sits

**Between Youngstown and Trump.** [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] asked whether the President could act without Congress; Nixon asks whether he can withhold evidence from the courts. Nixon borrows Jackson's method, quoting his concurrence for the Constitution's "separateness but interdependence, autonomy but reciprocity."

**The judicial half of a removal in fact.** The tapes the Court compelled were released in early August 1974, and Nixon resigned on August 9 before the House voted on the articles his Judiciary Committee had approved. Compare [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]], where the political process alone failed.

**The Executive bound by itself.** The Special Prosecutor was shielded only by a revocable regulation, and the Court held the Executive bound while it stood. Congress later created a statutory independent counsel, upheld in *Morrison v. Olson* (1988). [[Trump v. Slaughter]] and [[Trump v. United States]] treat control of prosecution and removal as core executive power, straining how far such a regulation can bind the President.

**The settings reserved.** *Nixon v. Fitzgerald* (1982) gave absolute civil immunity for official acts; *Clinton v. Jones* (1997) denied temporary immunity for unofficial conduct; *Trump v. Vance* (2020) allowed a state criminal subpoena for private records; *Trump v. Mazars* (2020) set its own test for congressional subpoenas.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

- **Is this a Youngstown case?** Not in form: no statute is at issue, and the conflict is with the courts rather than Congress. But Nixon borrows Jackson's method, treating presidential power as relational and balanced against another branch's core function. The distinguishing fact is that the competing function is Article III's duty "to do justice in criminal prosecutions."
- **Vary the claim.** Had the President asserted "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," the Court signals much more deference. A congressional demand is expressly reserved, and *Mazars* later answered it with a test of its own; a civil demand is also reserved, and *Clinton v. Jones* later let the suit proceed.
- **Vary the prosecutor.** Had the Attorney General revoked the regulation and fired the Special Prosecutor before suit, there would have been no "concrete adverseness," and the President could have directed his subordinate to drop the subpoena — which is what happened to the first Special Prosecutor in October 1973. So Nixon is about the Executive binding itself, not about Congress limiting removal, which is how it survives [[Trump v. Slaughter]]. The live question: can the President be bound by his own subordinate's regulation while it stands?
- **Nixon against Trump v. United States.** Nixon makes a President surrender evidence for someone else's trial under a balancing test. [[Trump v. United States|Trump]] holds that a former President cannot be prosecuted for core acts and is presumptively immune for other official acts. The Trump majority cites Nixon and *Burr* (1807), where Marshall held a subpoena could issue to President Jefferson, as rejections of "absolute privilege," and its test of "dangers of intrusion on the authority and functions of the Executive Branch" resembles Nixon's balance. The distinguishing facts are the defendant and the burden: the President himself, facing a prosecution rather than a subpoena.
- **Nixon and impeachment.** The case never decided whether a President can be prosecuted; it assumed a criminal process in which the President was a named coconspirator. Resignation and President Ford's pardon left that question open until 2024. Sotomayor's dissent in Trump argues that the pardon and its acceptance "rested on the understanding that the former President faced potential criminal liability."
- **Originalism against doctrine.** The privilege is not in the text; the Court infers it from the nature of enumerated powers, much as [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] infers implied congressional powers. Be ready to say whether an implied presidential privilege is more or less legitimate than McCulloch's implied powers.
- **Formalism and functionalism in one opinion.** The judicial-power holding is formalist: courts say what the law is. The privilege holding is functionalist: confidentiality is weighed against criminal justice. Contrast [[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]] and Black in Youngstown, which refuse to balance.
- **Memorable lines.** "[P]resumptively privileged." "[D]emonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial." "The very integrity of the judicial system . . . depend[s] on full disclosure of all the facts."
