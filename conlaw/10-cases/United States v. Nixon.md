## Rule

The President has a constitutionally based privilege for confidential communications, and those communications are "presumptively privileged," but the privilege is qualified: "[t]he generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial." The courts, not the President, decide the privilege's scope, and a dispute between the President and a Special Prosecutor protected by a binding regulation is justiciable.

## Facts

- **The subpoena sought evidence for someone else's criminal trial.** The Special Prosecutor subpoenaed tapes and documents of the President's conversations for the trial of seven indicted Watergate defendants. *The holding is confined to criminal trials; civil and congressional demands are reserved.*
- **The President claimed only a generalized interest in confidentiality.** *Decisive: there was no claim "to protect military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," which would have drawn more deference.*
- **The Special Prosecutor held office under an Attorney General regulation.** It gave him "explicit power to contest the invocation of executive privilege." *This defeats the claim of an unreviewable intra-branch dispute: "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force the Executive Branch is bound by it."*
- **The grand jury named the President an unindicted coconspirator.** *It linked the President's own conversations to the charged conspiracy, which bears on relevance under Rule 17(c).*
- **The District Court would inspect the materials in camera first.** *The privilege is protected by a judge's private review, not defeated by public disclosure.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (8–0; Burger, C.J., for the Court; Rehnquist, J., took no part)

- The District Court's order was appealable, and the case was properly before the Court on certiorari before judgment.
- The dispute is justiciable. "The mere assertion of a claim of an 'intra-branch dispute,' without more, has never operated to defeat federal jurisdiction." The regulation binds the Executive while in force, and the setting supplies "that concrete adverseness which sharpens the presentation of issues."
- The subpoena satisfied Rule 17(c)'s three "hurdles": "(1) relevancy; (2) admissibility; (3) specificity."
- The courts decide the claim of privilege. Quoting [[Marbury v. Madison|Marbury]]: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
- "Neither the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances."
- The privilege is real and "constitutionally based," and conversations are "presumptively privileged," but "[t]he generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial." Affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *The separation of powers makes each branch the final judge of its own powers, so the President alone decides whether his communications are privileged.* Burger: the judicial power "can no more be shared with the Executive Branch" than the President can share the veto with the courts; Marbury settles who says what the law is.
- *This is an internal Executive Branch dispute between the President and his subordinate, which the President alone may resolve.* Burger: the Attorney General has delegated authority to the Special Prosecutor by regulation, and "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force the Executive Branch is bound by it."
- *Confidentiality is essential to candid advice, because "those who expect public dissemination of their remarks may well temper candor."* Burger accepts the premise and calls the interest "weighty indeed and entitled to great respect," but "we cannot conclude that advisers will be moved to temper the candor of their remarks by the infrequent occasions of disclosure" in criminal prosecutions.
- *Even if the privilege is qualified, the independence of the Executive "within its own sphere" insulates the President from a judicial subpoena.* Burger: an absolute privilege would impede "the primary constitutional duty of the Judicial Branch to do justice in criminal prosecutions."

## Context

Nixon sits between [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] and [[Trump v. United States]]. Youngstown asked whether the President could act without Congress; Nixon asks whether he can withhold evidence from the courts. Nixon quotes Jackson's Youngstown concurrence for the idea that the Constitution "enjoins upon its branches separateness but interdependence, autonomy but reciprocity," and its method is a balance rather than a categorical rule. The ancestor is [[United States v. Burr]] (1807), where Marshall held that a subpoena could issue to President Jefferson.

The Court decided the case on July 24, 1974. The tapes it compelled were released in early August, and Nixon resigned on August 9 before the House voted on the articles of impeachment its Judiciary Committee had approved. The case is thus the judicial half of the only presidential removal-in-fact; compare [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]], where the political process alone failed to remove.

Nixon also bears on removal. The Special Prosecutor was protected only by an Attorney General regulation, which the Executive could revoke, and the Court held the Executive bound while it stood. Congress later created a statutory independent counsel, upheld in [[Morrison v. Olson]] (1988). [[Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) and [[Trump v. United States]] (2024) both treat the President's control of prosecution and removal as core executive power, which puts pressure on how far a regulation like this one can bind the President himself.

Later cases on presidential process: [[Nixon v. Fitzgerald]] (1982) gave former Presidents absolute immunity from civil damages for official acts; [[Clinton v. Jones]] (1997) denied temporary immunity from civil suit for unofficial conduct; [[Trump v. Vance]] (2020) allowed a state criminal subpoena for a sitting President's private records; [[Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP|Trump v. Mazars]] (2020) set a separate test for congressional subpoenas, one of the settings Nixon reserved.

## Class layer

### Posture

- The District Court (Sirica, J.) denied the President's motion to quash on May 20, 1974, and ordered the materials produced for in camera examination. The President sought review in the D.C. Circuit.
- On May 24 the Special Prosecutor petitioned for certiorari before judgment, and the President cross-petitioned; the Court granted review, heard argument July 8, and decided July 24, 1974.

### Facts

After the 1972 Watergate break-in, the Attorney General appointed a Special Prosecutor under a Justice Department regulation that delegated authority over Watergate offenses and limited his removal. In March 1974 a grand jury indicted seven men, including former senior White House and campaign officials, for conspiracy to defraud the United States and obstruct justice, and named the President an unindicted coconspirator. The Special Prosecutor obtained a Rule 17(c) subpoena for tapes and documents of specified conversations between the President and his aides. The President's counsel moved to quash, asserting executive privilege and arguing that the dispute was an internal Executive Branch matter. The District Court denied the motion and ordered the materials delivered for in camera review.

### Issue

- Is the order reviewable, and is the dispute between the President and the Special Prosecutor justiciable?
- Did the subpoena satisfy Rule 17(c)?
- Is the President's privilege absolute, so that the President alone decides whether confidential communications must be produced?
- If the privilege is qualified, does it yield to a subpoena for use in a criminal trial?

### Holding

- Yes to both. The order was appealable, and the regulation binding the Executive made the dispute a concrete controversy rather than an intra-branch disagreement.
- Yes. The Special Prosecutor showed relevance, admissibility and specificity.
- No. The courts decide the scope of the privilege, and neither separation of powers nor the need for confidentiality supports an absolute privilege.
- Yes. A generalized interest in confidentiality, with no claim of military, diplomatic or national-security secrets, yields to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial, subject to in camera review.

### Rule

- Presidential communications are "presumptively privileged," and the privilege has "constitutional underpinnings" even though the Constitution nowhere mentions it.
- The presumption yields to "the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."
- Claims to protect "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets" stand on different footing.
- The Executive is bound by its own regulation "[s]o long as [it] remains in force."

### Reasoning

**(Burger, C.J., for the Court)**

- **Justiciability.** The Attorney General has statutory power to appoint subordinates and delegated authority by regulation. The regulation "gives the Special Prosecutor explicit power to contest the invocation of executive privilege," so the dispute is one courts can resolve.
- **Who decides.** "Many decisions of this Court, however, have unequivocally reaffirmed the holding of Marbury v. Madison." The judicial power under Article III cannot be shared with the Executive.
- **Source of privilege.** "Nowhere in the Constitution, as we have noted earlier, is there any explicit reference to a privilege of confidentiality, yet to the extent this interest relates to the effective discharge of a President's powers, it is constitutionally based." "The President's need for complete candor and objectivity from advisers calls for great deference from the courts."
- **The balance.** "[W]e must weigh the importance of the general privilege of confidentiality of Presidential communications in performance of the President's responsibilities against the inroads of such a privilege on the fair administration of criminal justice."
- **Criminal justice.** "The very integrity of the judicial system and public confidence in the system depend on full disclosure of all the facts, within the framework of the rules of evidence." The Fifth and Sixth Amendments guarantee due process and compulsory process, which a blanket privilege would defeat.
- **Minimal harm.** "The President's broad interest in confidentiality of communications will not be vitiated by disclosure of a limited number of conversations preliminarily shown to have some bearing on the pending criminal cases."
- **Limits.** "We are not here concerned with the balance between the President's generalized interest in confidentiality and the need for relevant evidence in civil litigation, nor with that between the confidentiality interest and congressional demands for information, nor with the President's interest in preserving state secrets."
- **Procedure.** In camera inspection "is always a procedure calling for scrupulous protection against any release or publication of material not found by the court" to be relevant and admissible.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous (8–0). Rehnquist, J., who had served in Nixon's Justice Department, took no part.

### Cold-call notes

- **Is this a Youngstown case?** Not in form: there is no statute at issue, and the conflict is with the courts rather than Congress. But Nixon borrows Jackson's method, treating presidential power as relational and balanced against another branch's core function. The distinguishing fact: the competing function is Article III's duty "to do justice in criminal prosecutions," not Congress's legislative power.
- **Vary the claim.** If the President had asserted "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," the Court signals much more deference; it does not say the privilege would then be absolute. If the demand came from Congress, Nixon reserves the question ([[Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP|Mazars]] later answered it with its own test). If the case were civil, the balance is reserved; [[Clinton v. Jones]] later let a civil suit proceed.
- **Vary the prosecutor.** If the Attorney General had revoked the regulation and fired the Special Prosecutor before suit, there would be no "concrete adverseness," and the President could have directed his subordinate to drop the subpoena. That is what happened to the first Special Prosecutor in October 1973, and why the regulation was rewritten with consultation requirements. Nixon is thus a case about the Executive binding itself, not about Congress limiting removal.
- **Nixon and removal after Slaughter.** [[Trump v. Slaughter]] holds that officers exercising executive power must be removable at will, and [[Trump v. United States]] calls prosecution decisions core executive power. A regulation limiting removal of a prosecutor is the President's own choice and revocable, so it is consistent with both. The question to be ready for: can the President be bound by his subordinate's regulation while it stands? Nixon says yes; the tension with the unitary-executive cases is that the President could have ordered revocation at any time.
- **Nixon versus Trump v. United States.** Nixon: a President must give up evidence for someone else's trial, under a balancing test. Trump: a former President cannot be prosecuted himself for core acts and is presumptively immune for other official acts, and official conduct is largely inadmissible. The Trump majority cites Nixon (and Burr) as cases rejecting "absolute privilege," and reads them as showing the President is not immune from all criminal process; its presumptive-immunity test ("dangers of intrusion on the authority and functions of the Executive Branch") resembles Nixon's balance. The distinguishing facts are the defendant (the President himself) and the burden (a prosecution, not a subpoena).
- **Nixon and impeachment.** The case did not decide whether a President can be prosecuted; it assumed a criminal process in which a President was a named coconspirator. Resignation, and President Ford's pardon, left the question of prosecution open until 2024. Sotomayor's dissent in Trump argues that the pardon and its acceptance "rested on the understanding that the former President faced potential criminal liability."
- **Originalism versus doctrine.** The privilege is not in the text; the Court derives it from the "nature of enumerated powers," as [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] derives implied powers. The same structural inference later supports civil immunity ([[Nixon v. Fitzgerald|Fitzgerald]]) and criminal immunity (Trump). Be ready to say whether an implied, structural presidential privilege is more or less legitimate than the implied congressional powers in McCulloch.
- **Formalism and functionalism.** The judicial-power holding is formalist (Marbury: courts say what the law is). The privilege holding is functionalist (weigh confidentiality against criminal justice). Contrast [[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]] and Black in Youngstown, which refuse to balance.
- **Memorable lines.** "[P]resumptively privileged"; "demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial"; "separateness but interdependence, autonomy but reciprocity" (Jackson, quoted); "The very integrity of the judicial system . . . depend[s] on full disclosure of all the facts."
