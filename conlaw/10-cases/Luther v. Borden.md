## Rule

Which of two rival governments is the lawful government of a State is a political question: under the Guarantee Clause the decision rests with Congress, and under the 1795 militia act with the President, and courts must follow the political branches' decision.

## Facts

- **Luther, a supporter of the Dorr government, sued Borden and others in trespass for breaking into his house; the defendants justified the entry as militiamen of the charter government acting under martial law.** *The defense stood or fell on which government was lawful in 1842.*
- **Luther offered ballots, voter registers and census figures to prove that a majority of adult male citizens, and of qualified voters, had ratified the people's constitution; the Circuit Court excluded the evidence.** *Taney held that no court could count those votes, because no prior state law fixed who was qualified to vote.*
- **Rhode Island's own courts, including in the trial of Thomas Dorr, had held that the question belonged to the political power and that the charter government was lawful.** *Federal courts follow state courts on questions of state constitutional law.*
- **On the charter governor's application, the President recognized him as the State's executive and prepared to call out the militia; that announcement ended the armed opposition.** *The militia was never actually called out, but Taney held the recognition "as effectual" and "equally authoritative."*
- **No senators or representatives were elected under the Dorr government, so Congress never had to choose between the two governments.** *The right to decide still lay with Congress, "and not in the courts."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Taney, C.J., for the Court; Woodbury, J., dissenting)

- The Circuit Court's judgment for the defendants is affirmed.
- Under Article IV, § 4, "it rests with Congress to decide what government is the established one in a State," because Congress must decide what government exists before deciding whether it is republican.
- Congress's seating of a State's senators and representatives recognizes that State's government, and that recognition "could not be questioned in a judicial tribunal."
- Under the domestic-violence clause, Congress gave the President, by the act of February 28, 1795, the power to decide whether an insurrection exists, which requires him to decide which government is lawful. A court may not review that decision.
- Judicial power presupposes an established government; a state court that found its own government displaced "would cease to be a court."
- Sovereignty resides in the people of each State, but whether they have in fact changed their government "is a question to be settled by the political power."

**Rejected**

- *Sovereignty resides in the people; a majority ratified the new constitution; so the charter government was displaced, and a court can confirm the vote from the ballots and registers.* Taney accepts popular sovereignty but holds that the political power decides whether a change occurred, and that setting voter qualifications without a prior law is making law: "It is the province of a court to expound the law, not to make it."
- *Leaving the decision to the President is dangerous to liberty and may be abused.* "All power may be abused if placed in unworthy hands"; no other hands would be safer and equally effective, and court proceedings are "utterly unfit for the crisis." If courts could second-guess the President mid-conflict, "the guarantee contained in the Constitution of the United States is a guarantee of anarchy, and not of order."

## Context

The casebook presents Luther as predating the modern political question doctrine but shaping it, paired with *Nixon v. United States* (1993). Its afterlife was political. Attorney General Bates relied on Luther for deference to the President's finding of insurrection in [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]], against [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]]. The Reconstruction Congress invoked Luther to support military reconstruction, and [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] relies on Luther for Congress's primacy under the Guarantee Clause while deciding Texas's status itself. See [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]], [[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]], [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] and [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].

Luther's holding that the Guarantee Clause is non-justiciable remains important today. *Pacific States Telephone & Telegraph Co. v. Oregon* (1912) applied it to challenges to the initiative and referendum, and Guarantee Clause challenges to malapportionment failed on the same ground. *Baker v. Carr* (1962) let malapportionment challenges proceed under the Equal Protection Clause instead, reading Luther as holding only that the Guarantee Clause "is not a repository of judicially manageable standards." The casebook's Baker notes press the "deep similarity" between the two cases: in both, state electoral rules let a minority hold power. See also [[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]].
