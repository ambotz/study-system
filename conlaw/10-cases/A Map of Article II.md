## What it is

A two-page casebook note (pp. 168–169) that walks through Article II's four sections, asks which royal prerogatives each one assigns, shares or denies, and names four themes of the executive: energy, independence, unity, and law-executing rather than law-making. It sets up [[The Federalist No. 70]], which follows immediately.

## What to notice

- **The four sections.**
  - § 1 vests "the executive Power" in one "President of the United States" and covers selection, term and oath.
  - § 2 "specifies, describes, and limits" particular powers, several of them English prerogatives: command, pardon, treaties, appointments.
  - § 3 lists five duties or powers: the State of the Union, recommending legislation, receiving ambassadors, taking care that the laws are faithfully executed, and commissioning "all officers of the United States."
  - § 4 makes the President and Vice President, "unlike the king, . . . subject to impeachment."
- **The editors' sorting questions.** For each prerogative on the list in [[Introduction to Article II]]: is it given to the President, given to Congress in Article I, given to the courts in Article III, shared, denied, or left unmentioned? And "what about the Constitution's silence"? A working sort, useful on the exam:
  - To Congress: tax, spend, borrow, declare war, raise armies, regulate foreign commerce, grant patents.
  - Shared: the veto (qualified and overridable); treaties and appointments (with the Senate).
  - To the President alone: the pardon; receiving ambassadors; command of the armed forces.
  - Denied outright: titles of nobility; suspending and dispensing are implicitly denied by the Take Care Clause; suspending habeas corpus appears only in Article I.
  - Unmentioned: removal of officers. The whole removal unit is an argument about this silence.
- **Theme one: energy.** The President keeps "a number of prerogative powers needed for vigorous leadership of the nation, especially in war."
- **Theme two: independence from Congress.** The contrast is a parliamentary prime minister, who depends on the legislature to stay in office. The President has a fixed four-year term and "cannot be removed by Congress on a vote of no confidence." The one exception runs the other way: Congress selects the President only if no one wins an Electoral College majority.
- **Theme three: unity.** "The framers of the Constitution made a deliberate choice to vest the executive power in a single individual, rejecting the options of a plural executive or an executive council or cabinet." This is the premise the unitary-executive argument builds on. If the choice of a single executive was deliberate, an agency run by officers the President cannot remove looks like the plural executive the framers rejected. That is the argument of [[Myers v. United States|Myers]], [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] and [[Trump v. Slaughter]], and the answer to it in [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] is that the commission exercises no executive power at all.
- **Theme four: law-executing, not law-making.** The Stuart lawmaking prerogatives (proclamations, dispensing, suspending) are absent. "Except for the pardon power, none of these lawmaking prerogative powers are expressly given to the president," and the President instead has the duty to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." The Take Care Clause is thus a limit as well as a grant. Black reads it that way in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]], and the same limit returns with non-enforcement in [[Adams v. Richardson]].
- **The themes pull against each other.** Energy and unity favor presidential control of officers; the law-executing theme can favor Congress, which writes the laws that officers execute and may structure the offices that execute them. The removal cases can be read as a contest between theme three and Congress's power to create offices "by Law."
