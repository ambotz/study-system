## What it is

The casebook's second note in the taxing-power assignment (Paulsen, McConnell, Bray & Baude, 4th ed., pp. 603–607, running onto the top of p. 608). It opens with the Sixteenth Amendment's answer to [[Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co|Pollock]], then changes the question. [[Hylton v. United States|Hylton]] and Pollock were about *how* a tax must be laid — apportioned or uniform — and never doubted that Congress could tax carriages or land at all. This note asks whether anything besides apportionment and uniformity limits the taxing power: in particular, whether Congress may tax in order to regulate, and whether it may tax activity it has no enumerated power to regulate directly.

The answer comes as a chronological survey of six decisions, from the [[License Tax Cases]] (1866) to [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] (1953), with [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Bailey]] (1922) as the one case in the line that struck a tax down. It is assigned to set up [[NFIB v. Sebelius]], where every question the note raises is argued again.

## What to notice

- **The Sixteenth Amendment as an Article V answer to a case.** "It was the third time in history that the country had reversed a Supreme Court decision with a constitutional amendment, and it would not be the last." The casebook's own question is the good one: does the Amendment show Pollock was wrong, or concede that Pollock was right and that only an amendment could change the result? Pollock itself had pointed the way, saying of the Constitution that "the instrument defines the way for its amendment. In no part of it was greater sagacity displayed." Compare [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], overturned by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments rather than by the Court.
- **The Amendment does not moot the direct-tax question.** It exempts taxes "on incomes, from whatever source derived" and nothing else. A tax on wealth is not an income tax, so the Hylton/Pollock fight still decides whether it must be apportioned. The casebook poses exactly that hypothetical, and then a second one — "a tax on not purchasing health insurance" — which is [[NFIB v. Sebelius]].
- **The framing question of the section.** "May Congress impose a tax, for regulatory purposes, even where it might not have the express power to regulate that activity directly?" The general answer the cases give is yes: "the Court has held that the Taxing Clause power is an independent power that can be used to achieve goals not attainable under the other enumerated powers."
- **[[License Tax Cases]] (1866): taxing what you cannot regulate.** The Court conceded Congress had "no power of regulation nor any direct control" over intrastate lottery ticket sales and liquor retailing, and upheld the federal license fee anyway: the taxing power "reaches every subject, and may be exercised at discretion." The limit is that a federal license confers no permission — "Congress cannot authorize a trade or business within a State in order to tax it," and the license implies "nothing except that the licensee shall be subject to no penalties under national law, if he pays it." So the taxing power can burden what the Commerce Clause cannot reach, but cannot license what state law forbids.
- **[[Veazie Bank v. Fenno]] (1869): size is for Congress.** A tax on state bank notes heavy enough to destroy them survived, because "the responsibility of the Legislature is not to the courts, but to the people by whom its members are elected." The dictum is what matters later: "There are, indeed, certain virtual limitations, arising from the principles of the Constitution itself," and a tax would be an abuse "if so exercised as to impair the separate existence and independent self-government of the States, or if exercised for ends inconsistent with the limited grants of power in the Constitution." [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Bailey]] turns that dictum into a holding.
- **[[McCray v. United States]] (1904): motive is off limits.** The oleomargarine excise was "almost certainly enacted to benefit butter producers," and the Court refused to look. Reviewing Congress's motive would mean that "the abuse by one department of the government of its lawful powers is to be corrected by the abuse of its powers by another department"; the remedy "lies, not in the abuse by the judicial authority of its functions, but in the people." The tension with [[Veazie Bank v. Fenno|Veazie]]'s dictum is plain: if motive is unreviewable, the "virtual limitations" cannot be enforced.
- **Bailey / the Child Labor Tax Case (1922): the one time the line bites.** Congress lost [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] on the Commerce Clause in 1918 and answered with a 10 percent excise on the net profits of any mine, quarry, mill, cannery, workshop or factory that employed children below the prescribed ages or hours. The statute is reprinted in full at pp. 605–606, and its details are the holding: the age and hour specifications, the exemption for "a bona fide boys' or girls' canning club," a defense for a reasonable mistake or reliance on a government age certificate, and enforcement "either by the commissioner of internal revenue or the secretary of labor." The Court distinguished [[Veazie Bank v. Fenno|Veazie]] and [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] because they did not involve "the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern and business with a heavy exaction to promote the efficacy of such regulation," and held the case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart."
- **Bailey's two propositions.** First, motive can be read off the face of the statute: "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children within the age limits prescribed. Its prohibitory and regulatory effect and purpose are palpable. All others can see and understand this. How can we properly shut our minds to it?" Second, the federalism stake: "To give such magic to the word 'tax' would be to break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states."
- **The line itself, stated as a matter of degree.** "Taxes are occasionally imposed . . . with the primary motive of obtaining revenue from them and with the incidental motive of discouraging them by making their continuance onerous. They do not lose their character as taxes because of the incidental motive. But there comes a time in the extension of the penalizing features of the so-called tax when it loses its character as such and becomes a mere penalty, with the characteristics of regulation and punishment." The casebook's question is whether Bailey tells you where that time comes, and the honest answer is that it identifies factors — detail, weight, scienter, enforcing agency — without supplying a threshold.
- **The 8–1 vote is the puzzle (Note 4).** [[Hammer v. Dagenhart|Hammer]] was 5–4, but Bailey was 8–1, with only Justice Clarke dissenting in both. Three Justices who thought Congress could ban child-made goods from commerce still thought it could not tax child labor. One reading is institutional: the tax was an open attempt to get around a decision four years old, and the Court was defending its own authority. A better one is on the merits — a shipping ban operates on goods crossing state lines, while the tax operated inside the factory, prescribing minimum ages, a daily and weekly hours cap and the earliest and latest hours a child could work, with the Department of Labor entitled to inspect. The tax was the more intrusive statute even though it was formally the gentler one.
- **[[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]] (1937): the tack back.** The Social Security Act's unemployment payroll tax, with a 90 percent credit for contributions to a certified state fund, was designed to induce states to pass laws Congress could not require them to pass, and it survived 5–4. The challenge was not to the tax but to coercion of the states, and the Court answered that "[t]he statute does not call for a surrender by the states of powers essential to their quasi-sovereign existence," distinguishing the Child Labor Tax Case as presenting different issues. Note the year: [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]] and [[Helvering v. Davis]] sit beside [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]] and [[West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish]] in the "Revolution of 1937."
- **[[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] (1953): revenue is enough.** The tax on bookmakers was upheld though "the intent to curtail and hinder, as well as tax," was "manifest." "[A] federal excise tax does not cease to be valid merely because it discourages or deters the activities taxed. Nor is the tax invalid because the revenue obtained is negligible. . . . But regardless of its regulatory effect, the wagering tax produces revenue." Revenue production becomes the operative criterion, which is what Roberts later relies on in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]. Jackson concurred with a warning: "I concur in the judgment and opinion of the Court, but with such doubt that if the minority agreed upon an opinion which did not impair legitimate use of the taxing power I probably would join it."
- **Can [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Bailey]] and [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] both stand?** Yes, but only on the details. Kahriger's tax was a flat annual occupational levy on bookmakers with no schedule of prescribed conduct and no non-revenue enforcement agency; Bailey's was a heavy exaction keyed to a detailed code of employment practice, with a scienter requirement and Labor Department inspection. If the distinction is unconvincing, the casebook's alternative is that "Kahriger prove[s] that the Child Labor Tax Case couldn't come up with an administrable line between taxes and penalties."
- **The summary question (Note 7).** Whether a tax must raise revenue to be a tax, whether Congress's own label counts, and whether the tax/penalty distinction has content are the three questions that decide [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]. Roberts answers: revenue matters (Kahriger), the label controls only statutory questions like the Anti-Injunction Act, and the distinction has content supplied by Bailey's factors — which he then applies to reach the opposite result from Bailey's.
- **Why the taxing power is the important workaround.** [[Hammer v. Dagenhart|Hammer]], [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] and Roberts's opinion in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] all say there is activity beyond the commerce power. The [[License Tax Cases]], [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] and [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] say the taxing power reaches it anyway. The only thing holding the two halves together is that a tax can only take money: it "is limited to requiring an individual to pay money into the Federal Treasury, no more" (Roberts in NFIB). Whether that is a real limit is the question the whole unit turns on.
