## What it is

The opening of Chapter 4 (The Amendment Process), pp. 803–804. It sets out Article V's text and then poses the problem the whole chapter is built on: the Congressional Pay Amendment, proposed in 1789 as part of the original package of amendments and declared ratified in 1992, now listed as the Twenty-seventh Amendment. "But is this amendment, sometimes called the Congressional Pay Amendment, really part of the Constitution? Was it proposed and ratified in accordance with the procedures specified by the Constitution for making amendments?"

## What to notice

- **The rules, in the casebook's own summary.** Proposal by two-thirds of both houses, or by a national convention called by Congress on the application of two-thirds of the states. Ratification by three-fourths of the states, "acting either through their state legislatures or through special state ratifying conventions—whichever method Congress chooses in the particular case."
- **Why the original Constitution used conventions instead of legislatures.** The framers meant "to circumvent the state legislatures, which jealously guarded their own powers." Article V carries that choice forward as a congressional option, and Congress has used the convention route once, for the Twenty-first Amendment.
- **The ratification timeline, which is the fact that generates every question.** Six states ratified in the 1789–1791 period; Ohio ratified in 1873; "[a] large number of states ratified the amendment in the 1980s and early 1990s, with Michigan's ratification on May 7, 1992, as the thirty-eighth state (of fifty) putting the amendment over the three-fourths mark." Roughly 203 years from proposal to the scale-tipping ratification.
- **Congress ratified the result politically, and nearly unanimously.** It voted "414–3 in the House and 99–0 in the Senate to 'accept' the Twenty-seventh Amendment as validly ratified."
- **The executive branch moved first.** The Archivist of the United States, Don W. Wilson, decided to "certify the adoption of the amendment" in reliance on an OLC opinion, before Congress voted (citing Sanford Levinson, *Authorizing Constitutional Text: On the Purported Twenty-Seventh Amendment*, 11 Const. Commentary 101, 107 (1994)). The question the casebook asks: "Does the National Archives have a role in deciding what is in the text of the Constitution?"
- **Almost no judicial law here, and that is the pedagogical point.** "Very few judicial opinions interpret Article V, which means this is an opportunity for you to engage in constitutional interpretation without the courts having given their view first." Expect Baude to treat this class as interpretation from the text, structure and practice rather than doctrine.
- **What is actually unsettled about the Twenty-seventh Amendment.** Nobody disputes that two-thirds of both houses proposed it or that thirty-eight legislatures ratified it. The dispute is whether those ratifications, spread over two centuries, can count together — which is the contemporaneous-consensus question [[Dillon v. Gloss]] raises and never answers for an amendment proposed without a deadline.
