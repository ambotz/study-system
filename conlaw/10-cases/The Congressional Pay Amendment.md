## What it is

The opening of Chapter 4 (The Amendment Process), the 5th ed.'s framing for the whole class. It states Article V's rules, lists the ambiguities they leave, and then sets up three worked examples: [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]], the ratification of the Congressional Pay Amendment as the Twenty-seventh Amendment, and [[The Equal Rights Amendment]]. The two questions it says all three raise: "What are the rules for amending the Constitution, and who decides if an amendment is valid?"

## What to notice

- **The rules, as the casebook states them.** Proposal by two-thirds of both houses, or by a convention called by Congress on the application of two-thirds of the states. Ratification by three-fourths of the states, "acting either through their state legislatures or through special state ratifying conventions — whichever method Congress chooses in the particular case."
- **Why the original Constitution used conventions.** To "circumvent the state legislatures, which jealously guarded their own powers." Article V carries that choice forward as a congressional option.
- **The four ambiguities, each with its authority.** Must an amendment go to the President? No: *Hollingsworth v. Virginia*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 378 (1798). Is there a time limit? A "reasonable time": [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]], 256 U.S. 368 (1921). How long is reasonable — unanswered. Who resolves these questions: Congress, under [[Coleman v. Miller]], 307 U.S. 433 (1939), "suggesting, without a controlling majority opinion, either that Congress possesses this power or that its acceptance of a state ratification is a 'political question'"?
- **The scale of the problem, per Pozen & Schmidt.** There are so many ambiguities that "the overwhelming majority of recognized amendments had significant arguable legal infirmities at the time of their adoption." David E. Pozen & Thomas P. Schmidt, *The Puzzles and Possibilities of Article V*, 121 Colum. L. Rev. 2317 (2021). Worth holding onto: the course's other classes assume the Constitution's text is fixed, and this one says the text itself rests on contested judgments.
- **The executive branch moved first, and that is the chapter's real subject.** Archivist Don W. Wilson "had already decided to 'certify the adoption of the amendment,'" relying on [[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment|the OLC opinion]], before Congress voted to accept it (citing Levinson, 11 Const. Commentary 101, 107 (1994)). "Does the National Archives have a role in deciding what is in the text of the Constitution?"
- **The ratification history, which is the fact pattern.** Proposed in 1789 as part of the original package: "No law, varying the compensation for the services of Senators and Representatives, shall take effect, until an election of Representatives shall have intervened." Six ratifications in the Bill of Rights period; Ohio in 1873; then "a large number of states" in the 1980s and early 1990s, "with Michigan's ratification on May 7, 1992, as the thirty-eighth state (of fifty) putting the amendment over the three-fourths mark."
- **Never tested in court.** The casebook calls it the "(alleged?)" ratification and says so twice: no judicial decision exists because Congress has not tried to violate the Amendment.
- **The chapter's own framing of the conflict.** "The Office of Legal Counsel in the executive branch disagreed with Dillon and concluded that despite this very long time, the amendment was valid." The disagreement is the assignment, not a footnote to it.
- **Where the ERA fits.** Proposed 1972; Congress "imposed a time limit, then extended it, while some states tried to 'rescind' their ratifications and others tried to ratify it decades after the deadline expired. Some people think it is already the 28th Amendment."
