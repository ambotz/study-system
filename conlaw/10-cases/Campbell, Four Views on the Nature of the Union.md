## What it is

The Canvas posting is a set of four diagrams, one per founding-era theory of the nature of the Union, illustrating Jud Campbell's framework. Each figure plots the same elements — state governments and constitutions, the U.S. Constitution, the state "body politic" of Virginia and Pennsylvania, their citizens, and (where it exists) a national or federal body politic — and shades in **black the locus of sovereignty**. The single idea the figures carry: you cannot know how to interpret the Constitution until you decide *who is sovereign in the Union*, and the four founders answer that differently. The reading opens the Federalism unit and pairs with [[The Bank Debate]], because the founders' fight over implied powers was downstream of this prior question. Read the diagrams against [[A Map of the Federalism Provisions]] (the structure) and [[The Federalist No. 10]] (the extended-republic theory).

## What to notice

- **The organizing question is the locus of sovereignty, and it fixes the method.** Decide which body politic made the Constitution — the peoples of the several states, a shared federal body, or one national people — and you have decided where sovereignty lies; decide that, and you have decided how to construe federal power. Two sub-questions separate the four views: *who* holds sovereignty, and *when* a national polity (if any) came into being.

- **Figure 1 — Jefferson's Compact Theory.** "The peoples of the several states formed an interstate compact by adopting the federal Constitution... the Constitution did not transfer or diminish state sovereignty. It merely delegated certain powers to the federal government." Sovereignty stays entirely in the **state** bodies politic (black); there is **no** national body politic. The Constitution is more enduring than the Articles of Confederation only because it was made by the peoples themselves, not by state governments — but it remains a compact among sovereigns.
  - *Interpretive payoff:* a compact among sovereigns is read under the law of nations, where "a legal instrument could be read to alienate sovereign rights only if it did so in clear and express terms." That is the bridge from compact theory to **strict construction** and the Tenth Amendment — and, pressed further, to nullification and secession.

- **Figure 2 — Madison's Quasi-Nationalism.** "The peoples of the several states, upon adopting the Constitution, transferred part of their sovereignty to a federal body politic... the Constitution blended confederal and national features, and it therefore created a distinctive polity — one that remained somewhat tethered to state polities. The methods and processes for construing the Constitution should thus reflect its idiosyncratic origins and character." A **Federal Body Politic** appears (dashed, established 1787), alongside the still-sovereign state bodies politic; the dashes signal the hybrid, tethered character.
  - *Interpretive payoff:* neither strict nor loose. Madison's "reasonable medium between expounding the Constitution with the strictness of a penal law... and expounding it with a laxity which may vary its essential character." The method tracks the idiosyncratic origins.

- **Figure 3 — Marshall's 1787 Nationalism.** "The peoples of the several states, upon adopting the Constitution, transferred part of their sovereignty to a national body politic. The national polity thus had idiosyncratic origins, having been created through a written agreement among states rather than through an imagined social contract among individuals. But after ratification, the federal polity became fully autonomous and sovereign within the scope of its authority. Consequently, the Constitution paralleled state constitutions and should be interpreted using standard methods." A **National Body Politic** (black, established 1787) sits beside the state bodies politic; the origin lines are drawn but **faded**, because the mode of creation no longer matters once the nation is sovereign.
  - *The Madison/Marshall split is the subtle one.* Both say part of sovereignty moved to a 1787 national body; they divide on whether its state-by-state origins still govern interpretation. Madison says yes (idiosyncratic methods for a tethered hybrid); Marshall says no (standard methods, because after ratification the polity is autonomous and parallels a state constitution). On the diagram, that is the difference between dashes that *persist* (Madison) and dashes that *fade* (Marshall).

- **Figure 4 — Wilson's 1776 Nationalism.** "The people of the United States — acting in a national capacity, and not through the states — formed a national body politic upon declaring independence in 1776... the Constitution formed a new system of government but did not create the nation." The National Body Politic is dated **1776**, and the U.S. Constitution is marked "revised 1787." Wilson alone denies that the states were ever fully sovereign: the nation came first, in 1776, and the Constitution only reorganized its government.
  - *Interpretive payoff:* the most nationalist premise of the four — the Union is older than the Constitution, and sovereignty was national from the start. This is Lincoln's position on secession, and the judicial high-water mark is Wilson's own opinion in *Chisholm v. Georgia* (1793).

- **The two axes, compressed.** *Who is sovereign:* the state peoples (Jefferson) → a shared federal body (Madison) → a national body (Marshall, Wilson). *When the national polity arose:* never (Jefferson) → 1787 (Madison, Marshall) → 1776 (Wilson). *Does the mode of formation still bind interpretation:* yes-as-a-compact (Jefferson), yes-as-a-hybrid (Madison), no (Marshall), no (Wilson). The further you move from Jefferson toward Wilson, the less the states' prior sovereignty constrains federal power and the more "standard" the interpretive method.

- **Mapping the views onto the Bank Debate.**
  - *Jefferson's opinion* rests on the Tenth Amendment and reads "necessary" as indispensable — the strict construction a compact among sovereigns demands.
  - *Madison's speech* takes a strict view of enumeration but accepts incidental means, the "reasonable medium"; his later acceptance of the Bank by settled practice fits the same middle.
  - *Hamilton's opinion* treats every federal power as "in its nature sovereign" and reasons from "the very definition of Government" — nationalist reasoning, closer to Marshall in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] than to Jefferson.
- **Why this orders McCulloch.** Maryland's argument is compact theory (the states made the Constitution and remain sovereign); Marshall answers with 1787 nationalism (the people made it, through state conventions). The dispute over "necessary" follows from the dispute over sovereignty, not the other way round.
- **Where the views lead downstream.** Compact theory runs to nullification and secession; the nationalist views run to Lincoln's claim that the Union is older than the Constitution (Wilson's 1776 position) and to [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]]. Modern doctrine mostly avoids the question; *U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton* (1995) is the rare case that fought it openly, the majority taking a nationalist view and Justice Thomas's dissent a compact view.
- **The critique to hold.** If interpretation depends on a prior theory of sovereignty, text alone cannot settle federalism cases, and an originalist must first pick which founding theory of the Union counts. The diagrams illuminate "the range of interpretive possibilities" rather than choosing among them — which is exactly why the choice resurfaces, unresolved, whenever the modern Court draws a federalism line.
