## Snapshot

The Jeffersonian Congress repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801, abolishing sixteen life-tenured circuit judgeships and returning the Justices to circuit duty. It came up as a bond dispute: a new circuit court had entered judgment on a forthcoming bond given in a cause pending in the court Congress destroyed. The Court sustained the reorganization — Congress may create inferior tribunals and transfer causes among them, and practice since 1789 fixes that Justices may sit as circuit judges. The Appointments Clause and good-behaviour tenure went undiscussed.

## Issue

The casebook calls this "the case that looked like it would be the big confrontation," over "re-imposing circuit riding and abolishing federal judgeships"; the Court answers the first and never reaches the second.

- May a newly constituted inferior court proceed on a forthcoming bond given in a cause pending in a court Congress has since abolished?
- May Supreme Court Justices sit as circuit judges without distinct commissions?

## Rule

Congress's authority to ordain and establish inferior tribunals "from time to time" includes reorganizing them and transferring a pending cause from one to another, and no text restrains it; a forthcoming bond is part of the cause and moves with it. An objection to a practice begun with the organization of the government and acquiesced in since is answered by the practice itself: Justices may ride circuit without separate commissions because use has fixed the construction. Founding-era practice is contemporary interpretation, not mere tradition.

- "Congress have constitutional authority to establish from time to time such inferior tribunals as they may think proper; and to transfer a cause from one such tribunal to another."
- The bond "is an appendage to the cause, or rather a component part of the proceedings."
- "[P]ractice and acquiescence . . . commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer, and has indeed fixed the construction."

## Operative facts

- **From 1789 the Justices rode circuit under the first Judiciary Act with no objection, until the Federalists' Judiciary Act of 1801 created new circuit courts staffed by sixteen new life-tenured judges and ended that duty.** *The practice is the whole answer to the second objection — short, but founding-era, which is what lets Paterson call it "contemporary interpretation" rather than habit. The 1801 judgeships are the problem nobody puts before the Court.*
- **In December term 1801 Laird won judgment against Stuart in a court for the fourth circuit, eastern district of Virginia; execution issued returnable to April term 1802, property was levied on, and Stuart and Charles L. Carter gave a bond for its forthcoming and delivery.** *The bond is tied to an execution out of a court about to cease to exist.*
- **The Repeal Act abolished the 1801 courts and their judgeships, cancelled the Court's 1802 session, and was followed by impeachments of Federalist judges.** *The casebook calls both the abolition and the reimposition "acts of dubious constitutionality." The Court decides under threat, which is what makes the result look like a retreat.*
- **The act of April 29, 1802 "to amend the judicial system of the United States" reorganized the circuits, restored circuit riding, and in § 9 authorized the new courts to proceed on causes from the old.** *Paterson concedes the objection "is true, if there be no statutable provision"; § 9 is the provision, so the case turns on Congress's power to enact it.*
- **In December term 1802 the court for the fifth circuit, sitting in the Virginia district, entered judgment on the bond against Stuart and Carter.** *A court that did not exist when the bond was given enforcing it.*
- **Justices holding no circuit commissions sat on the new circuit courts, Marshall among them, and he had decided this case below.** *Generated by the Justices' own compliance with the Repeal Act — and it put the Chief Justice in the position of reviewing himself.*
- **No displaced 1801 judge was a party; counsel assigned only two reasons for reversal, and Paterson delivered the opinion one week after [[Marbury v. Madison]], Cushing absent for ill health.** *So tenure and compensation are never presented; the abolition is sustained by silence. The sequence is the point: review claimed where the Court ordered nothing, then declined where an order would have been defied.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (Paterson, J., for the Court; Cushing, J., absent on account of ill health; no dissent or concurrence)

- **Transfer of the cause.** "Congress have constitutional authority to establish from time to time such inferior tribunals as they may think proper; and to transfer a cause from one such tribunal to another," and in that particular "there are no words in the constitution to prohibit or restrain the exercise of legislative power." Section 9 of the April 1802 act effected the transfer, and the bond moved with the cause.
- **Circuit riding.** Justices may sit as circuit judges without distinct commissions, because practice and acquiescence "commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer, and has indeed fixed the construction."
- Both grounds assigned for reversing the judgment on the forthcoming bond fail.

**Paterson, for the Court**

- **The first objection is statutory before it is constitutional.** That no court but the one issuing the execution could proceed on the bond "is true, if there be no statutable provision to direct and authorize such proceeding." Once § 9 is identified the only question left is Congress's power, which the Inferior Courts Clause and the absence of restraining text dispose of. "[I]t is nothing more than the removal of the suit" from the fourth circuit to the fifth, "which is authorized to proceed upon and carry it into full effect."
- **The bond is not a separate proceeding.** It "is an appendage to the cause, or rather a component part of the proceedings," so it has no court of its own to lose.
- **The second objection is met with practice, not text.** Paterson observes only that the objection "is of recent date" and that use has settled the question: "It is a contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature. This practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled. Of course, the question is at rest, and ought not now to be disturbed." The Appointments Clause is never analysed, and the claim that circuit judge is a distinct office is never engaged.
- **What the opinion does not say.** It never discusses good-behaviour tenure, the Compensation Clause, or the abolition of the sixteen 1801 judgeships. The analysis is two paragraphs, one textual and one not; the broadest thing at stake goes unmentioned.

## Arguments rejected

- *A bond is an incident of the process that produced it. This one was given for property levied under an execution issuing out of and returnable to a court for the fourth circuit, so once Congress abolished that court no other court could proceed upon it.* Paterson concedes the premise, but only "if there be no statutable provision." Congress may establish inferior tribunals from time to time and transfer causes among them, nothing in the Constitution restrains it, § 9 authorized the fifth circuit to proceed, and the bond is a component part of the proceedings.
- *Circuit judge is a distinct office. A Justice is commissioned to the Supreme Court and never appointed a circuit judge, so he may not sit on a circuit court without a distinct commission; Article II requires an appointment for each office, and acquiescence cannot supply one.* The objection "is of recent date." Practice since 1789 "affords an irresistible answer" and is "a contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature." The Court never answers the argument on its own terms; it refuses to reopen it.

## Where it sits

**The Marbury pairing.** One week after [[Marbury v. Madison]] the Court upheld the Repeal Act. Marbury declared the executive's conduct unlawful while disclaiming power to remedy it; Stuart sustained the legislature's attack on the judiciary. McConnell: "Marbury must be understood as the product of a defeated and demoralized Court."

**Argument from practice.** The cleanest early example of practice as a constitutional argument. It belongs with [[The Decision of 1789]] and [[The Post Roads Debate]], with Hamilton's courts that "liquidate and fix" meaning in [[The Federalist No. 78]], with Frankfurter's historical gloss in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]], and with the modern practice cases, *NLRB v. Noel Canning* and *Zivotofsky v. Kerry*. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].

**Congressional control of the courts.** The first data point in the line running through [[Ex parte McCardle]] to the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]]: Congress may create, abolish and restructure the inferior courts. Whether it may abolish a court to remove its judges has never been squarely decided.

**The appointments point.** The footing Paterson's reasoning lacks came later: an officer may be given duties germane to his office without a new appointment (*Shoemaker* (1893), *Weiss* (1994)).

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "What do you think of the constitutional analysis in Stuart? (What is the analysis?)"**

- Two analyses of different kinds. Transfer: text plus the absence of text — Congress may establish inferior tribunals "from time to time," and "there are no words in the constitution to prohibit or restrain" transferring causes among them. Circuit riding: practice alone, with no textual analysis at all.
- By conceding the objection would be good "if there be no statutable provision," Paterson converts a complaint about losing one's forum into a question of legislative power, which the Inferior Courts Clause answers.
- The weakness is the second question. The strongest objection — a Justice is appointed to one office, a circuit judgeship is another — is never engaged.
- What is absent is larger than what is present: nothing on tenure, compensation, or the sixteen judgeships.

**Note 2. "Was the Court's decision a capitulation? A wise tactical retreat? When deciding whether to confront another branch, should the Court consider the risk to its institutional standing and authority? Or is that an improper consideration?"**

- Capitulation: the Justices had most to lose from circuit riding and ruled for it anyway, with impeachments pending and their 1802 term cancelled.
- Retreat: a court with "neither Force nor Will" could not have made a judgment against Congress stick, and confrontation risked the review power just claimed.
- The third question is the live one. Marbury's rhetoric — the duty is to "say what the law is" — leaves no room for weighing institutional risk; yet avoidance, standing and the political question doctrine all institutionalize that weighing.
- A middle answer: prudence about *when* to decide is legitimate, prudence about *how* — bending the merits to survive — is not; the silence on the judgeships is what makes Stuart hard to place.

### Further drilling

- **Vary the objection.** Had a displaced 1801 judge sued for his salary, tenure and compensation would have been unavoidable. For Congress: tenure protects judges in offices that exist, and the power to establish includes the power to disestablish. Against: then good-behaviour tenure lasts only as long as Congress's forbearance.
- **Vary the practice.** Strip out the twelve years of circuit riding and the appointments question must be decided on its merits. The practice argument is load-bearing, not confirmatory.
- **How long must practice run?** Paterson's twelve years are short but founding-era, so they pass as evidence of original meaning; the land withdrawals in *Midwest Oil* ran eighty years but began long after. Duration or proximity to the founding?
- **Who decides?** Stuart treats a construction settled by early practice as binding on the Court, an early departmentalist note that returns in Class 13 with [[Cooper v. Aaron]] and [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]].
- **Memorable lines.** "[O]f recent date." "[A]n appendage to the cause." "This practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled."
