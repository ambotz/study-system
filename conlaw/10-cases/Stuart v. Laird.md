## Rule

Congress may reorganize the inferior federal courts and transfer a pending cause from one such court to another; and Supreme Court Justices may sit as circuit judges without separate commissions, because "practice and acquiescence" since 1789 "has indeed fixed the construction." The Court sustains the Jeffersonian reorganization without addressing whether Congress could abolish the judgeships of sitting life-tenured judges.

## Facts

- **Laird won a judgment against Stuart in a circuit court created by the Judiciary Act of 1801, and Stuart gave a forthcoming bond on property levied under the execution.** *The court that issued the execution no longer existed when the bond was enforced.*
- **After the Repeal Act abolished that court, a new circuit court under the April 1802 act entered judgment on the bond.** *Paterson treats this as "nothing more than the removal of the suit," which Congress may authorize.*
- **The April 1802 act expressly authorized the new court to proceed on causes from the old.** *Paterson grants that the objection "is true, if there be no statutable provision"; the statute supplies one.*
- **Supreme Court Justices, holding no circuit commissions, sat on the new circuit courts.** *The objection is answered by practice under the 1789 system, not by constitutional text.*
- **No displaced circuit judge was a party.** *The case never squarely presents whether abolishing the 1801 judgeships violated good-behaviour tenure, and the opinion does not mention it.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Paterson, J., for the Court; Cushing, J., absent)

- **Transfer of the cause.** "Congress have constitutional authority to establish from time to time such inferior tribunals as they may think proper; and to transfer a cause from one such tribunal to another." The Constitution contains "no words . . . to prohibit or restrain the exercise of legislative power" in transferring causes. The forthcoming bond "is an appendage to the cause," so it moves with the cause.
- **Circuit riding.** Justices may sit as circuit judges without distinct commissions. "[P]ractice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer." "[T]he question is at rest, and ought not now to be disturbed."
- Both grounds for reversing the judgment on the bond fail.

**Rejected**

- *Only the court that issued the execution could proceed on a bond given under it; once Congress abolished that court, no other court could enforce the bond.* True absent a statute, but Congress may create inferior tribunals and transfer causes among them, and § 9 of the April 1802 act did so.
- *Circuit judge is a distinct office; a Justice appointed to the Supreme Court has never been appointed to it, so he may not sit on a circuit court without a separate commission.* The objection "is of recent date." Practice since 1789 is "a contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature," and "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled."

## Context

Stuart was the case "that looked like it would be the big confrontation." The casebook's Background to Marbury frames the stakes: the Jeffersonians repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801, abolished sixteen life-tenured circuit judgeships, reimposed circuit riding, cancelled the Court's 1802 session and began impeaching Federalist judges; the casebook calls both the abolition and the reimposition "acts of dubious constitutionality." One week after [[Marbury v. Madison]], the Court upheld the Repeal Act. Marbury asserted judicial review in a case where it cost the political branches nothing; Stuart declined to use it where it would have.

The opinion's method is the argument from practice. It belongs with [[The Decision of 1789]], [[The Post Roads Debate]] and Hamilton's courts that "liquidate and fix" meaning in [[The Federalist No. 78]]; with Frankfurter's historical-gloss argument in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]; and with the modern historical-practice cases, [[NLRB v. Noel Canning]] and [[Zivotofsky v. Kerry]]. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].

On congressional control of the courts, Stuart is the first data point in the line the course follows through [[Ex parte McCardle]] and the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] (Class 15): Congress may create, abolish, restructure and redistribute the business of the inferior courts. The question the Court avoided, whether Congress may abolish a court to remove its judges, has never been squarely decided. On the appointments point, the Court later held that Congress may assign an officer additional duties germane to his office without a new appointment ([[Shoemaker v. United States]] (1893); [[Weiss v. United States]] (1994)), which gives Stuart's result a doctrinal footing its reasoning does not supply.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Review in the Supreme Court of a judgment entered in December 1802 by the circuit court for the fifth circuit, Virginia district, on a forthcoming bond given by Stuart and Charles L. Carter. Counsel assigned two reasons for reversal.
- The Court, decided one week after Marbury, rejected both.

### Facts

The Federalists' Judiciary Act of 1801 created new circuit courts and ended the Justices' duty to ride circuit. Laird won a judgment against Stuart in December 1801 in the court for the fourth circuit, eastern district of Virginia; execution issued, property was levied on, and Stuart and Carter gave a bond for its forthcoming and delivery. The Jeffersonian Congress then repealed the 1801 Act, abolishing that court and its judgeships, and an April 29, 1802 act "to amend the judicial system" reorganized the circuits and restored circuit riding. In December 1802 the court for the fifth circuit, sitting in Virginia, entered judgment on the bond against Stuart and Carter. Stuart argued that only the abolished court could proceed on the bond and that Justices sitting as circuit judges lacked valid commissions.

### Issue

- May a newly constituted inferior court proceed on a bond given in a cause pending in a court that Congress abolished?
- May Supreme Court Justices sit as circuit judges without commissions as circuit judges?

### Holding

- Yes. Congress may establish inferior tribunals and transfer causes between them; § 9 of the April 1802 act transferred the cause, and the bond is part of the cause.
- Yes. Practice and acquiescence since the organization of the judicial system in 1789 have fixed the construction that Justices may sit on circuit courts, and the question "ought not now to be disturbed."

### Rule

- Congress's power to "ordain and establish" inferior courts includes power to transfer pending causes from one inferior court to another; nothing in the Constitution restrains it.
- Long practice and acquiescence beginning at the founding is "a contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature" and can settle a constitutional question.
- Supreme Court Justices may perform circuit duties without separate appointments.

### Reasoning

**(Paterson, J., for the Court)**

- **Transfer.** Stuart's first objection is correct only "if there be no statutable provision to direct and authorize such proceeding." Congress may create inferior tribunals "from time to time" and move causes among them; "there are no words in the constitution to prohibit or restrain the exercise of legislative power." The 1802 act authorized the new court to proceed; the bond is "a component part of the proceedings."
- **Circuit riding.** The objection is new ("of recent date"). The Justices rode circuit under the 1789 Act from the beginning, with the acquiescence of all. That practice "has indeed fixed the construction," and "the question is at rest."
- **What the opinion does not say.** It does not discuss the Appointments Clause, good-behaviour tenure, the Compensation Clause, or the abolition of the 1801 judgeships. The Repeal Act is sustained by silence.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. Paterson wrote for the Court; Cushing was absent for ill health.

### Cold-call notes

- **What is the constitutional analysis? (Note 1).** Two steps. Transfer: text (Congress may "ordain and establish" inferior courts "from time to time") plus the absence of any restraining text. Circuit riding: practice alone, with no textual analysis. Be ready to say that the opinion never engages the strongest objection, that a Justice is appointed to one office and a circuit judgeship is another.
- **Capitulation or tactical retreat? (Note 2).** The case for capitulation: the Court, whose members had the most to gain from the end of circuit riding, sustained an act the casebook calls of "dubious constitutionality" while impeachments were under way, and did not even name the tenure question. The case for wisdom: a weak Court with "neither Force nor Will" (Hamilton) could not have enforced a judgment against Congress, and a confrontation might have cost it the power it had just claimed in Marbury. Whether institutional self-preservation is a legitimate consideration for a court is the real question; Marbury's rhetoric says the Court's duty is simply to "say what the law is."
- **Marbury and Stuart together.** Marbury: the Court declares the executive's conduct unlawful but disclaims power to remedy it, and uses judicial review only to strike down a grant of power to itself. Stuart: the Court upholds the legislature's attack on the judiciary. Read together, the Court asserted review in principle and avoided every order the political branches could resist. McConnell: "Marbury must be understood as the product of a defeated and demoralized Court."
- **Practice and acquiescence as a constitutional argument.** Stuart is the cleanest early example: practice beginning "with the organization of the judicial system" is "contemporary interpretation," so it counts as evidence of original meaning, not merely as tradition. Compare [[The Decision of 1789]] (early congressional practice on removal) and Frankfurter's test in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] (practice that is systematic, unbroken, long pursued and known to Congress). Twelve years of circuit riding is short but founding-era; the withdrawals in [[United States v. Midwest Oil|Midwest Oil]] ran eighty years but began long after the founding. Which matters more?
- **Vary the objection.** Had a displaced 1801 circuit judge sued for his salary, the Court would have faced the good-behaviour and Compensation Clause question directly; practice could not help, because there was no practice of abolishing judgeships. The argument for Congress: the tenure guarantee protects judges in offices that exist, and Congress's power to "ordain and establish" includes the power to disestablish. The argument against: if Congress can abolish a court to remove its judges, good-behaviour tenure is only as secure as Congress's forbearance.
- **Vary the practice.** Had Justices never ridden circuit before 1802, the Court would have had to decide the appointments question on its merits. The practice argument is doing all the work.
- **Who decides?** Stuart treats the political branches' long-settled construction as binding on the Court. That is an early instance of the departmentalist idea that the other branches' constitutional judgments count, the theme of Class 13 ([[Cooper v. Aaron]], [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]).
- **Memorable line.** "This practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled."
