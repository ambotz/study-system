## What it is

The casebook's last war-initiation materials (pp. 322–325): the text of the Authorization for Use of Military Force of September 18, 2001 (Pub. L. No. 107–40) with two notes, and a note titled "The War Powers Debate Continues: Syria and Soleimani," covering the Office of Legal Counsel's opinion on the April 2018 Syria airstrikes, the January 2020 Soleimani strike and the vetoed Iran resolution, and President Biden's February 2021 Syria strike. The 4th edition stops in early 2021; the syllabus cites the 5th edition, which may carry this note forward.

## What to notice

- **The AUMF's grant.** The President "is authorized to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed or aided the terrorist attacks that occurred on September 11, 2001, or harbored such organizations or persons, in order to prevent any future acts of international terrorism."
  - The enemy is defined by connection to September 11, not by name. No nation is named.
  - "He determines" gives the President the choice of enemy.
  - No time limit and no geographic limit.
- **The AUMF's preamble concedes the executive's position.** "Whereas, the President has authority under the Constitution to take action to deter and prevent acts of international terrorism against the United States." Congress in 1973 wrote § 2(c) of the WPR to confine that authority to attack and emergency; Congress in 2001 recited a broader constitutional power in the President. Note 4 to the Nixon veto asks whether the AUMF "repudiate[s] (or revise[s])" Congress's view. The preamble does, in part; § 2(b) does not.
- **The AUMF complies with the WPR.** § 2(b)(1) declares, "[c]onsistent with section 8(a)(1) of the War Powers Resolution," that it is "specific statutory authorization"; § 2(b)(2) says nothing in it "supercedes any requirement of the War Powers Resolution." The WPR's clear- statement rule worked as designed: Congress used the magic words.
- **Is the AUMF a declaration of war (Note 1)?** Functionally, as to initiation, yes: it is Congress's decision to use force, which is what the Declare War Clause requires (see [[Declarations of War]]). It differs in scope (no named enemy), duration (none stated) and legal form (no formal state of war). The casebook's sub-questions:
  - Necessary to fight al Qaeda? After an attack on the United States, the President could respond on his own under [[The Prize Cases]]; the AUMF matters for sustained war beyond the immediate response.
  - Sufficient for Iraq? No; Iraq had no link to September 11, and the Iraq War "was supported by a later, specific authorization."
  - Nations that "harbor" or fail to expel terrorists, and persons only suspected? "Harbored" reaches the first only if linked to September 11 actors; the statute's text does not reach future terrorist groups with no such link, so action against them needs a new statute, a strained reading of "aided," or Article II.
- **Can Congress delegate the war power (Note 2)?** "He determines" lets the President pick the enemy. Under the intelligible-principle test the principle is clear (the September 11 perpetrators and harborers). Under Gorsuch's [[Gundy v. United States|Gundy]] framework, a delegation in a field that overlaps executive power (foreign affairs, command) is one of his permissible categories. Either way the delegation survives; the harder question is whether the Declare War Clause, unlike ordinary legislative power, requires Congress itself to name the enemy. Tonkin's "as the President determines" raised the same question in 1964.
- **The OLC theory (Syria, April 2018).** The President, "as Commander in Chief and Chief Executive, has the constitutional authority to deploy the military to protect American persons and interests without seeking prior authorization from Congress." OLC concedes that the Declare War Clause reserves to Congress "to decide whether to commit the Nation to a sustained, full-scale conflict with another Nation," but claims for the President "more limited hostilities to advance the Nation's interests."
- **OLC's two-part test.** (1) "the President could reasonably determine that the action serves important national interests"; (2) "the 'anticipated nature, scope and duration'" would not "rise to the level of a war under the Constitution" (the test comes from an earlier memo on Libya under President Obama).
  - Interests cited: regional stability, a humanitarian crisis, deterrence of chemical weapons.
  - Limits cited: no ground troops, no aircraft in Syrian airspace, a specific target, a few-hours operation, low risk of retaliation.
- **OLC's history.** "We do not write on a blank slate"; "dozens of occasions over the course of 230 years, quite literally running from the halls of Montezuma to the shores of Tripoli and beyond." Earlier opinions cover Cambodia, Somalia (1992), Bosnia (1993–95), Haiti (1994 and
  2004) and Libya (2011). This is Frankfurter's historical-gloss argument from [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] made by the executive about itself.
- **Has practice liquidated the question?** The strongest answer for OLC: two centuries of limited unilateral uses of force, with Congress funding the military and rarely objecting, meet the test of a practice long pursued to the knowledge of Congress. The answer against:
  - Frankfurter's gloss requires a practice Congress has never questioned, and Congress questioned this one in the WPR itself, passed over a veto.
  - Liquidation settles ambiguous text; OLC's own concession that "declare War" reserves the decision for sustained conflict shows the text is not ambiguous about initiation.
  - Many of OLC's precedents are opinions of OLC. A branch's opinions of its own power are evidence of its claims, not of acquiescence by the other branch.
  - The best synthesis: practice has plausibly liquidated a narrow category of brief, limited strikes and rescues; it has not liquidated unilateral initiation of sustained hostilities.
- **Bas v. Tingy's "imperfect war."** OLC quotes [[Bas v. Tingy]] (1800) to distinguish "a declared war" from an "imperfect war" "limited as to places, person and things." In Bas the imperfect war with France was authorized by Congress; it shows that Congress may authorize limited war, not that the President may begin one. OLC concedes the first ("Congress may authorize the use of force in such conflicts") and uses the category for the second.
- **Soleimani (January 2020).** A drone strike killing the commander of the Quds Force (and an Iraqi military commander). Two justifications: the same Article II factors, "though the possibility of Iranian retaliation seemed more plausible and ominous"; and the 2002 Iraq AUMF, on the ground that Soleimani participated in attacks in Iraq. The statutory route "would sidestep the constitutional question" by moving the case to category one.
- **The vetoed Iran resolution (May 2020).** Congress passed a resolution under the WPR forbidding force against Iran; because of [[INS v. Chadha]], it was presented to the President, and he vetoed it as "unnecessary and dangerous" and in "violation of Article II." The casebook calls it a concurrent resolution; the veto message quoted calls it a "joint resolution," which is the form that can be presented. The episode shows the practical effect of Chadha on § 5(c): Congress can stop a war only by a two-thirds majority in each House.
- **Biden (February 2021).** A strike in Syria against "Iran-supported non-state militia groups," justified by self-defense where the host state "is unwilling or unable to prevent the use of its territory," citing Article II and the U.N. Charter "but not any congressional authority." Bipartisan continuity is the point: the claim does not depend on the party of the President.
- **The casebook's closing questions.**
  - What clause confers a "national interest" power to strike? Not the Commander in Chief Clause on the Convention's reading, which gives command and repulsion, not initiation. The Vesting Clause argument is the only candidate, and it depends on the prerogative reading Jackson rejected in Youngstown.
  - Is the absence of ground troops controlling? Nothing in "war" as the Convention used it turns on the means; a naval blockade was an act of war in [[The Prize Cases]].
  - Is expected duration controlling? A test based on "anticipated" duration makes the constitutional line depend on the President's prediction, and "[a] brief and tidy operation can grow into something big (as in the case of the Vietnam War)."
  - Goldsmith: "That is our system: One person decides." Accurate as description; its legitimacy is the liquidation question above.
- **Hypotheticals under the same OLC test.**
  - A one-night strike on chemical-weapons sites, no ground troops, low retaliation risk: within OLC's test (Syria 2018).
  - A strike killing a senior official of a foreign state's armed forces, with high retaliation risk: the second OLC factor weakens because escalation is foreseeable (Soleimani).
  - A months-long air campaign: OLC's own reservation that "prolonged and substantial engagements" may require authorization is triggered, and the WPR 60-day clock runs.
  - A ground invasion to remove a government: OLC's reasoning does not reach it; Panama 1989 is the executive's precedent, and it is contested.
- **The Youngstown frame throughout.** Every episode here is external force, where Jackson gave the President his widest latitude. The limit comes from the Declare War Clause, not from the inward/outward line: outward force is where the President is strongest to command, but the Convention placed the decision to begin in Congress.
- **After the casebook (verified).** In December 2025 the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2026 repealed the 1991 Gulf War and 2002 Iraq AUMFs, removing the statutory basis claimed for the Soleimani strike. In June 2025 the United States struck Iranian nuclear facilities without congressional authorization, and on June 27, 2025 the Senate rejected a war powers resolution directed at further hostilities with Iran. Check the 5th edition and the syllabus for what Baude assigns.
