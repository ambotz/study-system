## Rule

Once Congress acts in a way that is "legislative in its character and effect," it must follow Article I, § 7: passage by both Houses and presentment to the President. A one-House veto of an executive decision made under delegated authority is legislation without bicameralism or presentment, so it is void, and with it every legislative veto of the same design.

## Facts

- **The House acted alone.** The resolution "was not submitted to the Senate or presented to the President." *This is the defect the holding turns on; passed by both Houses and signed, it would have been an ordinary private law.*
- **The House overrode a decision already made under delegated authority.** An immigration judge had suspended Chadha's deportation, subject to a veto by either House under § 244(c)(2). *Neither House could otherwise compel the Attorney General to deport; the veto did what only legislation could.*
- **The veto targeted six named individuals and "was passed without debate or recorded vote."** *Irrelevant to the majority; decisive for Powell, who reads it as adjudication.*
- **Chadha conceded he was deportable.** *So severability mattered: an inseverable veto would have taken his suspension down with it.*
- **The Executive Branch agreed with Chadha; the Senate and House defended the statute.** *Congress's participation supplied the adverseness (Note 2).*
- **The device was everywhere: 295 veto-type procedures in 196 statutes since 1932.** *For the majority this sharpens the inquiry; for White it proves the stakes.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (7–2; Burger, C.J., for the Court; Powell, J., concurring in the judgment; White, J.,
and Rehnquist, J., dissenting)

- The Court has jurisdiction; § 244(c)(2) is severable from the rest of the Act; and the case is not a nonjusticiable political question.
- Whether an action of either House is subject to Article I, § 7 "depends not on their form but upon 'whether they contain matter which is properly to be regarded as legislative in its character and effect.'"
- The House's action was "essentially legislative in purpose and effect": it "had the purpose and effect of altering the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons, including the Attorney General, Executive Branch officials and Chadha, all outside the Legislative Branch."
- The Constitution names only four occasions on which one House may act alone with the force of law: House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation, Senate treaty ratification. Where the Framers meant one House to act alone "they did so in explicit, unambiguous terms."
- The legislative power must be exercised "in accord with a single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure." "Amendment and repeal of statutes, no less than enactment, must conform with Art. I."
- § 244(c)(2) is unconstitutional, and the Ninth Circuit's judgment is affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *The legislative veto is an important, even indispensable, political invention that lets Congress delegate broadly while keeping delegated power accountable.* Burger concedes the utility and holds it beside the point: "policy arguments supporting even useful 'political inventions' are subject to the demands of the Constitution," and "the Framers ranked other values higher than efficiency."
- *Every change in the legal status quo under § 244 still has the assent of all three actors: the Attorney General recommends, and both Houses approve by declining to veto (White).* The majority answers by characterization: the veto is itself legislative action by one House, and legislative action needs both Houses and the President, whatever the statute that authorized it.
- *Congress may delegate lawmaking to agencies that act without bicameralism or presentment, so it may reserve a smaller check for itself (White).* The majority's answer is the presumption: "When the Executive acts, he presumptively acts in an executive or administrative capacity," and when one House acts, it acts in its legislative sphere.
- *The case should turn on the narrower ground that the House adjudicated Chadha's individual case (Powell).* The majority does not take the narrow route; its presentment rationale reaches every legislative veto.
- *Congress's decision to use the device in hundreds of statutes over fifty years deserves deference (White, Powell).* "[O]ur inquiry is sharpened rather than blunted by the fact that congressional veto provisions are appearing with increasing frequency." Practice cannot excuse noncompliance, citing [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]].

## Context

Chadha is the leading formalist separation-of-powers case of the modern era, and it is the direct ancestor of [[Clinton v. City of New York]]: Stevens quotes Chadha's "finely wrought" procedure and its line that repeal "no less than enactment, must conform with Art. I," then turns both against the President. Chadha protected the President's place in lawmaking from Congress; Clinton protects Congress's place from the President. The two together make Article I, § 7 an exclusive procedure that neither political branch can alter by statute, even with the other's consent.

The case is also a nondelegation case in disguise. The legislative veto grew up as Congress's answer to its own broad delegations, the problem of [[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]] and [[Gundy v. United States]]. White's dissent says it plainly: without the veto, Congress faces a "Hobson's choice" between writing impossibly specific laws and abdicating to agencies. Chadha took away the check while leaving the delegation in place. Gorsuch's Gundy dissent lists Chadha among the structural cases through which the Court polices delegation under other names, and Chadha itself warns of "[t]he hydraulic pressure inherent within each of the separate Branches to exceed the outer limits of its power."

Burger's citation of [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] is to Black's formalism: the Constitution's cumbersome procedures cannot be avoided "either by the Congress or by the President." White answers with Jackson's concurrence and its flexibility. Chadha replays the Black–Jackson divide, and this time the formalist side writes the majority.

Powell's concurrence connects forward to [[United States v. Lovett]] and the [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3|Bill of Attainder Clause]]: both concern Congress deciding the case of named individuals. His reliance on [[Buckley v. Valeo]] links to the appointments material in Class 6.

**Statutory response.** Congress could no longer reserve a one-House or two-House veto, but it could still reserve review through a joint resolution of disapproval, passed by both Houses and presented to the President. That is the design of the Congressional Review Act (1996) and of the "disapproval bill" in the Line Item Veto Act struck down in [[Clinton v. City of New York]]. The catch is that the President can veto the disapproval, so a determined President can hold an agency's action in place unless two-thirds of each House object.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Chadha challenged § 244(c)(2) in his reopened deportation proceedings; after a deportation order he petitioned the Ninth Circuit, where the INS agreed the provision was unconstitutional and the Senate and House were invited to file briefs. The Ninth Circuit held the provision violated the separation of powers and directed the Attorney General to cease efforts to deport Chadha on the basis of the House resolution.
- The Supreme Court affirmed after resolving jurisdiction, severability and justiciability in Chadha's favor (summarized by the editors). Rehnquist's dissent, joined by White, is omitted.

### Facts

Chadha, an East Indian born in Kenya who held a British passport, was lawfully admitted on a nonimmigrant student visa in 1966; the visa expired in 1972. The INS ordered him to show cause why he should not be deported for having "remained in the United States for a longer time than permitted." He applied for suspension of deportation under § 244(a)(1), and the immigration judge granted it. In December 1975 Representative Eilberg, chairman of the House Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration, introduced a resolution opposing permanent residence for six aliens, including Chadha, which the House passed. The immigration judge reopened the proceedings to carry out the House's order. Chadha moved to terminate on the ground that § 244(c)(2) is unconstitutional, and a deportation order followed.

### Issue

- Does the Court have jurisdiction, is § 244(c)(2) severable from the rest of the Act, and is the challenge justiciable?
- Does the one-House veto in § 244(c)(2) violate the Constitution?

### Holding

- Yes to all three. Severability was essential: it let Chadha keep the suspension while losing the veto.
- Yes. The House's resolution was an exercise of legislative power, because it altered the legal rights and duties of persons outside the Legislative Branch; it fell within none of the four express exceptions for one-House action; so it required passage by both Houses and presentment.

### Rule

- Action by either House is subject to Article I, § 7 when it is "legislative in its character and effect," which means it alters "the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons ... outside the Legislative Branch."
- One House may act alone with the force of law only where the Constitution expressly says so: impeachment, impeachment trials, confirmations and treaties.
- Enactment, amendment and repeal of law must all follow bicameralism and presentment.
- Efficiency and long practice do not save a procedure the Constitution forbids.

### Reasoning

**(Burger, C.J., for the Court)**

- Text first. Art. I, § 1 vests legislative powers in "a Congress ... which shall consist of a Senate and House"; § 7, cl. 2 requires presentment of every bill; § 7, cl. 3 extends presentment to "Every Order, Resolution, or Vote" requiring both Houses' concurrence.
- History of presentment. Madison worried at the Convention that presentment could be evaded "by the simple expedient of calling a proposed law a 'resolution' or 'vote,'" and clause 3 was added to stop it. Hamilton in Federalist 73: the veto lets the President "defend himself" and guards against "bad laws through haste, inadvertence, or design."
- History of bicameralism. Wilson: "In a single house there is no check, but the inadequate one, of the virtue & good sense of those who compose it." Story on public bodies "impatient, irritable, and impetuous." Federalist 51 on dividing the legislature.
- The branches are "not 'hermetically' sealed" but are "functionally identifiable," and each branch acting is presumed to exercise its own power.
- The legislative character of the veto is "confirmed by the character of the congressional action it supplants": without § 244(c)(2), overriding the Attorney General would have required legislation. Nor is the veto a permissible amendment or repeal, since those must also follow Article I.
- The four express one-House powers show that the Framers spoke clearly when they meant one House to act alone.
- The procedures are "clumsy, inefficient, even unworkable," but "those hard choices were consciously made by men who had lived under a form of government that permitted arbitrary governmental acts to go unchecked."
- "With all the obvious flaws of delay, untidiness, and potential for abuse, we have not yet found a better way to preserve freedom than by making the exercise of power subject to the carefully crafted restraints spelled out in the Constitution."

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Powell, J., concurring in the judgment)**

- "The breadth of this holding gives one pause." Respect for Congress as a coordinate branch counsels a holding "no more extensive than necessary."
- The narrower ground: "When Congress finds that a particular person does not satisfy the statutory criteria for permanent residence in this country it has assumed a judicial function."
- "On its face, the House's action appears clearly adjudicatory. The House did not enact a general rule." Even mere review of INS findings is a function "ordinarily entrusted to the federal courts."
- Congress adjudicating is unchecked power. It is bound by no substantive rules and gives none of the procedural safeguards of a court or agency, such as counsel and an impartial tribunal. "Congress is most accountable politically when it prescribes rules of general applicability"; individual rights decided by Congress are subject to "the tyranny of a shifting majority."
- Marshall in [[Fletcher v. Peck]]: "It is the peculiar province of the legislature to prescribe general rules for the government of society; the application of those rules would seem to be the duty of other departments."

**(White, J., dissenting)**

- The Court "sounds the death knell for nearly 200 other statutory provisions" and "strikes down in one fell swoop provisions in more laws enacted by Congress than the Court has cumulatively invalidated in its history."
- The veto is "an important if not indispensable political invention." Without it Congress faces a "Hobson's choice": write laws of impossible specificity, or "abdicate its law-making function to the Executive Branch and independent agencies."
- The veto has been a shield, not a sword: "a means of defense, a reservation of ultimate authority." Presidents have "more often agreed to legislative review as the price for a broad delegation of authority."
- The question is hard: "The Constitution does not directly authorize or prohibit the legislative veto." It is "incomprehensible" that Congress, bound by oath, would have enacted the device in nearly 200 laws over 50 years if it so flagrantly violated Article I.
- Clause 3's purpose was to stop Congress from circumventing presentment "in the making of new legislation," and the Necessary and Proper Clause, read through [[McCulloch v. Maryland]], gives Congress wide choice of means.
- Delegation already means that "Art. I does not require all action with the effect of legislation to be passed as a law." Agencies are "a veritable fourth branch" (Jackson in FTC v. Ruberoid). "If Congress may delegate lawmaking power to independent and Executive agencies, it is most difficult to understand Art. I as prohibiting Congress from also reserving a check on legislative power for itself."
- Bicameralism and presentment are satisfied in substance: the Attorney General recommends, both Houses acquiesce, so "a change in the legal status quo—the deportability of the alien—is consummated only with the approval of each of the three relevant actors."
- The separation of powers is "a history of accommodation and practicality," quoting [[Buckley v. Valeo|Buckley]] against "a hermetic sealing off of the three branches."
- He reserves a harder case: "A legislative check on an inherently executive function, for example, that of initiating prosecutions, poses an entirely different question."

### Cold-call notes

- **What branch's power is the House exercising? (Note 1)** The majority reasons by elimination: not executive, because the President holds that; not judicial, because the courts hold that; so legislative, and legislative power must follow § 7. Be ready to press the step: Congress has in effect delegated the suspension power to a part of itself. If it can delegate that power to the INS, why not to one House? The majority's only answer is the presumption that an actor exercises its own branch's power. When the Attorney General acts, it is execution; when a House acts, it is legislation.
- **Is the majority's test formalist?** The procedure is formalist: once action is legislative, § 7 is exclusive and no functional excuse works. But the classification is functional: action is legislative by its "character and effect," not its form. By that test the Attorney General's suspension also altered Chadha's legal rights. The presumption, not the effects test, is what keeps agency action outside § 7.
- **Does clause 3 even reach a one-House resolution?** By its terms clause 3 covers votes "to which the Concurrence of the Senate and House ... may be necessary." A one-House veto requires no concurrence. The textual argument therefore depends on first classifying the veto as legislation under § 1 and clause 2. White's reading of the Madison history, that clause 3 guards only against disguised "new legislation," is a fair original-meaning argument.
- **Two-House veto.** Both Houses pass the resolution; it is not presented. Still void: Chadha's reasoning rests on presentment as well as bicameralism, and clause 3 by its terms requires presentment of concurrent resolutions. Committee veto: worse still.
- **Joint resolution of disapproval, presented to the President.** Valid. It is an ordinary law. That is the Congressional Review Act model and the Line Item Veto Act's "disapproval bill."
- **Report-and-wait.** A statute delays an agency rule for 60 days so Congress can legislate if it wishes. Valid under Chadha: nothing changes the law except a later statute passed under § 7.
- **Note 3's hypothetical.** Proposals are deemed "passed" after 30 days unless a member forces a vote and a majority of each House defeats them; "passed" proposals are presented to the President. Formally it satisfies presentment, and each House's § 5 rules power arguably lets it define what counts as passage. The discomfort is that the difference from Chadha is thin: White's argument was exactly that silence in both Houses is approval. The distinguishing fact is that in the hypothetical the final product goes to the President, and one House's affirmative vote alone never changes anyone's rights.
- **Where does Powell's rule leave the other vetoes?** Intact. A one-House veto of a general agency rule is not adjudication and would survive his rationale. Powell's opinion is the true narrow alternative; White's dissent says the Court should have taken it.
- **Could Congress deport Chadha by private bill passed by both Houses and signed?** The majority says the result "could have been achieved, if at all, only by legislation requiring deportation." The "if at all" hedges. Powell's reasoning, and the Bill of Attainder Clause in [[United States v. Lovett]], suggest a statute singling out named persons for adverse treatment raises its own problem.
- **Why is the case in court at all? (Note 2)** The Executive agreed with Chadha. The Houses defended the statute. Be ready to say whether the Department of Justice should defend every act of Congress, and whether a President who thinks a law unconstitutional should also decline to enforce it. Tie to [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] and [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]].
- **Chadha and the delegation cases.** The veto was a substitute for a nondelegation doctrine the Court had stopped enforcing after [[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]]. Striking the veto while upholding broad delegations leaves the agency with more power and Congress with less. On Gorsuch's view in [[Gundy v. United States|Gundy]], the fix is at the delegation end; on White's view, the veto was the fix.
- **Chadha and the Post Roads Debate.** Hartley proposed a sunset clause as a check on delegating the post roads to the President. A sunset written into a statute is valid after Chadha; a reserved veto is not. The difference is that a sunset operates by the terms of a law that went through § 7. See [[The Post Roads Debate]].
- **Black, Jackson, Burger, White.** Burger cites [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] for the rule that constitutional procedures cannot be avoided "either by the Congress or by the President," which is Black's formalism. White invokes Jackson's concurrence. Know which side the later cases took: [[Clinton v. City of New York|Clinton]] follows Burger.
- **What is a "bill"? (Note 4)** Congress bundles appropriations into one enormous bill, leaving the President an all-or-nothing veto. Nothing in the text limits bundling, and each House's § 5 rules power arguably lets Congress define the unit. The President cannot veto a provision and sign the rest; that is the question [[Clinton v. City of New York|Clinton]] answers.
- **Three possible objections to the legislative veto.** The editors list them: it interferes with the President's executive power once authority is conferred; it is lawmaking that skips Article I, § 7; or it lets one House exercise judicial power. The majority takes the second; Powell's concurrence takes the third.
- **Memorable lines.** "a single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure"; "the Framers ranked other values higher than efficiency"; "[t]he hydraulic pressure inherent within each of the separate Branches"; White's "Hobson's choice" and "death knell"; Powell's "tyranny of a shifting majority."
