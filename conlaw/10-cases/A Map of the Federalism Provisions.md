## What it is

The casebook's opener to Chapter 3 (Federalism), pp. 461–462: a short essay on why the framers built a federal republic, followed by "A Map of the Constitution's Federalism Provisions," which sorts the federalism provisions into three structural features. It is assigned to frame the whole federalism unit, starting with the Bank debate and [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].

## What to notice

- **The problem federalism solves is size.** Republics had been small, and "As republics grew, they tended to become empires." At the Revolution "many assumed it was impossible to sustain an extended republic," but the colonies had "more than two million people and extended along the length of the Atlantic coast." [[The Federalist No. 10]] is the framers' answer to that assumption.
- **Federalism was a compromise, not a theory worked out in advance.** The casebook calls it "the child of political necessity": "States were a given. Yet the need for a strong national government was also clear."
- **There is no single clause.** "The Constitution has no “Federalism Clause.” Instead, federalism is the term for a pattern of structural features in the Constitution." Every federalism argument in the unit is therefore structural or built from several clauses together, which is why [[Types of Constitutional Argument|structural argument]] dominates it.
- **Feature one: the states persist as states.** They are "not merely subordinate administrative districts of the national government." Article IV governs the states' relations with the nation and with one another. This is the root of the anti-commandeering line in [[Printz v. United States]].
- **Feature two: enumeration limits national power.** National powers are "specifically enumerated, and thus limited," and the rest are "reserved" to the states. The key provisions: Art. I, § 8 (grants), Art. I, § 10 (limits on the states), Art. VI (national law prevails in a conflict), and the Tenth Amendment, which "confirms the reserved authority of the states." The Reconstruction Amendments add national power. The word "confirms" matters: the casebook reads the Tenth Amendment as declaratory, not as an independent limit, which is where [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] and [[United States v. Darby|Darby]] come out.
- **Feature three: the states are built into the national government.** Equal Senate representation, House seats drawn along state lines with at least one per state, and a state-chosen Electoral College sized by House plus Senate seats. These are the "political safeguards" on which [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]] later rests judicial abstention.
- **The Seventeenth Amendment (1913) removed the most important safeguard.** Roger Sherman, 1789: senators "dependent on them for re-election, will be vigilant in supporting their rights against infringement by the legislative or executive of the United States." Direct election ended state legislatures' control of reelection and their practice of "instruction." The casebook's verdict: "it removed what the framers had expected would be a check on the exercise of national power."
- **What the Seventeenth Amendment does to Garcia's premise.** If the states' protection is supposed to come from the political process, and the Seventeenth Amendment cut the states' institutional hold on the Senate, the political-safeguards argument is weaker in 1985 than it was in 1789. The counter: the amendment was itself a democratic choice, and a court restoring the lost check would be undoing it. Be ready to take either side.
- **Enumeration versus structure as the real protection.** The map lists two different kinds of safeguard: legal limits a court can enforce (enumeration, reserved powers) and political ones it cannot (Senate, House, Electoral College). The whole unit swings between them: [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] and [[Wickard v. Filburn|Wickard]] lean on politics, while [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]], [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius|NFIB]] revive judicial enforcement.
