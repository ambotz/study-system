## Rule

Now repudiated: no person of African descent whose ancestors were enslaved could be a "citizen" of the United States or of a State under Article III, and Congress could not exclude slavery from a federal territory because the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause protected slaveholders' property there. What survives is the case's lesson about judicial power: the Court reached out, after holding that it had no jurisdiction, to void an act of Congress on the most divisive question of the age, and the political branches, the country, and finally Article V (the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments) answered it.

## Facts

- **Dr. Emerson, an army surgeon, held Dred Scott as a slave for about two years in the free State of Illinois.** *Taney and Nelson send this claim to Missouri law under [[Strader v. Graham]].*
- **Emerson then held Scott at Fort Snelling, where the Missouri Compromise prohibited slavery, and consented to his marriage there.** *Puts the Compromise's validity in issue; Curtis treats the consent as abandonment of the master's rights.*
- **Scott returned to Missouri, whose Supreme Court in [[Scott v. Emerson]] (1852) overruled its own precedents and held him still a slave.** *Taney and Nelson follow it; McLean and Curtis do not.*
- **Scott sued Sanford, a New York citizen, in federal court as a citizen of Missouri.** *Diversity jurisdiction turned on whether a free Black man could be a state citizen.*
- **Sanford's plea in abatement rested only on Scott's African descent and his ancestors' enslavement.** *Curtis: "If any such person can be a citizen, this plaintiff has the right to the judgment of the court that he is so."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (7–2; Taney, C.J., for the Court; Wayne, Nelson, Grier, Daniel, Campbell and Catron, JJ., each writing separately; McLean and Curtis, JJ., dissenting)

- **Citizenship and jurisdiction.** Descendants of imported slaves, "whether emancipated or not," were "not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word “citizens” in the Constitution." A State may make a person its own citizen but cannot "introduce a new member into the political community created by the Constitution of the United States." Scott was therefore "not a citizen of Missouri within the meaning of the Constitution," and the Circuit Court had no jurisdiction.
- **Reaching the merits.** That the parties would fare about the same under a dismissal or a judgment for the defendant "would not justify this court in sanctioning an error in the judgment which is patent on the record," so the Court examined whether the facts entitled Scott to freedom.
- **The Missouri Compromise.** The Territories Clause was "a special provision for a known and particular territory, and to meet a present emergency, and nothing more." Congress's power over later-acquired territory is implied from the power to admit new States, and it is bounded by the Bill of Rights. A law depriving a citizen of property "merely because he came himself or brought his property into a particular Territory" "could hardly be dignified with the name of due process of law." Because "the right of property in a slave is distinctly and expressly affirmed in the Constitution," the prohibition "is not warranted by the Constitution, and is therefore void."
- **Illinois residence.** Under [[Strader v. Graham]], Scott's status on return "depended on the laws of Missouri, and not of Illinois," and the Missouri Supreme Court had settled that he was a slave.
- **Disposition.** The Circuit Court's judgment for Sanford is reversed, with "a mandate issued, directing the suit to be dismissed for want of jurisdiction."

**Rejected**

- *Free Black men were citizens, and voters, in several States when the Constitution was adopted; the Constitution made citizens of the States citizens of the United States; so their descendants born free are citizens too.* Taney concedes the middle premise ("every person, and every class and description of persons, who were at the time of the adoption of the Constitution recognized as citizens in the several States, became also citizens of this new political body") and denies the first as a matter of history: the class was "not even in the minds of the framers." Curtis shows the history is false.
- *The Declaration's "all men are created equal" includes everyone.* Taney admits the words "would seem to embrace the whole human family, and if they were used in a similar instrument at this day would be so understood," but holds that the framers' conduct shows they did not mean them that way.
- *A Court that has found no jurisdiction must stop.* Taney: the jurisdictional error was "patent on the record," and an uncorrected error "might be drawn into precedent." The Court's final judgment treats the merits as a further reason for the jurisdictional result: the Circuit Court "had no jurisdiction, when, by the laws of the State, the plaintiff was a slave, and not a citizen."
- *The Territories Clause gives Congress plenary power over all federal territory, and Congress has used it to ban slavery since the Northwest Ordinance.* Taney: the clause speaks of "the" territory, pairs it with other "property," and uses "needful rules and regulations," which "are not the words usually employed by statesmen in giving supreme power of legislation."
- *Slave property is different, because slavery exists only by positive local law.* Taney: "there is no law of nations standing between the people of the United States and their Government," and no tribunal may "draw such a distinction" when the Constitution itself recognizes the property.
- *Scott became free in Illinois and did not revert on his return.* Taney and Nelson: no State's law operates "extra-territorially," and Missouri decides for itself whether to give Illinois law effect.
- *Scott may have this Court review the Missouri judgment by suing afresh in federal court.* Taney: a direct writ of error from the Missouri court would have been dismissed under § 25 of the Judiciary Act, and "[i]t would ill become this court to sanction such an attempt to evade the law, or to exercise an appellate power in this circuitous way."

## Context

Dred Scott sits in Part I.C (Judicial Power), right after [[Marbury v. Madison]] and [[Stuart v. Laird]], and it is the second case in which the Court held an act of Congress unconstitutional. Marbury used judicial review to decline a jurisdiction Congress had granted; Dred Scott used it, on a record where the Court had just held it lacked jurisdiction, to void a thirty-seven-year-old compromise between the sections, one Congress had already repealed in the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, so the ruling mattered only as a declaration about Congress's power going forward. The pairing is the point: the same power Hamilton defended in [[The Federalist No. 78]] as belonging to the least dangerous branch is the power [[Brutus No. 11]] feared would let judges reshape the Constitution beyond correction.

Class 13 is the response. Lincoln's [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates|debates with Douglas]] (casebook p. 397) develop the departmentalist answer: the judgment binds the parties, but the political branches need not treat the Court's reasoning as a settled rule of political action. [[Cooper v. Aaron]] (1958) states the opposite, judicial-supremacist position, and [[Ex parte Merryman]] puts Taney himself on the other side of a confrontation with Lincoln. The casebook calls the reaction "the strongest and most sustained backlash against the authority of the Supreme Court’s decisions in our nation’s history."

The Article V response came after the war. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, and the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause made everyone born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction a citizen of the United States and of the State of residence. That text adopts Curtis's birthright principle and reverses Taney's ordering, in which state citizenship could never generate national citizenship. [[United States v. Wong Kim Ark]] (1898) later read the clause to confirm birthright citizenship. Taney had himself pointed to the remedy: "If any of its provisions are deemed unjust, there is a mode prescribed in the instrument itself by which it may be amended."

Within Article IV, the case follows [[Prigg v. Pennsylvania]] and the comity cases ([[Commonwealth v. Aves]], [[Somerset v. Stewart|Somersett's case]]) and precedes [[Lemmon v. The People]] (not assigned), the New York case Lincoln feared could become a "Second Dred Scott" nationalizing slavery. Taney's due process holding is the first use of substantive due process to void a federal statute, a lineage later critics of [[Lochner v. New York]] and its successors invoke. His view that territory is acquired "to become a State, and not to be held as a colony" was set aside for overseas possessions in the [[Downes v. Bidwell|Insular Cases]]. On acquisition itself, Jefferson's doubts about the Louisiana Purchase (casebook pp. 724–725) frame the enumerated-powers question that Taney answers through the New States Clause.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Scott sued Sanford in the U.S. Circuit Court for the District of Missouri to establish the freedom of himself and his family. Sanford pleaded in abatement that Scott was not a citizen of Missouri; the Circuit Court rejected the plea, took jurisdiction, and gave judgment for Sanford on the agreed facts. Scott brought a writ of error.
- The Supreme Court heard the case twice (argument and reargument, 1856) and decided it in March 1857: judgment reversed and the case remanded with directions to dismiss for want of jurisdiction.

### Facts

Dred Scott was enslaved by Dr. John Emerson, an army surgeon, who in 1834 took him from Missouri to Rock Island, Illinois, and then to Fort Snelling in present-day Minnesota, north of the Missouri Compromise line. There Scott married Harriet with Emerson's consent; their daughter Eliza was born on the Mississippi north of Missouri, and Lizzie later at Jefferson Barracks in Missouri. The family returned to Missouri in 1838. Scott first sued Emerson's widow in Missouri state court and won at trial, but the Missouri Supreme Court reversed, abandoning its earlier comity decisions. Scott was then apparently sold or transferred to Mrs. Emerson's brother, John Sanford of New York (misspelled "Sandford" in the reports), and Scott's lawyers filed a new suit in federal court invoking diversity jurisdiction, with the state case held open to await the result.

### Issue

- Can a free person of African descent, whose ancestors were brought to the country and sold as slaves, be a "citizen" of a State for purposes of Article III diversity jurisdiction?
- Having found no jurisdiction, may the Court go on to examine whether the facts entitled Scott to his freedom?
- Did Scott's residence at Fort Snelling make him free, which turns on whether Congress had power to prohibit slavery in the territory by the Missouri Compromise?
- Did Scott's residence in Illinois make him free on his return to Missouri?

### Holding

- No. Neither imported slaves nor their descendants, "whether emancipated or not," were part of "the people" or "citizens" in 1787, and a State cannot confer national citizenship, so Scott could not sue in diversity. Turned on Taney's reading of founding-era opinion, the Declaration, the Articles of Confederation, and the Slave Trade and Fugitive Slave Clauses.
- Yes, on Taney's view: an error "patent on the record" may be corrected so that it is not "drawn into precedent." Turned on treating the whole record as open on a writ of error; Curtis says the question was not "a fit subject to be thus reached."
- No. The Missouri Compromise's prohibition was void. Turned on confining the Territories Clause to the 1787 territory and on reading the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause to protect the right to carry slave property into any territory.
- No. Scott's status on return depended on Missouri law, as the Missouri Supreme Court had settled it. Turned on [[Strader v. Graham]] and the absence of any extraterritorial force in Illinois law.

### Rule

- "The words “people of the United States” and “citizens” are synonymous terms, and mean the same thing" (Taney), overruled by the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause.
- National citizenship is fixed by the Constitution and by Congress's naturalization power, which "is evidently exclusive"; a State may make its own citizens but not citizens "in the sense in which that word is used in the Constitution" (Taney).
- The Constitution "must be construed now as it was understood at the time of its adoption"; changed opinion is a reason to amend, not to reinterpret (Taney).
- Congress governs acquired territory under an implied power, derived from the New States Clause, that is limited by every guarantee of the Bill of Rights, including due process as a limit on what Congress may do to property (Taney).
- A State decides for itself whether to give effect to another jurisdiction's law of slavery within its borders (Taney, following [[Strader v. Graham]]; Nelson).

### Reasoning

**(Taney, C.J., for the Court)**

- **The question framed as membership.** "Citizens" and "the people" are the "sovereign people"; the question is whether persons of African descent "compose a portion of this people." He answers that they "are not included, and were not intended to be included."
- **Two citizenships.** A State may confer state citizenship "upon an alien, or any one it thinks proper," but that status is "confined to the boundaries of the State." Because the naturalization power is exclusive, "no State can, by any act or law of its own, passed since the adoption of the Constitution, introduce a new member into the political community created by the Constitution of the United States."
- **The founding-era record.** He asserts that at the founding persons of African descent were regarded as an inferior class who "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect," and presents that as the "fixed and universal" opinion of the time. The sentence is Taney's account of founding-era opinion, which he then treats as fixing the Constitution's meaning; it is the premise of the holding, not an aside.
- **The Declaration.** The general words "would seem to embrace the whole human family," but the framers were "great men" who would have been "flagrantly inconsistent" had they meant it, so they must not have.
- **The constitutional text.** The Slave Trade Clause and the Fugitive Slave Clause "point directly and specifically to the negro race as a separate class of persons," and both treat that class as property.
- **The Articles of Confederation.** The Privileges and Immunities article spoke of "free inhabitants," but the troop requisition clause counted "white inhabitants," which in his view marks "the line of distinction between the citizen and the subject."
- **Method.** Changed opinion cannot justify "a more liberal construction"; the Constitution "speaks not only in the same words, but with the same meaning and intent with which it spoke when it came from the hands of its framers." "Any other rule of construction would abrogate the judicial character of this court, and make it the mere reflex of the popular opinion or passion of the day."
- **The Territories Clause.** It "does not speak of any territory, nor of Territories," but of "the" territory then belonging to the United States. It begins with disposing of land, pairs territory with movable "property" like ships and arms, and closes with a savings clause for existing claims. "Needful rules and regulations" contrasts with the "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" granted over the seat of government.
- **The implied territorial power.** The power to admit new States implies the power to acquire territory "to become a State, and not to be held as a colony." The power to acquire "necessarily carries with it the power to preserve," so Congress may establish territorial government, but only "acting within the scope of its constitutional authority."
- **The Bill of Rights in the territories.** Congress could not establish a religion, abridge speech, deny the right "to keep and bear arms," or quarter soldiers in a territory. The Fifth Amendment unites "the rights of property" with "the rights of person." A citizen who "had committed no offence against the laws" cannot lose his property for entering a territory with it.
- **Slave property.** "[T]he right of property in a slave is distinctly and expressly affirmed in the Constitution," and "no word can be found in the Constitution which gives Congress a greater power over slave property." The only power Congress holds is "the power coupled with the duty of guarding and protecting the owner in his rights."
- **Illinois and the forum question.** [[Strader v. Graham]] controls; Missouri law, as its highest court "firmly settled," makes Scott a slave; and the federal suit is an improper end-run around § 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789.

**(Nelson, J., concurring)**

- Decides only the Illinois question: "the law of the State is supreme over the subject of slavery within its jurisdiction," and whether Missouri gives effect to Illinois law "is a question for her to determine."
- Laws have "no inherent authority extra-territorially"; recognition is "purely from comity."
- Even assuming the Missouri Compromise valid within the territory, "it can have no operation or effect beyond its limits, or within the jurisdiction of a State." "If Congress possesses the power, under the Constitution, to abolish slavery in a Territory, it must necessarily possess the like power to establish it."
- The casebook notes that this opinion "apparently was originally drafted as the majority opinion of the Court."

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Curtis, J., dissenting)**

- **Citizenship in the States in 1787.** At the ratification of the Articles, "all free native-born inhabitants of the States of New Hampshire, Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, and North Carolina, though descended from African slaves, were not only citizens of those States, but such of them as had the other necessary qualifications possessed the franchise of electors, on equal terms with other citizens." Taney's own premise (citizens of the States became citizens of the new body) then answers the question.
- **The Articles of Confederation.** The framers of the fourth article knew free persons of color were state citizens, and "more restricted language, which would have excluded such persons, was deliberately and purposely rejected."
- **Birthright.** The Constitution's reference to "a natural-born citizen" "assumes that citizenship may be acquired by birth," under the public-law principle "which referred citizenship to the place of birth." The naturalization power is "confined to the removal of the disabilities of foreign birth," so Congress has no power to disfranchise the native-born. The rule: "every free person born on the soil of a State, who is a citizen of that State by force of its Constitution or laws, is also a citizen of the United States."
- **The Preamble.** Because free Black men voted in five of the thirteen States, "it is not true, in point of fact, that the Constitution was made exclusively by the white race," and the Preamble's "for themselves and their posterity" includes them.
- **No authority to reach the merits.** He dissents from "their assumption of authority to examine the constitutionality of the act of Congress commonly called the Missouri compromise act." "Into that judgment, according to the settled course of this court, nothing appearing after a plea to the merits can enter. A great question of constitutional law, deeply affecting the peace and welfare of the country, is not, in my opinion, a fit subject to be thus reached."
- **Territory law and Missouri recognition.** Two questions must be kept distinct: what the territory's law did to Scott's status, and whether Missouri recognizes it. Congress positively prohibited slavery in the territory, so the master's rights ended there. A State may refuse recognition only through its statute or customary law, not on "political considerations," which are "functions of diplomatists and legislators, not of judges."
- **The marriage.** A slave cannot contract marriage; Emerson's consent to a lawful marriage in free territory was the most "effectual abandonment of the legal rights of a master over his slave," and a Missouri law annulling the marriage would impair the obligation of contracts ([[Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward]]).
- **Missouri's reversal.** When a State's highest court contradicts its own decisions, "the last decision is not necessarily to be taken as the rule."
- **The Territories Clause text.** "[T]his is a grant of power to the Congress—that it is therefore necessarily a grant of power to legislate." "An enactment that slavery may or may not exist there, is a regulation respecting the territory," and "it is necessarily left to the legislative discretion to determine whether a law be needful." He relies on "the long line of legislative and executive precedents under it" (the casebook omits his rejection of the original-territory-only reading).
- **No implied exception.** None of the three political theories urged at the bar (prohibition only, popular sovereignty, a constitutional right to carry slaves) rests on any clause. To insert an exception "upon reasons purely political, renders its judicial interpretation impossible." "Political reasons have not the requisite certainty to afford rules of juridical interpretation. They are different in different men. They are different in the same men at different times." When "the theoretical opinions of individuals are allowed to control its meaning," "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men, who for the time being have power to declare what the Constitution is, according to their own views of what it ought to mean."
- **Due process.** Congress could always ban the slave trade under the commerce power: "A citizen of the United States owns slaves in Cuba, and brings them to the United States, where they are set free by the legislation of Congress. Does this legislation deprive him of his property without due process of law? If so, what becomes of the laws prohibiting the slave trade? If not, how can a similar regulation respecting a Territory violate the fifth amendment of the Constitution?" And a right to hold slaves in a territory without any "municipal regulations which are essential to the existence of slavery" is inconceivable.

**(McLean, J., dissenting)**

- **Slavery is local.** Under [[Prigg v. Pennsylvania|Prigg]] and [[Somerset v. Stewart|Somersett's case]], "[t]he state of slavery is deemed to be a mere municipal regulation, founded upon and limited to the range of the territorial laws." A master who enters free territory carries no law with him: "colored persons are made property by the law of the State, and no such power has been given to Congress."
- **Congressional discretion.** Even if "territory" means only land, Congress may organize a temporary government, and "[i]f Congress may establish a Territorial Government in the exercise of its discretion, it is a clear principle that a court cannot control that discretion."
- **No taking, no forfeiture.** The Compromise "did not purport to forfeit property, or take it for public purposes. It only prohibited slavery; in doing which, it followed the ordinance of 1787."
- **Equal sovereignty.** "I can perceive no reason why the institutions of Illinois should not receive the same consideration as those of Missouri." The majority's rule lets slave States "introduce slavery into a free State" and restore it on return, and Scott's return was not voluntary: "It would be a mockery of law and an outrage on his rights to coerce his return, and then claim that it was voluntary."
- **The Missouri decision.** For twenty-eight years Missouri freed slaves domiciled on free soil; the reversal came "whether by some new light suddenly springing up, or an excited public opinion, or both." A state court that refuses to notice an act of Congress on a claim of freedom raises a federal question reviewable under § 25: "If a State court may do this, on a question involving the liberty of a human being, what protection do the laws afford?"

### Cold-call notes

- **Why is this case in Judicial Power rather than only in Article IV?** Because it is the paradigm of the Court deciding more than it had to, on the most divisive political question in the country, in the belief that a judicial ruling could settle it. Note 1 gives the circumstances: Bleeding Kansas, the caning of Sumner, a narrower Nelson draft abandoned once the dissenters signaled they would reach the Compromise, and President-elect Buchanan lobbying the Court "about a matter that was sub judice." The ruling hardened Southern resistance and helped elect Lincoln instead of settling anything.
- **Was the Missouri Compromise holding dictum?** State both sides.
  - *Dictum:* the judgment is dismissal for want of jurisdiction, and a court without jurisdiction cannot decide the merits. Curtis: nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" into that judgment. The modern rule is that a federal court must establish jurisdiction before anything else, and once it finds none it can only announce that fact and dismiss ([[Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment]], 1998, echoing [[Ex parte McCardle]], Class 15).
  - *Holding:* Note 2 says each part "purports to be part of the Opinion of the Court," and a majority joined it. Taney's answers are that the error was "patent on the record," that the Court sits to correct the whole record, and, most strongly, that the territorial ruling is itself jurisdictional: if the Compromise did not free Scott, he was a slave, and a slave cannot be a citizen, so the Circuit Court "had no jurisdiction, when, by the laws of the State, the plaintiff was a slave, and not a citizen." On that view the merits are an alternative ground for the same jurisdictional judgment, and alternative grounds are holdings.
  - *Baude's likely press:* Marbury also decided that Marbury had a right and a remedy before holding the Court lacked jurisdiction. If Marbury's ordering was acceptable, what makes Dred Scott's different? One answer: Marbury's extra discussion invalidated nothing, while Dred Scott's voided a statute; another: Marbury decided jurisdiction last and Dred Scott decided it first, then kept going.
- **Taney's originalism against Curtis's originalism (Note 3).** Both claim to follow the Constitution as understood in 1787, and they reach opposite results. Taney asks what the framers "intended" and who was "in the minds of the framers," and treats the prevailing racial attitudes, and the framers' own conduct, as controlling even against general words he concedes "would seem to embrace the whole human family." That is expected-application reasoning. Curtis asks what the words meant in law ("natural-born citizen," "naturalization," "the people") and what the legal facts were in 1787 (who was actually a citizen and voter under state law). Curtis wins on Taney's own terms: Taney conceded that everyone recognized as a state citizen in 1787 became a national citizen, and Curtis proved that free Black men were. Frame it with [[Types of Constitutional Argument]]: the same modality (history and original meaning) can be done honestly or dishonestly, and Dred Scott is evidence against bad history rather than against the method. Curtis thought the Court had departed from the original meaning ("the theoretical opinions of individuals"), not followed it too closely.
- **The due process "sleight-of-hand" (Note 2b).** The clause permits deprivations of property with "due process of law"; Taney converts it into a substantive bar on a whole class of legislation. Curtis's Cuba hypothetical is the cleanest refutation: if a ban on bringing slaves into the country is valid despite the Fifth Amendment, so is a ban on bringing them into a territory.
- **Why not the Territories Clause?** Taney's textual points (the definite article, the pairing with "property," "needful rules and regulations" against "exclusive legislation") are his best arguments; answer each with Curtis (any grant to a legislature is a grant to legislate) and with practice (the Northwest Ordinance re-enacted by the First Congress, and the Compromise itself, then thirty-seven years old). Then notice that the holding does no work: Taney finds the power anyway, implied from the New States Clause, so the case really turns on the due process limit.
- **A tension in Taney's own opinion.** On what territory belongs to the United States he defers completely: "the political department of the Government shall recognize as within the limits of the United States, the judicial department is also bound to recognize." On what Congress may do in that territory he refuses any deference. McLean's "a court cannot control that discretion" and Curtis's "legislative discretion to determine whether a law be needful" state the deference he withheld.
- **Vary the facts.**
  - Illinois had adjudicated Scott free before his return: Note 2c asks whether Full Faith and Credit would then oblige Missouri to honor the judgment. The distinguishing fact from the actual case is a judgment, not mere residence.
  - Scott had fled into the territory: the Compromise's own proviso allowed reclamation of fugitives, and the Fugitive Slave Clause would govern.
  - Scott had been domiciled in Illinois permanently rather than taken there by an army officer on assignment: the free-soil claim is stronger, but on Taney's and Nelson's comity reasoning Missouri still decides.
  - The Missouri Supreme Court had followed its old precedents: Nelson's own reasoning would then have freed Scott, which shows how much the result turned on the 1852 reversal McLean attacks.
- **Is Taney's Illinois holding defensible?** Note 2c suggests it is "perhaps not entirely indefensible" as a choice-of-law rule. The press: if Missouri may apply its own law to a returning domiciliary despite Illinois law, why may it not do the same despite the Missouri Compromise? Curtis's answer is that the federal statute operated directly on Scott's status and ended it, and the marriage confirms it.
- **From Dred Scott to departmentalism.** Lincoln (Class 13, [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]) did not propose to free Scott or defy the judgment; he denied that the reasoning bound Congress, the President, or voters as a political rule. Be ready to state the three positions: judicial supremacy ([[Cooper v. Aaron]]), departmentalism (Lincoln; each branch interprets under its Article VI oath), and the middle view that judgments bind parties but opinions do not bind other branches. Dred Scott is the case departmentalists cite first; supremacists answer that the remedy for a wrong decision is Article V, which is what happened.
- **The Article V answer.** Taney said the remedy for injustice is amendment, and the country took him at his word. The Citizenship Clause adopts Curtis's rule of birth on the soil and makes national citizenship primary and state citizenship follow from residence. Taney's premise that a State cannot make a national citizen is inverted: now the nation makes state citizens.
- **Accuracy about the worst line.** "No rights which the white man was bound to respect" is Taney's description of how he claimed the founding generation regarded Black people, offered as proof of what "citizens" meant. It is not dicta and not a mere historical aside: it is the premise of the citizenship holding, and Curtis's evidence shows it was false as history.
- **Memorable lines.** Taney: "the mere reflex of the popular opinion or passion of the day"; "It is acquired to become a State, and not to be held as a colony." Curtis: "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men"; "functions of diplomatists and legislators, not of judges." McLean: "a mockery of law and an outrage on his rights." Casebook: "Did you catch the sleight-of-hand?"
