## Snapshot

Dred Scott, held as a slave in free Illinois and in territory where the Missouri Compromise barred slavery, sued the New York man who claimed him in federal court on diversity, for his family's freedom. The Court held that no person of African descent could be a "citizen" under Article III, so there was no jurisdiction, then voided the Compromise because the Fifth Amendment protected slave property there. Article III, § 2, Article IV, § 3 and the Due Process Clause are in play.

## Issue

The casebook asks whether Congress could "prohibit slavery in . . . the territories"; this case answers no.

- Can a free person of African descent be a State "citizen" under Article III?
- Having found no jurisdiction, may the Court reach the merits?
- Could Congress ban slavery at Fort Snelling, a question reached only after the citizenship holding?
- Did Scott's Illinois residence free him on his return?

## Rule

Now repudiated: no person of African descent whose ancestors were enslaved could be a "citizen" under Article III, and Congress could not bar slavery from a federal territory, because the Fifth Amendment protected slaveholders' property there. Power over later-acquired territory is implied from the New States Clause and bounded by the Bill of Rights. What survives is the lesson about judicial power: the Court voided an act of Congress after holding it had no jurisdiction.

- "The words “people of the United States” and “citizens” are synonymous terms"; a State may make its own citizens but cannot "introduce a new member into the political community created by the Constitution." Overruled by the Fourteenth Amendment.
- The Constitution "must be construed now as it was understood at the time of its adoption" — changed opinion is a reason to amend, not reinterpret.
- A State decides whether another jurisdiction's law of slavery has effect within its borders (*Strader v. Graham*).

## Operative facts

- **In 1834 Dr. John Emerson, an army surgeon, took Scott from Missouri to Rock Island in free Illinois and held him there as a slave for about two years.** *The free-State claim, which Taney and Nelson remit to Missouri law under Strader v. Graham.*
- **In 1836 Emerson moved Scott to Fort Snelling, where the Missouri Compromise of 1820 prohibited slavery, and consented to his marriage to Harriet; Eliza was born north of Missouri.** *Puts the Compromise — repealed in 1854 — in issue; Curtis treats the consent as the master's "effectual abandonment" of his rights.*
- **In 1838 Emerson removed the family to Missouri, where they resided ever after.** *The return raises the choice-of-law question; McLean denies it was voluntary — "a mockery of law and an outrage on his rights."*
- **Scott sued Emerson's widow in Missouri state court and won at trial, but in 1852 the Missouri Supreme Court overruled its own comity precedents and held him still a slave.** *Taney and Nelson follow it; Curtis answers that "the last decision is not necessarily . . . the rule."*
- **Scott was then sold or transferred to Mrs. Emerson's brother, John Sanford, a citizen of New York, whose name the reports misspell "Sandford."** *The sale makes a diversity suit possible, and so puts citizenship at the center.*
- **Scott's lawyers filed a fresh federal suit as a citizen of Missouri, holding the state case open.** *Taney calls it "an attempt to evade the law," a direct writ of error being barred by § 25 of the Judiciary Act.*
- **Sanford's plea in abatement rested only on Scott's African descent and his ancestors' enslavement; the Circuit Court rejected it and gave judgment for Sanford.** *The writ of error put the bare question of race and citizenship to the Court — Curtis: "this plaintiff has the right to the judgment of the court."*
- **The Court heard argument twice, the second in 1856, and decided in March 1857.** *The reargument is where the case changed: Nelson's narrow draft was dropped once the dissenters signalled they would reach the Compromise.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (7–2; Taney, C.J., for the Court; Wayne, Nelson, Grier, Daniel, Campbell and Catron, JJ., each writing separately; McLean and Curtis, JJ., dissenting)

- **No citizenship, so no jurisdiction.** Descendants of imported slaves were "not included . . . under the word “citizens” in the Constitution."
- **The merits are open.** An error "patent on the record" may be corrected, lest it "be drawn into precedent."
- **The Missouri Compromise is void** — reached only after the citizenship holding, and a second ground for the same judgment: there was "no jurisdiction, when . . . the plaintiff was a slave, and not a citizen."
- **Illinois residence did not free him.** His status on return "depended on the laws of Missouri, and not of Illinois."
- **Disposition.** Judgment reversed, the suit "to be dismissed for want of jurisdiction."

**Taney, C.J., for the Court**

- **Membership and method.** Persons of African descent are no part of the "sovereign people": at the founding, he says, they were "an inferior class" with "no rights which the white man was bound to respect." Changed opinion cannot reshape the text, lest the Court become "the mere reflex of the popular opinion . . . of the day."
- **The territories.** The clause speaks of "the" territory and grants only "needful rules and regulations," so power over territory acquired later comes from the New States Clause, bounded by the Bill of Rights.

**Nelson, J., concurring**

- Decides the Illinois question only: whether Missouri gives Illinois law effect "is a question for her to determine."

**Curtis, J., dissenting**

- **Citizenship.** At the ratification of the Articles, "all free native-born inhabitants" of five States, "though descended from African slaves," were citizens and voters — which answers the question on Taney's own premise.
- **The merits and the territories.** "[N]othing appearing after a plea to the merits can enter" into the judgment, and such a question "is not . . . a fit subject to be thus reached." The Territories Clause, being a grant to Congress, is "necessarily a grant of power to legislate."

**McLean, J., dissenting**

- Slavery is "a mere municipal regulation . . . limited to the range of the territorial laws," so a master entering free territory carries no law with him; and a court "cannot control" Congress's discretion to govern a territory.

## Arguments rejected

- *Free Black men were state citizens and voters in 1787, and state citizens became United States citizens; so their free-born descendants are citizens too.* Taney concedes the middle premise and denies the first: the class was "not even in the minds of the framers."
- *The Declaration's "all men are created equal" includes everyone.* Taney admits the words "would seem to embrace the whole human family," but framers who meant them so would have been "flagrantly inconsistent."
- *A court that has found no jurisdiction must stop.* Taney: the error was "patent on the record," and the territorial ruling is itself jurisdictional: a slave is no citizen.
- *The Territories Clause gives Congress plenary power, used since the Northwest Ordinance.* Taney: "needful rules and regulations" are "not the words usually employed by statesmen in giving supreme power of legislation."
- *Slave property is different, existing only by positive local law.* Taney: "there is no law of nations standing between the people of the United States and their Government."

## Where it sits

**The second exercise of judicial review.** The syllabus sets the case after [[Marbury v. Madison]] and [[Stuart v. Laird]]. Marbury declined a jurisdiction Congress had granted; Dred Scott, on a record where the Court had just found no jurisdiction, voided a sectional compromise — the power [[The Federalist No. 78]] called least dangerous and [[Brutus No. 11]] feared.

**The backlash.** The decision drew the fiercest attack on the Court's authority in the nation's history. Lincoln's [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates|debates with Douglas]] develop departmentalism; [[Cooper v. Aaron]] states judicial supremacy; [[Ex parte Merryman]] puts Taney against Lincoln.

**Repudiated, and what survives.** The Reconstruction Amendments killed the case: the Thirteenth abolished slavery and the Fourteenth's Citizenship Clause adopted Curtis's birthright rule, so the citizenship holding and the slaveholder's territorial right are dead law. What stays live is the Article IV, § 3 reasoning that power over acquired territory is implied and bounded by the Bill of Rights — the frame the *Insular Cases* later reworked. The due process holding survives only as an ancestor — the first use of the clause to void a federal statute, the lineage that critics of *Lochner* invoke.

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "[T]he Court seemed to go out of its way to decide as many issues as possible, in the most pro-slavery manner possible. Why?"**

- "Any answer remains speculative." The moment: "Bleeding Kansas," the caning of Sumner, a contentious election. Taney went comprehensive once the dissenters signalled they would free Scott, Buchanan lobbying "sub judice."

**Note 2. "Consider the three holdings in Taney's opinion . . . each purports to be part of the Opinion of the Court."**

- *Dictum:* nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" into a judgment of dismissal for want of jurisdiction ([[Ex parte McCardle]]). *Holding:* a majority joined each part, and the ruling is itself jurisdictional — no Compromise, no freedom, no citizenship — an alternative ground, as in [[Marbury v. Madison|Marbury]].
- **(a) "How many ways is this wrong? . . . What is really going on here?"** Taney conceded that state citizens in 1787 became national ones; Curtis shows that free Black men were among them. The note's answer is motive: such citizens would claim Article IV "privileges and immunities."
- **(b) "Did you catch the sleight-of-hand?"** The clause allows deprivations with due process; Taney reads those words out, making it "a judicially invented fundamental right of white persons to own slaves." Curtis's Cuba hypothetical: "what becomes of the laws prohibiting the slave trade?"
- **(c) "Might Missouri . . . have been required to honor . . . the Illinois judgment under . . . Full Faith and Credit?"** Yes — a judgment, not mere residence, distinguishes the case. The note then presses the dissenters: why not against the Compromise too?

**Note 3. "How would you describe . . . Taney's interpretive method? Was it originalist? . . . Did [Curtis] think the Court followed the original meaning . . . too closely, or disregarded it?"**

- Taney asks who was "in the minds of the framers," treating founding-era conduct as controlling against words that "would seem to embrace the whole human family" — expected application, not original meaning. Curtis asks what the words meant in law, and concludes that the Court disregarded it ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).

**Note 4. "The Court's decision produced the strongest and most sustained backlash against the authority of the Supreme Court's decisions in our nation's history."**

- Lincoln ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]) never proposed to free Scott or to defy the judgment; he denied that its reasoning bound the political branches — departmentalism under the Article VI oath, against [[Cooper v. Aaron]].

### Further drilling

- **Vary the facts.** Scott as a fugitive into the territory: the Compromise's proviso and the Fugitive Slave Clause govern instead.
- **A tension in Taney.** On what counts as United States territory, courts are "bound to recognize" what the political department recognizes; on what Congress may do there, he defers not at all. The territorial holding does no work anyway: he finds the power, so the case turns on due process.
- **Accuracy about the worst line.** "No rights which the white man was bound to respect" is Taney's claim about founding-era attitudes, offered as proof of what "citizens" meant. It is the holding's premise, and false as history.
- **Memorable lines.** Curtis: "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men."
