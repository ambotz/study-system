## Rule

A federal tax that produces revenue is valid even though it discourages the taxed activity, reaches conduct Congress cannot regulate directly, and raises only negligible revenue.

## Facts

- Congress taxed "persons engaged in the business of accepting wagers," that is, bookmakers.
- The challengers argued that the wagering tax was really a penalty on illegal intrastate gambling, a matter within the police power reserved to the states. *The Court accepted that the wagering tax had a regulatory effect and held that effect immaterial.*
- The wagering tax produced revenue. *The amount was conceded to be irrelevant, even if negligible.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The wagering tax is valid, because "regardless of its regulatory effect, the wagering tax produces revenue."
- A federal excise tax "does not cease to be valid merely because it discourages or deters the activities taxed."
- A tax is not invalid "because the revenue obtained is negligible."
- The Court relied on the *License Tax Cases*, *Veazie Bank v. Fenno*, [[McCray v. United States]], and other decisions upholding taxes where "the intent to curtail and hinder, as well as tax," an activity was "manifest."
- (Jackson, J., concurring) Jackson joined the opinion "with such doubt that if the minority agreed upon an opinion which did not impair legitimate use of the taxing power I probably would join it."

**Rejected**

- *"Congress, under the pretense of exercising its power to tax has attempted to penalize illegal intrastate gambling through the regulatory features of the Act . . . and has thus infringed the police power which is reserved to the states"; the "sole purpose" of the statute is to penalize illegal gambling "through the guise of a tax measure."* The Court answered that the wagering tax, like the excises it had upheld before, has a regulatory effect but still produces revenue, and revenue is what makes it a tax.

## Context

Kahriger closes the survey in [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]]. The casebook asks what happened to [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]: either Bailey is distinguishable, or Kahriger shows that "the Child Labor Tax Case couldn't come up with an administrable line between taxes and penalties." The best distinction is structural. The wagering tax contained no schedule of prescribed conduct and no non-revenue enforcement agency; the Bailey tax was keyed to a detailed code of employment practice, with a reasonable-mistake defense and enforcement by the Secretary of Labor as well as the tax commissioner. Jackson's concurrence shows how close the question was. See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].

In [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]], Roberts cited Kahriger for "the essential feature of any tax: it produces at least some revenue for the Government." After Congress set the shared responsibility payment at $0 in 2017, challengers argued that a payment raising no revenue was no longer a tax under Kahriger (casebook Note 5f). *California v. Texas* (2021) dismissed that challenge for lack of standing, so the question was never reached.
