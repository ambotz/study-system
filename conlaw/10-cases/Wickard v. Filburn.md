## Snapshot

An Ohio dairy farmer sowed twice his wheat allotment and was penalized on 239 bushels he may never have sold. He won below; the Court reversed. Congress may reach even local, non-commercial activity that, aggregated with all similar activity, "exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce," and labels like "production" and "indirect" no longer decide federal power. Art. I, § 8, cl. 3 is argued; the Necessary and Proper Clause does the work.

## Issue

- May Congress reach wheat production "not intended in any part for commerce but wholly for consumption on the farm"?
- Do the categories "production," "consumption" and "marketing," or the old "direct/indirect" line, decide the question of federal power?
- Does a grower's own trivial contribution matter, or may it be aggregated with that of all others similarly situated?

## Rule

Congress may regulate even local, non-commercial activity — growing wheat for use on one's own farm — if that activity, aggregated with all similar activity, exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce. The categories that had decided earlier cases do no work.

- Whether the activity is "production," "consumption" or "marketing" "is, therefore, not material for purposes of deciding the question of federal power."
- Local activity "may still, whatever its nature, be reached by Congress if it exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce and this irrespective of whether such effect is what might at some earlier time have been defined as 'direct' or 'indirect.'"
- A trivial individual contribution is regulable where, "taken together with that of many others similarly situated," it is "far from trivial."

## Operative facts

- **Through the 1920s and 1930s wheat exports collapsed from over 25 per cent of production to under 10 per cent, and surpluses clogged markets and led elevators to turn grain away.** *Wheat was a "problem industry" — the factual premise for a national quota scheme.*
- **The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 aimed "to control the volume moving in interstate and foreign commerce in order to avoid surpluses and shortages," by a national acreage allotment broken down to individual farms.** *A comprehensive scheme, the feature [[Gonzales v. Raich]] later leans on.*
- **The Act penalized excess wheat whether or not it was ever sold, and defined "market" to include feeding wheat to livestock whose products are sold.** *"Penalties do not depend upon whether any part of the wheat . . . is sold or intended to be sold." This is what takes the case beyond [[United States v. Darby]], which covered production "for commerce."*
- **Filburn ran a small dairy and poultry farm in Ohio, raising a winter wheat crop that he sold in part, fed in part to his livestock, ground into flour for his family and kept as seed.** *The record never stated the intended disposition of the 1941 excess, so the Court decided the case assuming the wheat might never be marketed.*
- **He was notified of his 11.1-acre allotment before planting and again before harvest, and sowed 23 acres.** *Notice forecloses a reliance defence; the overplanting was deliberate.*
- **The 11.9 excess acres yielded 239 bushels, carrying a penalty of 49 cents a bushel, $117.11.** *The smallness is the point: those bushels are the whole subject of the penalty, which is why aggregation had to carry the case.*
- **He neither paid the penalty nor stored or surrendered the excess, so the committee denied him the marketing card a buyer needed.** *The sanction works through the market, not by seizing the wheat.*
- **Home-grown consumption was "the most variable factor in the disappearance of the wheat crop," varying by more than 20 per cent of average production.** *That variability makes homegrown wheat, in aggregate, a substantial influence on price.*
- **Filburn sued the Secretary of Agriculture and the committeemen to enjoin the penalty and have the quota provisions declared unconstitutional, and the district court ruled for him.** *The appeal is the Secretary's; the casebook omits the Fifth Amendment holding.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (reversed; Jackson, J., for a unanimous Court)

- **The categories do not decide.** Whether the activity is "production," "consumption" or "marketing" "is . . . not material for purposes of deciding the question of federal power."
- **Substantial economic effect.** "[E]ven if appellee's activity be local and though it may not be regarded as commerce, it may still, whatever its nature, be reached by Congress if it exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce."
- **Aggregation.** "That appellee's own contribution to the demand for wheat may be trivial by itself is not enough to remove him from the scope of federal regulation where, as here, his contribution, taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial." Congress may therefore include homegrown wheat in the quotas.

**Jackson, J., for the Court**

- *Darby* nearly controls: the question "would merit little consideration" after [[United States v. Darby]] but for the extension to production "not intended in any part for commerce but wholly for consumption on the farm."
- Marshall in *Gibbons v. Ogden* described the commerce power "with a breadth never yet exceeded" and warned that "effective restraints on its exercise must proceed from political rather than from judicial processes." Serious use began only after 1887, and the resulting cases drove the Court to economic reality.
- The formulas are abandoned: "[o]nce an economic measure of the reach of the power . . . is accepted, questions of federal power cannot be decided simply by finding the activity in question to be 'production' nor can consideration of its economic effects be foreclosed by calling them 'indirect.'"
- Local character still bears on whether Congress "intended to reach" an activity, or whether a State may regulate absent federal action — but not on whether Congress has the power.
- Interstate commerce in wheat is "large and important": sixteen surplus States supply thirty-two others and the District of Columbia.
- Two channels of effect: price may be supported "as effectively by sustaining or increasing the demand as by limiting the supply." Homegrown wheat "overhangs the market" and may enter it when prices rise, and even if never sold it "supplies a need of the man who grew it which would otherwise be reflected by purchases in the open market" — so it "competes with wheat in commerce."
- Fairness is for Congress: conflicts of economic interest are "wisely left . . . to resolution by the Congress under its more flexible and responsible legislative process."

## Arguments rejected

- *Production and consumption on a farm are local, and their effect on interstate commerce is at most "indirect"; no decision has allowed regulation of production where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce.* Jackson concedes the second point — "there is no decision of this Court that such activities may be regulated where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce" — and holds the categories themselves wrong: federal power is not decided by "nomenclature such as 'production' and 'indirect.'"
- *Forcing farmers into the market to buy what they could grow is an unfair subsidy to specialized wheat growers.* "It is of the essence of regulation that it lays a restraining hand on the self interest of the regulated," and "with the wisdom, workability, or fairness, of the plan of regulation we have nothing to do."
- *The government's own framing: the statute regulates only "marketing."* Not decided; the label does not matter.

## Where it sits

**The Modern Debate.** Wickard opens the casebook's unit of three cases on the outer boundaries of the commerce power, with *Heart of Atlanta Motel* (1964) and [[United States v. Lopez]] (1995), the first in fifty years to hold a statute beyond it.

**The arc behind it.** *Gibbons v. Ogden* (a broad power, restrained by politics not courts); *E.C. Knight* (manufacture is not commerce); the direct/indirect line of *Schechter Poultry*; [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (production is local, prohibition is pretext); [[United States v. Darby]] (Hammer overruled, production "for commerce" reachable as a means); and Wickard, reaching production not for commerce at all. Jackson writes the obituary of the categories: "the mechanical application of legal formulas no longer feasible."

**Aggregation's afterlife.** [[United States v. Lopez]] confines aggregation to economic activity, so gun possession near a school cannot be aggregated. [[Gonzales v. Raich]] applies Wickard to marijuana grown and consumed at home — the closest fact pattern to Filburn's — as controlling.

**The outer limit.** [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] draws the line: Filburn was at least growing wheat, while the mandate reached people not active in the market. Roberts, quoting Lopez, called Wickard "perhaps the most far reaching example of Commerce Clause authority over intrastate activity"; the joint dissent called it "the ne plus ultra of expansive Commerce Clause jurisprudence."

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "The doctrinal innovation of Wickard v. Filburn is the aggregation principle: the idea that one should look at all homegrown wheat in the country and its effect on commerce . . . . Is the principle sound? And what power does Wickard rely on? Is the regulation of homegrown wheat for home consumption a regulation of 'interstate commerce'? If not, is the decision wrong? Or can regulation of purely local production and consumption be 'necessary and proper' for regulation of an interstate commercial market?"**

- Aggregation is sound where the class comes from the statute's own scheme — everyone growing wheat against a quota — since exempting one grower implies exempting all. It becomes unbounded if Congress may define the class at will.
- Home consumption is not "interstate commerce" in any ordinary sense, so the answer to the second question is the third: the holding stands or falls as a Necessary and Proper one. The logic is means-end — homegrown wheat must be included to make the quota and price scheme work — and the government argued the point in the alternative, though Jackson speaks only of the Commerce Clause.
- Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] makes that basis explicit — the best evidence that the note's last question is the live one.

**Note 2. "Recall the Court's admonition in Gibbons v. Ogden that the Constitution's enumeration of powers 'presupposes something not enumerated.' In light of Darby and Wickard, is there anything Congress cannot do pursuant to its power to regulate commerce, as aided by the Necessary and Proper Clause? . . . Can you come up with any? If not, is that a problem?"**

- Candidates: a general federal homicide statute, or a federal family-law code — aggregate economic effects, no commercial character.
- [[United States v. Lopez]] supplies the doctrinal answer: noneconomic activity in a traditionally state field cannot be aggregated.
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] supplies the other: Congress may regulate activity but not compel entry into commerce. Wickard was the government's main precedent for the mandate, and the dissenters treated it as the limit.
- If no answer can be given, that is a problem on Gibbons's own terms: an enumeration that excludes nothing is not one.

### Further drilling

- **Did Filburn actually eat the wheat?** "The intended disposition of the crop here involved has not been expressly stated." Do not say he consumed the 239 bushels; say the Act reached them even if he did.
- **Distinguish Darby and Hammer.** Darby's lumber was produced "for commerce" and needed only a means-end link to a shipment ban; Wickard needed a theory of why non-market activity affects a market — substitution plus aggregation. [[Hammer v. Dagenhart|Hammer]]'s goods at least shipped interstate; Filburn's never left the farm.
- **Hypo: no quota scheme, and Congress simply caps home vegetable gardens.** Without a comprehensive scheme the means-end link weakens; [[Gonzales v. Raich]] leaned heavily on the comprehensiveness of the Controlled Substances Act.
- **Original meaning against doctrine.** On a trade-and-exchange reading of "commerce," Wickard is hard to defend as a Commerce Clause holding. Jackson's framing is functional and deferential — restraints come from politics, not courts. Does that match [[McCulloch v. Maryland|Marshall]], who deferred on means but reserved a check on pretext?
