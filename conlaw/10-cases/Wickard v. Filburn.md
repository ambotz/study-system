## Rule

Congress may regulate even local, non-commercial activity, such as growing wheat for use on one's own farm, if that activity, aggregated with all similar activity, "exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce"; labels such as "production" and "indirect" no longer decide questions of federal power.

## Facts

- **Filburn sowed 23 acres of wheat against an allotment of 11.1, and harvested 239 bushels from the excess acreage.** *The 239 bushels were the whole subject of the penalty; the case is about a small farmer's surplus, which is why aggregation had to carry it.*
- **The Act penalized excess wheat whether or not it was ever sold.** It defined "market" to include feeding wheat to livestock whose products are sold, and "Penalties do not depend upon whether any part of the wheat either within or without the quota is sold or intended to be sold." *This is what takes the case beyond [[United States v. Darby]], which covered production "for commerce."*
- **Filburn's practice was to sell part of his wheat, feed part to his livestock, grind part into flour for his family, and keep the rest as seed.** *The record did not say what he meant to do with the 1941 excess, so the Court decided the case on the assumption that the wheat might never be marketed.*
- **On-farm consumption was the most variable part of national wheat demand, varying by more than 20 per cent of average production.** *That variability is what made homegrown wheat, in the aggregate, a "substantial influence on price and market conditions."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (unanimous; Jackson for the Court)

- Whether the regulated activity is "production," "consumption," or "marketing" "is, therefore, not material for purposes of deciding the question of federal power." The test is actual economic effect.
- "[E]ven if appellee's activity be local and though it may not be regarded as commerce, it may still, whatever its nature, be reached by Congress if it exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce," whether or not that effect would once have been called "direct" or "indirect."
- **Aggregation.** "That appellee's own contribution to the demand for wheat may be trivial by itself is not enough to remove him from the scope of federal regulation where, as here, his contribution, taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial."
- Homegrown wheat affects interstate commerce because it "overhangs the market" and, even if never marketed, "supplies a need of the man who grew it which would otherwise be reflected by purchases in the open market." Congress may therefore include it in quotas to raise wheat prices.

**Rejected**

- *The Act regulates production and consumption on a farm, which are local, and their effect on interstate commerce is at most "indirect"; the Court has never allowed regulation of production where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce.* Jackson concedes the second point ("there is no decision of this Court that such activities may be regulated where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce") and holds that the categories themselves were wrong: questions of federal power are not to be decided by "nomenclature such as 'production' and 'indirect.'"
- *Forcing farmers into the market to buy what they could grow is an unfair subsidy to specialized wheat growers.* Jackson: "It is of the essence of regulation that it lays a restraining hand on the self interest of the regulated"; "with the wisdom, workability, or fairness, of the plan of regulation we have nothing to do."
- *The government's own framing: the statute regulates only "marketing."* The Court does not need to decide it; the label does not matter.

## Context

Wickard opens the casebook's "The Modern Debate," three cases on the outer bounds of the commerce power: Wickard, [[Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States]] (1964) and [[United States v. Lopez]] (1995), the first case in half a century to find a statute beyond the power.

The arc of the unit runs: [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] (a broad commerce power, with restraints to come from "political rather than from judicial processes," as Jackson reads it); [[United States v. E.C. Knight Co|E.C. Knight]] (manufacture is not commerce); the "direct/indirect" line of [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] and [[Carter v. Carter Coal Co|Carter Coal]]; [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (production is local; prohibition of harmless goods is pretext); [[United States v. Darby]] (Hammer overruled; production "for commerce" reachable as a means); and Wickard (production not for commerce reachable through aggregation). Jackson's opinion is written as the obituary of the formal categories: "the mechanical application of legal formulas no longer feasible."

Wickard's aggregation principle is the center of Class 19. [[United States v. Lopez]] confines aggregation to economic activity (gun possession near a school is not). [[Gonzales v. Raich]] (2005) applies Wickard to marijuana grown and consumed at home: the closest fact pattern to Filburn's, and the Court treats Wickard as controlling. [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] (Class 23) sets the outer limit: Filburn was at least growing wheat, but the individual mandate compelled people who were not active in the market to buy insurance. Roberts distinguished Wickard as a case about regulating activity; the joint dissent described Wickard as perhaps the most far-reaching exercise of the commerce power over intrastate activity.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Filburn sued the Secretary of Agriculture and local and state committee members in federal court to enjoin enforcement of the marketing penalty against his 1941 excess wheat and for a declaration that the quota provisions violated the Commerce Clause and the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The lower court ruled for Filburn.
- Appeal by the Secretary; reversed. The due process holding is omitted from the casebook.

### Facts

Filburn ran a small dairy and poultry farm in Montgomery County, Ohio, selling milk, poultry and eggs and raising a small crop of winter wheat each year. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 aimed "to control the volume moving in interstate and foreign commerce in order to avoid surpluses and shortages" and the price swings they caused; the Secretary set a national acreage allotment and broke it down to individual farms. Wheat was a "problem industry": exports had collapsed from over 25 per cent of production in the 1920s to under 10 per cent, and surpluses clogged markets, tied up railroad cars and led elevators to turn grain away. Filburn was notified of his allotment before planting and again before harvest, but planted more than twice his allotment. He neither paid the penalty of 49 cents a bushel ($117.11) nor stored or surrendered the excess, so the committee denied him the marketing card a buyer needed.

### Issue

- May Congress, under the Commerce Clause, regulate wheat production that is not intended in any part for commerce but for consumption on the farm?

### Holding

- Yes. Whatever the activity is called, Congress may reach it if it exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce; Filburn's excess wheat, aggregated with that of all similarly situated farmers, substantially affects the interstate wheat market by displacing market purchases and overhanging the market, and so defeats Congress's price-support scheme if left outside it.

### Rule

- Local activity "may still, whatever its nature, be reached by Congress if it exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce and this irrespective of whether such effect is what might at some earlier time have been defined as 'direct' or 'indirect.'"
- A trivial individual contribution is regulable where, "taken together with that of many others similarly situated," it is "far from trivial."
- "[T]he power to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the prices at which commodities in that commerce are dealt in and practices affecting such prices."
- "The stimulation of commerce is a use of the regulatory function quite as definitely as prohibitions or restrictions thereon."

### Reasoning

**(Jackson, J., for a unanimous Court)**

- **Darby nearly controls.** The question "would merit little consideration" after [[United States v. Darby]] but for the extension to production "not intended in any part for commerce but wholly for consumption on the farm."
- **History of the power.** Marshall in [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] described the commerce power "with a breadth never yet exceeded" and warned that "effective restraints on its exercise must proceed from political rather than from judicial processes." Only after 1887 did Congress use the power seriously, and the resulting cases drove the Court to economic reality.
- **Formulas abandoned.** "Once an economic measure of the reach of the power granted to Congress in the Commerce Clause is accepted, questions of federal power cannot be decided simply by finding the activity in question to be 'production' nor can consideration of its economic effects be foreclosed by calling them 'indirect.'"
- **Local character still has uses.** It may help decide "whether Congress intended to reach" an activity, or whether a state may regulate absent federal action (the dormant Commerce Clause), but not whether Congress has the power.
- **Economics of wheat.** Interstate commerce in wheat is "large and important"; sixteen states produce surpluses that supply thirty-two others and the District of Columbia. Homegrown consumption is "the most variable factor in the disappearance of the wheat crop."
- **Two channels of effect.** Price can be supported "as effectively by sustaining or increasing the demand as by limiting the supply." Homegrown wheat affects price because it may flow into the market when prices rise, and because, even if never sold, it replaces purchases: "Home-grown wheat in this sense competes with wheat in commerce."
- **Aggregation** (citing Darby): Filburn's trivial contribution, "taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial."
- **Fairness is for Congress.** Conflicts of economic interest are "wisely left under our system to resolution by the Congress under its more flexible and responsible legislative process."

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. The Court was unanimous.

### Cold-call notes

- **Say the numbers.** Allotment 11.1 acres; he sowed 23; 11.9 excess acres yielded 239 bushels; penalty 49 cents a bushel, $117.11. The smallness is the point: aggregation exists to reach a trivial individual effect.
- **Did Filburn actually eat the wheat?** The Court says "The intended disposition of the crop here involved has not been expressly stated." The statute penalized the excess regardless of use, so the Court had to assume the case in which the wheat never reached market and was fed to his animals, ground into flour or kept for seed. Don't state as a fact that he consumed all 239 bushels on the farm; state that the Act reached the wheat even if he did.
- **Distinguish Darby.** Darby's lumber was produced "for commerce"; Filburn's wheat was produced for home use. Darby needed only the means-end link to a shipment ban; Wickard needed a theory for why non-market activity affects a market: substitution (home-grown wheat replaces purchases) plus aggregation.
- **Distinguish Hammer.** Hammer said production is local and prohibition cannot be used to reach it. Wickard says whether an activity is "production" is "not material." The child-made goods in Hammer were at least shipped interstate; Filburn's wheat never left the farm, and Congress reached it anyway.
- **Note 1: what power does Wickard rely on?** Jackson speaks only of the Commerce Clause, but the government argued in the alternative that the Act was "necessary and proper," and the logic is means-end: including homegrown wheat is needed to make the national quota and price scheme work. Home consumption is not itself "interstate commerce" in any ordinary sense. Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] makes the Necessary and Proper Clause basis explicit.
- **Is aggregation sound?** Aggregation is sound when the class is defined by the statute's own scheme (everyone who grows wheat against the quota), since exempting any one farmer implies exempting all. It becomes unbounded if Congress may define the class however it likes. [[United States v. Lopez]] answers by aggregating only economic activity.
- **Note 2: is there anything Congress cannot do?** Gibbons said the enumeration "presupposes something not enumerated." After Wickard, try: a federal law against homicide, or a federal family-law code. The Lopez answer is that noneconomic, traditionally state activity cannot be aggregated. The [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] answer is that Congress cannot compel people to enter commerce. Wickard was the government's main precedent for the individual mandate, and the NFIB dissenters treated Wickard as the outer limit rather than a starting point.
- **Hypo: no quota scheme exists, and Congress simply caps the size of home vegetable gardens.** Without a comprehensive market-regulation scheme, the means-end link weakens; [[Gonzales v. Raich]] relied heavily on the comprehensive regulatory scheme of the Controlled Substances Act.
- **Original meaning versus doctrine.** On a trade-and-exchange reading of "commerce," Wickard is hard to defend as a Commerce Clause holding; it stands or falls as a Necessary and Proper holding. Jackson's own framing is functional and deferential: restraints on the power come from politics, not courts. Be ready to say whether that matches Marshall in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]], who both deferred on means and reserved a judicial check on pretext.
- **Memorable lines.** "Home-grown wheat in this sense competes with wheat in commerce"; "taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial"; "with the wisdom, workability, or fairness, of the plan of regulation we have nothing to do."
