## Snapshot

The House of Representatives sued two Cabinet Secretaries for spending billions on the Affordable Care Act's cost-sharing offsets after Congress refused to appropriate the money, and for rewriting the employer mandate by regulation. On a motion to dismiss, the district court held that the House has standing to press the appropriations claim but not the implementation claim, and reserved the merits. Article I, § 9, cl. 7 supplies the specific command; Article I, §§ 1 and 7 do not.

## Issue

The casebook arrives asking whether a President may answer an unconstitutional funding restriction by spending "money that Congress didn't authorize." Burwell asks who enforces it.

- Does the House have standing to sue over spending made without an appropriation under Article I, § 9?
- Does it have standing to sue over the alleged rewriting of the employer mandate?
- Are the surviving claims justiciable?

## Rule

The House of Representatives, as an institution, has Article III standing to sue executive officers for drawing money from the Treasury that Congress never appropriated, because an Appropriations Clause violation strips the House of its constitutional role. It has no standing to sue over the executive's allegedly unlawful implementation of a statute, even framed as usurpation of legislative power, because standing requires a violation of a specific constitutional command. A suit implicating another branch's constitutional conduct gets an "especially rigorous" standing analysis.

- "The House of Representatives as an institution would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the Treasury without a valid appropriation."
- An implementation claim is "fundamentally a statutory argument," and "citing its most general provisions does not transform a statutory violation into a constitutional case or controversy."
- "Article I is not a talisman."

## Operative facts

- **The ACA created two subsidies: the § 1401 Premium Tax Credit for beneficiaries with incomes from 100% to 400% of the federal poverty level, and the § 1402 Cost-Sharing Offset, under which insurers cut policyholders' out-of-pocket costs and the Treasury reimburses them.** *Only § 1402 is in suit, and only because of how it is funded.*
- **Section 1401 was funded by a permanent appropriation in the Internal Revenue Code, while § 1402, on the House's allegation, had to be funded and refunded by annual current appropriations.** *The court took the House's version as true, which makes the claim constitutional rather than statutory.*
- **Section 1513 imposed an "assessable payment" on any "applicable large employer" not offering full-time employees coverage, applicable "to months beginning after December 31, 2013."** *A date and a scope fixed by statute, so the rewriting claim is about obedience to a statute.*
- **Congress refused to fund the § 1402 offsets, and the House alleged that it "has not, and never has, appropriated any funds" for them.** *A deliberate refusal rather than a gap, so spending against it is constitutional; a dispute over scope would be statutory.*
- **Beginning in January 2014, the Secretaries drew and spent public money on the § 1402 program anyway.** *This is the injury: the money left the Treasury, so no political tool could undo it.*
- **Secretary Lew delayed the employer mandate past December 31, 2013 and altered the percentage of employees who must be offered coverage, which the House called "legislate[d] changes" that "usurp[ed] its Article I legislative authority."** *The House pleaded no clause but Article I, §§ 1 and 7, which is fatal.*
- **The House sued as an institution by vote, pressing Counts I–V on non-appropriation and Counts VI–VIII on the employer mandate.** *An institutional plaintiff distinguishes the member suits that* Raines v. Byrd *rejected.*
- **The Secretaries moved to dismiss for want of standing, urging the court to stay out of "a quintessentially political fight in which the House is already well armed" and pointing to "the elimination of funding" as the remedy.** *The House had eliminated the funding, and the executive "simply finds money elsewhere without consequence."*
- **The September 2015 opinion decided standing alone, the merits awaiting "another day."** *Everything said about the clause rests on assumed facts, so the opinion reads as half-deciding the merits.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (Collyer, J., for the District Court for the District of Columbia, sitting alone; motion to dismiss granted in part and denied in part)

- **Standing on the Non-Appropriation Theory.** "The House of Representatives as an institution would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the Treasury without a valid appropriation."
- **Only the constitutional counts survive.** Count III, under 31 U.S.C. § 1324, and Count IV, on the ACA's "statutory scheme," are statutory and dismissed.
- **No standing on the Employer-Mandate Theory.** It is "fundamentally a statutory argument," and "citing its most general provisions does not transform a statutory violation into a constitutional case or controversy."
- **Justiciable.** The surviving claims "involve pure questions of constitutional interpretation," and the House's status as plaintiff "does not turn this suit into a non-justiciable 'political' dispute."
- **Merits reserved.** "[O]nly the question of standing has been determined."

**Collyer, J., for the court**

- Distilled to their essences, one theory says that "the Executive was unfaithful to the Constitution," the other that it "was unfaithful to a statute." The line between them decides the case.
- The Secretaries' authorities about Congress supervising execution of the laws are inapposite, because the Non-Appropriation Theory "alleges a specific, constitutional violation that is wholly irrespective of the ACA's implementation."
- The injury is structural. The constitutional design "would collapse, and the role of the House would be meaningless, if the Executive could circumvent the appropriations process and spend funds however it pleases."
- The harm is "particularly insidious" because, if proved, "it would eliminate Congress's role via-a-vis the Executive," and "[t]he political tug of war anticipated by the Constitution depends upon Article I, § 9, cl. 7 having some force."
- Story, through the D.C. Circuit: without the clause "the executive would possess an unbounded power over the public purse of the nation." The purse "is the ultimate check on the otherwise unbounded power of the Executive."
- The employer-mandate theory "proves too much" and would create "general legislative standing."
- Justiciability needs no new tools. Familiar techniques suffice, and courts must sometimes read the Constitution "at variance with the construction given the document by another branch." The case "is inherently limited by the extraordinary facts of which it was born."

## Arguments rejected

- *The suit is about the executive's implementation of the ACA, and Congress has no standing to supervise execution of the laws.* Collyer: the Non-Appropriation Theory "is not about how Section 1402 is being applied, but rather how it is funded." The Secretaries used "implement" "no fewer than forty times."
- *The House has adequate political weapons, above all cutting off funding, so it suffers no cognizable injury.* The Secretaries were "apparently oblivious to the irony": defunding is what the House did, and if the executive may spend anyway, "the purse strings would be cut."
- *The branches have historically not sued each other, and this suit would upset the balance among them.* That Presidents never sued over the Tenure of Office Act shows only that an option went unused: "there will never be a history of litigation until the first lawsuit is filed."
- *Any executive action exceeding a statute usurps Congress's legislative power, so the House may sue over the employer mandate too.* The House's own argument, and "[t]he argument proves too much," since every extra-statutory act would then be a constitutional violation.

## Where it sits

**The section's second question.** [[United States v. Lovett (1946)|United States v. Lovett]] and [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] ask how Congress may limit the executive with the purse; Burwell asks how Congress enforces a limit the executive ignores. A court may hear the complaint, but only as a violation of the Appropriations Clause itself.

**What happened next.** On May 12, 2016, Collyer reached the merits, held that the Secretaries had violated Article I, § 9, and stayed her injunction pending appeal. The appeal was held in abeyance, the Trump administration stopped the payments in October 2017, and the case settled that December, so no appellate court ruled on the House's standing.

**Standing afterwards.** In *U.S. House of Representatives v. Mnuchin* Judge McFadden "decline[d] to apply Burwell," but the D.C. Circuit held in 2020 that the House had standing to litigate the clause. Contrast *Raines v. Byrd*, which denied standing to individual members challenging the Line Item Veto Act — struck a year later in [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)|Clinton v. City of New York]].

**Relation to Youngstown.** Spending against a refusal to appropriate would be category three in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] had the executive claimed an Article II power; it claimed instead that Congress had appropriated. The ACA's taxing theory returns in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]].

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "[W]hat is the court's fundamental concern here? What value would be served by denying the House the ability to sue? What value is served by allowing it to sue?"**

- The concern is that a clause of the Constitution would have no enforcer, since a spending bar that cannot be litigated binds nothing.
- Denying standing keeps courts out of interbranch politics and avoids general legislative standing.
- Allowing it protects the check the political tools cannot deliver, because cutting off funds is worthless if the executive spends anyway.

**Note 2. "Does the court's decision suggest that every misinterpretation of a spending statute is a violation of the Constitution? If so, is that rule too broad? If not, where does the court draw the line?"**

- No. The line runs between spending with no appropriation at all and spending under a contested one, which is why Count III was dismissed as statutory.
- Press it with the Secretaries' merits position, that § 1402 is payable from the § 1401 permanent appropriation. On that account the dispute is about an appropriation's scope, and so statutory.
- The court escapes by taking as true the allegation that Congress deliberately refused to appropriate, which comes close to deciding the merits in order to decide standing.

**Note 3. "In a subsequent opinion on May 12, 2016, the district court reached the merits and concluded that the secretaries . . . had indeed violated Article I, Section 9."**

- The injunction was stayed pending appeal, so nothing changed on the ground.
- The Trump administration then agreed the payments were unlawful, stopped them and settled, which left the standing holding unreviewed.

**Note 4. "But the D.C. Circuit disagreed, holding that the House did have standing to litigate the Appropriations Clause."**

- A judge of the same district court refused to follow Burwell on materially similar facts, which shows how thin a single standing opinion is as authority.
- The circuit's agreement makes the appropriations-versus-implementation line the governing one where such suits are brought, and the Supreme Court has still never passed on it.

### Further drilling

- **What gave the House standing.** Not that the executive broke the law, but that it spent money without an appropriation, taking away the one thing only Congress can give. Say why that injury belongs to the House as an institution.
- **Vary the plaintiff.** An individual member: *Raines* says no. Both chambers together: stronger. An insurer denied § 1402 payments: a classic private injury. A taxpayer: a generalized grievance.
- **Vary the conduct.** The executive refuses to spend money Congress appropriated. The clause bars drawing money without an appropriation; it does not require appropriated money to be drawn, so the Take Care Clause is the better frame.
- **Burwell and the signing statement.** Obama's § 2262 statement proposed paying advisers despite a funding bar. Had money been spent, Burwell's logic gives the House standing, with the constitutional defense going to the merits.
- **Memorable lines.** "Article I is not a talisman." "[A]pparently oblivious to the irony." "The argument proves too much." "[T]here will never be a history of litigation until the first lawsuit is filed."
