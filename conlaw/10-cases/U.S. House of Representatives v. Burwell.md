## Rule

The House of Representatives, as an institution, has Article III standing to sue executive
officers for spending money Congress never appropriated, because an Appropriations Clause
violation strips the House of its constitutional role. It has no standing to sue over the
executive's allegedly unlawful implementation of a statute, even when framed as usurpation of
legislative power.

## Facts

- **Congress refused to fund the § 1402 payments, and the executive paid them anyway.** The House
  alleged that Congress "has not, and never has, appropriated any funds" for the Section 1402
  Cost-Sharing Offsets, which need annual appropriations, and that the Secretaries paid insurers
  from the Treasury beginning in January 2014. *This is what makes the claim constitutional: money
  drawn without any appropriation, in the face of a deliberate refusal. Had Congress appropriated
  something and the dispute been over its scope, the claim would look statutory.*
- **The § 1401 credits had a permanent appropriation; § 1402 allegedly did not.** The House said
  § 1401 was funded through the Internal Revenue Code and § 1402 required current appropriations.
  *The Secretaries' merits position was that § 1402 is "a mandatory payment program that Congress
  has fully appropriated." The court held the merits for another day, and assumed the House's
  version for standing.*
- **The employer-mandate claim rested on Art. I, §§ 1 and 7 alone.** The House said Secretary Lew
  "legislate[d] changes" by delaying the mandate past December 31, 2013 and narrowing its
  coverage. *Citing only the general vesting and presentment provisions is why the claim failed:
  "Article I is not a talisman."*
- **The plaintiff was the House itself, by an institutional vote.** The House described the suit
  as "the result of an historic vote." *Institutional authorization distinguishes the case from
  suits by individual members, which [[Raines v. Byrd]] rejected. The court also relied on the
  suit's rarity to dismiss the floodgates concern.*
- **Defunding had already been tried.** The Secretaries argued the House had political remedies,
  chief among them "the elimination of funding." *The court turned the argument around: the House
  had eliminated funding, and the alleged injury was that the executive "simply finds money
  elsewhere without consequence."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Collyer, J., D.D.C.; motion to dismiss granted in part and denied in part)

- **Standing on the Non-Appropriation Theory.** "The House of Representatives as an institution
  would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the
  Treasury without a valid appropriation."
- **Only constitutional counts survive.** Count III (a claim under 31 U.S.C. § 1324) and Count IV
  (the ACA's "statutory scheme") are statutory and dismissed.
- **No standing on the Employer-Mandate Theory.** It is "fundamentally a statutory argument," and
  "citing its most general provisions does not transform a statutory violation into a
  constitutional case or controversy."
- **Justiciable.** The surviving claims "involve pure questions of constitutional interpretation,"
  and the House's status as plaintiff "does not turn this suit into a non-justiciable 'political'
  dispute."
- **Merits reserved.** "[O]nly the question of standing has been determined."

**Rejected**

- *The suit is about the executive's implementation of the ACA, and Congress has no standing to
  supervise how the executive executes the laws.* Collyer: the Non-Appropriation Theory "is not
  about how Section 1402 is being applied, but rather how it is funded." The Secretaries used
  "implement" "no fewer than forty times" and mischaracterized the claim.
- *The House has adequate political weapons, above all cutting off funding, so it suffers no
  judicially cognizable injury.* The Secretaries were "apparently oblivious to the irony":
  defunding was exactly what the House did, and if the executive can spend anyway, "the purse
  strings would be cut."
- *The branches have historically not sued each other, and allowing this suit would "upset the
  finely wrought balance" among them (drawing on [[Raines v. Byrd|Raines]] and Scalia's dissent in
  [[United States v. Windsor|Windsor]]).* Presidents' refusal to sue over the Tenure of Office Act
  shows only that an option went unused: "there will never be a history of litigation until the
  first lawsuit is filed." A dissent joined by two other Justices is not precedent.
- *Any executive action exceeding a statute usurps Congress's legislative power, so the House may
  sue over the employer mandate too.* The House's own argument. "The argument proves too much":
  every extra-statutory action would become a constitutional violation redressable by
  congressional suit, contrary to the rule against "general legislative standing."

## Context

**The second question of the section.** [[United States v. Lovett]] and
[[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] ask how Congress may limit the executive with the purse. Burwell
asks how Congress enforces a limit the executive ignores. The House's answer is a lawsuit; the
court's answer is that a court may hear it, but only when the claim is a violation of the
Appropriations Clause itself.

**What happened next.** The casebook's Note 3 reports the May 12, 2016 merits opinion: Collyer
held the Secretaries had violated Article I, § 9, would enjoin further § 1402 reimbursements until
a valid appropriation existed, and stayed the injunction pending appeal. Verified later history:
the D.C. Circuit stayed the appeal at the House's request on December 5, 2016, and it was held in
abeyance through the change of administration. The Trump administration stopped the cost-sharing
payments in October 2017, and the parties reached a settlement in December 2017 under which the
appeal was dismissed and the injunction dissolved. No appellate court ever ruled on the House's
standing in this case.

**The standing question afterwards.** Note 4: in [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Mnuchin]]
(border wall), Judge McFadden "decline[d] to apply Burwell" (D.D.C. 2019), but the D.C. Circuit
held in 2020 that the House had standing to litigate the Appropriations Clause. The Supreme Court
has never decided whether a House of Congress may sue the executive to enforce the clause.
Contrast [[Raines v. Byrd]] (1997), which denied standing to individual members challenging the
Line Item Veto Act, the statute struck the next year in [[Clinton v. City of New York]] at the
suit of injured private parties. Burwell also leans on
[[Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission]] (2015), where an
institutional legislature was allowed to sue. Standing returns in Article III.

**Relation to Youngstown.** Spending against an express refusal to appropriate would be category
three in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] if the President claimed an Article
II power, but here the executive claimed the reverse: that Congress had appropriated. That is why
the court could frame the case as a question of constitutional compliance. Douglas's concurrence
in Youngstown, that only the branch that can pay can authorize a taking, rests on the same clause.

**The statute on the merits.** The ACA and its taxing theory come back in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]],
which the opinion quotes for the ACA's purpose and for the "tax" characterization of the mandate's
payments.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Suit by the House of Representatives against the Secretaries of Health and Human Services and
  the Treasury and their departments, in the District Court for the District of Columbia. The
  Secretaries moved to dismiss for lack of standing.
- This opinion (September 2015) decides only standing and justiciability on the facts alleged. The
  merits opinion followed in May 2016; the casebook reports it in Note 3.

### Facts

Article I requires both authorization and appropriation before public money is spent. An
authorizing law establishes or continues a program; an appropriation "provides legal authority for
federal agencies to incur obligations and to make payments out of the Treasury." An appropriation
"must be expressly stated; it cannot be inferred or implied." A permanent or continuing
appropriation makes funds available indefinitely; a current appropriation must be renewed, usually
each year, and so ties the executive to Congress "via purse string."

The Affordable Care Act provides two subsidies at issue. The § 1401 Premium Tax Credit helps
people with household incomes from 100% to 400% of the poverty level buy insurance on the
exchanges. Section 1402 requires insurers to reduce out-of-pocket costs for eligible
policyholders, and the government reimburses insurers from the Treasury. The House alleged that §
1401 had a permanent appropriation in the Internal Revenue Code, that § 1402 needed annual
appropriations, that Congress never provided any, and that the Secretaries paid the § 1402
reimbursements anyway from January 2014.

Separately, § 1513 of the ACA subjects any "applicable large employer" that does not offer
full-time employees coverage to an "assessable payment," effective for months after December 31,
2013. The House alleged that Secretary Lew, through a Treasury rule and its preamble, delayed the
mandate beyond that date and reduced the percentage of employees who must be offered coverage.

The House sued as an institution. Counts I–V pressed the Non-Appropriation Theory, some under the
Constitution and some under statutes; Counts VI–VIII attacked the employer-mandate rule.

### Issue

- Does the House have standing to sue over spending allegedly made without an appropriation, in
  violation of Art. I, § 9, cl. 7?
- Does the House have standing to sue over the Secretary's alleged rewriting of the employer
  mandate?
- Are the claims for which the House has standing justiciable, or political questions?

### Holding

- Yes, for the constitutional counts. Drawing funds without an appropriation would deprive the
  House of its constitutional role, a concrete and particularized institutional injury; the
  statutory counts (III and IV) are dismissed.
- No. The employer-mandate claim alleges disobedience to a statute, not to a specific
  constitutional command, and general Article I provisions cannot convert it into a constitutional
  claim.
- Justiciable. The claims present "pure questions of constitutional interpretation" with
  manageable standards, and the House's identity as plaintiff does not make them political.

### Rule

- The House as an institution has standing to sue when the executive draws money from the Treasury
  without a valid appropriation.
- Standing requires a violation of a specific constitutional command; a claim that the executive
  exceeded or misread a statute does not qualify, even when framed under Art. I, §§ 1 and 7.
- Suits between the branches get an "especially rigorous" standing analysis
 
([[Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission|Arizona State Legislature]]).
- The political-question analysis follows [[Powell v. McCormack]].

### Reasoning

**(Collyer, J., for the court)**

- Distilled to their essences, one theory says "the Executive was unfaithful to the Constitution,"
  the other that it "was unfaithful to a statute." The line between them decides the case.
- The Secretaries' precedents about Congress supervising execution of the laws are inapposite,
  because the Non-Appropriation Theory "alleges a specific, constitutional violation that is
  wholly irrespective of the ACA's implementation."
- Injury: the constitutional structure "would collapse, and the role of the House would be
  meaningless, if the Executive could circumvent the appropriations process and spend funds
  however it pleases."
- The harm is "particularly insidious" because, if proved, "it would eliminate Congress's role
  via-a-vis the Executive." "The political tug of war anticipated by the Constitution depends upon
  Article I, § 9, cl. 7 having some force."
- Story, via the D.C. Circuit: without the clause "the executive would possess an unbounded power
  over the public purse of the nation." "Congress's power of the purse is the ultimate check on
  the otherwise unbounded power of the Executive."
- The employer-mandate theory "proves too much" and would create "general legislative standing."
  "Article I is not a talisman."
- Justiciability: familiar judicial techniques suffice ([[Marbury v. Madison|Marbury]]); courts
  must sometimes interpret the Constitution "at variance with the construction given the document
  by another branch" ([[Powell v. McCormack|Powell]]).
- No floodgates: the case "is inherently limited by the extraordinary facts of which it was born."

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; a single-judge district court opinion. The opposing view is the Secretaries', stated at
  its strongest in the Rejected bullets above, and Scalia's dissent in
  [[United States v. Windsor|Windsor]], which the court declines to follow.

### Cold-call notes

- **What exactly gave the House standing?** Not that the executive broke the law, but that it
  spent money without an appropriation, which takes away the one thing only Congress can give. Be
  ready to say why that injury belongs to the House as an institution and not to members or
  citizens.
- **Note 2: is every misreading of a spending statute a constitutional violation?** The court says
  no. Where Congress appropriated some money and the dispute is over whether a payment falls
  within it, the claim is statutory (Count III, under § 1324, was dismissed on that ground). The
  line is between spending with no appropriation and spending under a contested one. Test it: the
  executive pays § 1402 from the § 1401 permanent appropriation, claiming the statute covers both.
  The Secretaries said exactly that on the merits. Is that a constitutional or statutory claim?
  The court's answer is that the alleged deliberate refusal to appropriate makes it
  constitutional, which comes close to deciding the merits to decide standing.
- **Note 1: what values are at stake?** Denying standing keeps courts out of political fights,
  respects the separation-of-powers tradition that the branches settle disputes through political
  tools, and avoids "general legislative standing." Allowing it protects the one check the
  political tools cannot enforce, since cutting off funds is useless if the executive spends
  anyway.
- **Vary the plaintiff.** An individual member suing: [[Raines v. Byrd|Raines]] says no. Both
  Houses suing together: stronger. An insurer denied § 1402 payments: a classic private injury,
  and insurers later sued for the unpaid amounts. A taxpayer: a generalized grievance.
- **Vary the defendant's conduct.** The executive refuses to spend money Congress appropriated
  (impoundment). Is the House injured in the same way? The clause says money shall not be drawn
  without an appropriation; it does not say appropriated money must be drawn. The symmetry is
  imperfect, and the Take Care question of Class 8 is the better frame.
- **How does Burwell fit Youngstown?** If the President claimed a constitutional power to spend
  despite Congress's refusal, it would be category three, and a power to spend is the least
  plausible candidate for "conclusive and preclusive" authority given the Appropriations Clause.
  The executive in Burwell did not claim that power; it claimed statutory authorization, which is
  a category one argument.
- **Burwell and the signing statement.** Obama's § 2262 statement proposed paying advisers despite
  a funding bar on constitutional grounds. If the executive had spent money on those positions,
  would the House have standing under Burwell? On Burwell's logic, yes: no-money-
  without-appropriation is the specific command, and the executive's constitutional defense goes
  to the merits.
- **Know the aftermath.** Merits for the House (May 2016); appeal held in abeyance (December
  2016); the Trump administration stopped the payments (October 2017) and settled (December 2017),
  with the appeal dismissed. The D.C. Circuit later accepted House standing on the Appropriations
  Clause in [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Mnuchin|Mnuchin]] (2020). No Supreme Court ruling.
- **Formalism or functionalism?** The court's line is formal: a specific constitutional command
  yields standing, a general structural claim does not. Its justification is functional: without a
  suit the check would be empty. Be ready to argue the tension.
- **Memorable lines.** "Article I is not a talisman." "[A]pparently oblivious to the irony." "The
  argument proves too much." "[T]here will never be a history of litigation until the first
  lawsuit is filed." "Congress's power of the purse is the ultimate check on the otherwise
  unbounded power of the Executive."
