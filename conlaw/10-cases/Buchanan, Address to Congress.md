## What it is

President James Buchanan's annual message to Congress of December 3, 1860, sent a month after Lincoln's election and seventeen days before South Carolina seceded. The casebook sets it up with the Guarantee Clause question that runs through the whole class: was Lincoln bound "by his oath of office and the Take Care Clause to put down what he termed the War of the Rebellion," "Or was President Buchanan right that secession was unconstitutional, but the President—and even Congress—could do little about it?"

Buchanan's message is assigned as the first of the three positions in the secession crisis: secession is unlawful, and so is coercion. [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln's inaugural]] accepts the first half and rejects the second. [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]] rejects the first half. [[Texas v. White]] later adopts Lincoln's position as law.

## What to notice

- **Note 1 asks three questions, and Buchanan gives three different answers.** Morally, secession is not justified: Lincoln's election "does not of itself afford just cause for dissolving the Union," least of all when won by "a mere plurality." Legally, secession is not a constitutional right. Practically, neither the President nor Congress can stop it by force. Buchanan's answers are consistent only if the third question is about force against a *State*, not about enforcing federal law.
- **The test for revolution is borrowed from the compact theory.** Revolutionary resistance requires "a deliberate, palpable, and dangerous exercise" of powers not granted by the Constitution. That is the language of Madison's Virginia Resolutions of 1798, which Buchanan uses to justify revolution, not secession. The same words in the resolutions supported "interposition," which is the link Note 2 of the Lincoln reading presses.
- **The argument against a constitutional right to secede.** Secession would make the government "a mere voluntary association of States," and "the Confederacy is a rope of sand." The claimed right has no textual basis: "It is not pretended that any clause in the Constitution gives countenance to such a theory." It is inferred only "from the sovereign character of the several States." His answer is that a sovereign, "like an individual," may "yield a portion of its sovereign rights to secure the remainder."
- **Buchanan's Madison quotation puts the compact theory and the national theory in one sentence.** The Constitution "was formed by the States; that is, by the people in each of the States acting in their highest sovereign capacity." But the government it created "operates, like them directly on persons and things," and "has at command a physical force for executing the powers committed to it." The second half of the quotation is the best answer to Buchanan's own conclusion on coercion.
- **Perpetuity from the Articles and the Preamble.** The Articles declared "the Union shall be perpetual," and the Constitution was established "in order to form a more perfect union." Buchanan asks how a "more perfect union" could lack "the essential attribute of perpetuity." Lincoln and Chief Justice Chase in [[Texas v. White]] both make the same argument.
- **Secession versus revolution.** The right of resistance "exists independently of all constitutions" and is written into the Declaration of Independence. But "Secession is neither more nor less than revolution. It may or it may not be a justifiable revolution, but still it is revolution." [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]] claims the opposite: a constitutional right that is not revolution.
- **The Take Care duty meets impossibility.** The President is bound "to take care that the laws be faithfully executed." But in South Carolina every federal officer has resigned: "We no longer have a district judge, a district attorney, or a marshal in South Carolina." Buchanan concludes that the duty cannot be performed there without the officers who carry out the laws. Lincoln agrees on the offices ("no attempt to force obnoxious strangers among the people") but still holds federal property and collects duties.
- **The coercion argument.** "Has the Constitution delegated to Congress the power to coerce a State into submission which is attempting to withdraw or has actually withdrawn from the Confederacy?" Buchanan answers no. Such a power would be a power "to declare and to make war against a State"; it is not enumerated; it is not "necessary and proper" to any enumerated power. The Convention also rejected it: Madison said force against a State "would look more like a declaration of war than an infliction of punishment," and the clause was postponed.
- **The weakness of the coercion argument.** Buchanan treats the Union's response as war against a State as such. The Constitution's answer is to enforce federal law against individuals, backed by Congress's power to call forth the militia "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections" (Art. I, § 8, cl. 15) and the duty to protect each State against "domestic Violence" (Art. IV, § 4). Lincoln takes this route; [[The Prize Cases]] uphold the blockade on it.
- **The Reconstruction problem, stated in advance.** "Suppose such a war should result in the conquest of a State; how are we to govern it afterwards? Shall we hold it as a province and govern it by despotic power?" Buchanan saw no answer. [[Texas v. White]] finds one in the Guarantee Clause: the State never left, and Congress restores a republican government.
- **Prudence rather than law.** "[O]ur Union rests upon public opinion, and can never be cemented by the blood of its citizens shed in civil war." "Congress possesses many means of preserving it by conciliation, but the sword was not placed in their hand to preserve it by force." A court would not treat this as a constitutional argument; it is really an argument about policy.
- **How the Republicans answered.** The casebook reports (p. 773) that they mocked the message as saying "no state could secede—unless it wants to—and that the president has a duty to enforce the laws—unless somebody opposes him." It also notes evidence that Chief Justice Taney held the same no-coercion view. That is the premise of the Lincoln reading's Note 5 hypothetical about a Taney Court ruling for Virginia.
- **Is the position contradictory (Note 1)?** It is contradictory if a constitutional wrong must have a constitutional remedy: calling secession unlawful and then allowing it to take effect leaves the Constitution without a remedy for its own breach. It is coherent if the Constitution gives the Union no power over States as such, only over individuals. On that view Buchanan's error was not his theory of the Union but his refusal to use the power over individuals that the Constitution does give.
- **Hypothetical: South Carolina seizes the Charleston customs house.** Collecting duties is executing federal law against individuals, not coercing a State. Even on Buchanan's own theory the President could act. The line between "coercing a State" and "enforcing the laws" does the work in all three readings.

## Where it goes

- [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]] — the secession unlawful, coercion unlawful position; the Articles' "perpetual" Union and the Preamble's "more perfect union."
- [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]] — accepts Buchanan's conclusion on secession, rejects his conclusion on coercion, and reframes coercion as executing the laws.
- [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate]] — denies that secession is revolution; it is a right of State sovereignty.
- [[Texas v. White]] — adopts the perpetuity argument and answers the question Buchanan left open, how to govern a defeated State, through the Guarantee Clause.
- [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]] — a framework for placing Buchanan's hybrid position: the Constitution was made by the people of the States, yet it cannot be undone by any one of them.
