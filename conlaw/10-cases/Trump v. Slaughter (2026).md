## Snapshot

The President fired two Federal Trade Commissioners without alleging statutory cause; one sued to be restored to office and won below, and the Court stayed that ruling and granted certiorari before judgment. It held the FTC Act's for-cause removal provision contrary to the separation of powers, and left [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] standing at most as an observation about bodies exercising no executive power. Article II's Vesting and Take Care Clauses run against the FTC Act's removal limit.

## Issue

- Does the FTC Act's limit on removing commissioners to "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" violate the separation of powers?
- Does [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] require a different answer, and if so, should it be overruled?
- **Argued, not decided.** May a court prevent a removal from office, the second question on which certiorari was granted?

## Rule

An officer who exercises executive power must be removable by the President <u>at will</u>, whether the agency has one head or five. Rulemaking, investigation, in-house adjudication and enforcement litigation are each executive, so a for-cause limit protecting an officer who wields any of them is unconstitutional. What survives of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] is the observation that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the rule.

- "[T]he President may remove his subordinates at will"; those within his "general administrative control" must be removable at will.
- The character of the power governs, not the structure of the agency; even "the 'discretionary power to seek judicial relief'" is executive.
- There is no "quasi-legislative" or "quasi-judicial" power; "we long ago abandoned the notion that there are some powers that are only partly executive." An agency executing a statute against private parties "exercises executive power—no ifs, ands, or quasis about it."
- "If anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it."
- **Reserved:** offices with no executive or sovereign power, the Federal Reserve "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks," and tenure protection for judges of "non-Article III courts."

## Operative facts

- **Since 1914 the FTC Act has allowed removal of a commissioner only "for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office," and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] upheld that clause in 1935.** *The same words are at issue, so this is a direct challenge to the precedent, not an extension of it.*
- **The modern Commission administers "some 80 statutes" covering "almost every facet of our Nation's economy": it makes binding rules, investigates, adjudicates in house, and sues for civil penalties "in the billions of dollars."** *Decisive, because each power is executive under the modern cases; a body that only advised Congress could still fit what is left of Humphrey's.*
- **The FTC is a five-member commission with seven-year terms and a partisan-balance requirement.** *Irrelevant after this case: Seila Law turned on the single agency head, Slaughter on the kind of power exercised.*
- **January 2025: the second Trump term opened with two Republican and three Democratic commissioners; the President designated a new Chair, and the former Chair later resigned.** *That leaves the two Democrats as the only check on control of the agency.*
- **March 2025: the President removed those two, Rebecca Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya, telling them their service was "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities" and invoking Article II.** *No statutory ground was alleged, so the statute was squarely violated and the constitutional question could not be avoided.*
- **Slaughter sued for declaratory and injunctive relief restoring her to office, alleging that the removal was ultra vires and violated the APA and the Constitution.** *She sought her office rather than back pay, which is what puts the remedial question on the table.*
- **The District Court granted her summary judgment and enjoined interference with her duties, holding that *Myers* allows at-will removal of executive officers but that Humphrey's carved out an exception for the FTC.** *That exception is the only thing holding the judgment up.*
- **A divided D.C. Circuit denied the Government a stay, after which the Supreme Court stayed the District Court's order and granted certiorari before judgment.** *Slaughter was out of office while the appeal ran, so the Court reached the merits.*
- **Argued December 8, 2025; decided June 29, 2026, the same day as [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]].** *One opinion day produces the rule and its single historical exception.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (6–3; Roberts, C.J., for the Court, joined by Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh and Barrett, and by Thomas except as to Part III-B; Gorsuch concurring; Sotomayor dissenting, joined by Kagan and Jackson)

- **Unconstitutional.** The FTC's for-cause removal provision "is contrary to the separation of powers enshrined in the Constitution." Reversed and remanded.
- **Humphrey's goes.** "[A]ll that is left of Humphrey's is its observation that an agency that 'exercises no part of the executive power' need not fall within the rule," and "[i]f anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it."
- **Remedy not reached.** Part III-B reserves the Fed, non-Article III judges and powerless chartered bodies; Thomas joins everything but that reservation.

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court**

- **Why one President.** The States' executives were "little more than chairmen of their executive boards," and the Convention rejected that model. Randolph called "unity in the Executive magistracy" "the foetus of monarchy"; Wilson answered that unity was "the best safeguard against" monarchy. Pinckney: "Give him an able Council and it will thwart him; a weak one and he will shelter himself under their sanction."
- **Accountability is the point.** [[The Federalist No. 70]]: "a plurality in the executive . . . tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility," because no one can tell "on whom the blame or the punishment of a pernicious measure . . . ought really to fall."
- **Officers are deputies, not equals.** Federalist No. 72 makes the President's ministers "assistants or deputies of the Chief Magistrate . . . subject to his superintendence"; Iredell promised the President would "be personally responsible for everything."
- **Removal follows from both text and structure.** Text: Jefferson (1780) — "[h]e who appoints may remove." Structure: only removable deputies are truly "subordinate," so "the President had to be able to remove those who fail to live up to their duties, lest he fail to live up to his."
- **Liquidation.** The Decision of 1789 began "a regular course of practice" that "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the removal power (Madison), followed "thro all the vicissitudes of Party." [[The Decision of 1789]] statutes "implicitly endorsed the view that the President had a constitutional power to remove executive officers" (Bamzai & Prakash).
- **Humphrey's was fiction from the start.** It applied only to agencies with "no place in the executive department," a "highly circumscribed and almost fictional view of the FTC's role." [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]] put litigation on the executive side ("[a] lawsuit is the ultimate remedy for a breach of the law"); [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]] n.16 put delegated rulemaking there.
- **Humphrey's flunked its own test.** *Morrison* n.28 conceded the 1935 FTC's powers "would at the present time be considered 'executive,' at least to some degree."
- **What is left.** Only the "branchless agency" that exercises "no governmental power" (*Mistretta*, Scalia, J., dissenting). Congress may create independent bodies "to assist it with its functions," but "may not foist those agencies upon the President."
- **Reliance, turned around.** The dissent's search for reliance interests "somehow misses maybe the most important one: the reliance interests of the American people . . . in the preservation of our constitutionally promised liberties" — quoting *Ramos v. Louisiana* (Gorsuch, J., for a plurality including Sotomayor, J.). "When power is exercised well, the people know whom to thank; when power is exercised poorly, they know whom to blame—and whom to fire."
- **Stare decisis.** Only "blind feats of definition" made executive powers "quasi"; Humphrey's was applied once, in *Wiener*; and "no one knows how to apply Humphrey's in practice."
- **Application.** Enforcing and administering 80 statutes is "the very essence of 'execution' of the law" (*Bowsher*), so "this is not a close case." "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."
- **Part III-B, the reservations.** Not every chartered entity wields sovereign power: otherwise the Boy Scouts of America, the Society of American Florists and Ornamental Horticulturalists and Georgetown University would be executive agencies. *Myers* gave "the greatest weight" to early congressional practice, which is the hook for the Fed. Non-Article III judges pose "a different set of questions" (the Solicitor General at argument).

**Gorsuch, J., concurring** (not in the Canvas excerpt)

- Agrees with the holding but presses its consequences: independent agencies also exercise delegated legislative and judicial powers, and "after today's decision, the President can effectively exercise all those powers too."

**Sotomayor, J., dissenting (joined by Kagan and Jackson, JJ.)**

- **Framing.** Congress and Presidents together have long kept some functions — nuclear energy, the money supply, workplace and product safety — "at a distance from partisan politics." The Court "undoes centuries of political practice" and "gives the President a power unknown even to the English Crown."
- **Humphrey's controls.** A unanimous Court upheld this very statute, and the Court built on it: Jackson's *Youngstown* concurrence used Humphrey's as the lone example of the President's "lowest ebb," and *Wiener* (Frankfurter, J.) called it a "cause célèbre."
- **The FTC has not changed.** The 1935 FTC could already investigate, make rules and bring enforcement actions. If Congress later added too much power (civil penalties), the remedy is to sever those powers, not to adopt "a sweeping new rule."
- **Practice deserves weight.** "[L]ongstanding practice" gets "great weight" in separation-of-powers cases (*Mazars*), and practice "deliberately established" through "legislative acts" can "put at rest" the Constitution's meaning ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]).
- **Chadha is no model.** [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]] struck a practice the Court had never approved, and it rested on "[e]xplicit and unambiguous" text. Here the majority cites no clause requiring unfettered removal, only "structural inferences, legislative inaction, congressional debates, legal commentary, and private correspondence."
- **The Vesting Clause begs the question.** The issue is whether "executive Power" contains a removal power beyond Congress's power to "establis[h] by Law" offices under the Necessary and Proper Clause; agencies like the FTC "would not even exist" without Article I.
- **Take Care is a duty.** It "speaks of duty, not power" (*Seila Law*, Kagan, J.); at-will removal "might be one good way of fulfilling" that duty, not a rule Congress "could never modify."
- **1789 proves too much.** If text and structure already settled removal, "it is unclear what was left for the First Congress to 'decide,' much less spend days debating." *Ex parte Hennen* (1839) still called removal "a subject much disputed."
- **Accountability is one value among several.** Others are continuity and stability (Federalist No. 77's warning against a "general . . . revolution in the officers" at every election) and impartial administration (Madison wanted officers with judicial functions "responsible to the public generally").
- **Stare decisis and consequences.** FERC, the CPSC, the Chemical Safety Board, the NRC and the MSPB now likely become purely executive — "a profound bait and switch on a coequal branch."
- **Workability.** The majority's theory is "simultaneously all encompassing yet also subject to necessary but undefined exceptions":
    - Non-Article III adjudicators cannot be spared because they exercise judicial power, since agency adjudication is executive (*Arlington v. FCC* n.4), and *Wiener* rested on "[t]he philosophy of Humphrey's."
    - Inferior officers and civil servants have only *Perkins* (1886) left, the other of *Seila Law*'s "two exceptions"; the Government conceded its logic "extends to inferior officers."
    - The Fed exception raises its own questions: why only monetary policy, how close a historical analogue must be, whether "quasi-private" status matters, and what happens when Congress adds powers such as $1 million-a-day civil penalties.
- "The one thing that does appear to be clear going forward is that chaos will follow."
- Roberts's reply (outside the excerpt): "At least we have accorded Humphrey's a respectful burial; the dissent would cast Myers aside without a second thought."

## Arguments rejected

- *Humphrey's held that Congress may protect FTC commissioners, and Congress has built dozens of independent agencies on that holding for ninety years.* Roberts: Congress relied on Humphrey's to create agencies "insulated from presidential control," and "[t]hat is precisely the problem."
- *Ordinary Americans and regulated firms rely on independent agencies.* Roberts: the weightier reliance interest is the people's in their "constitutionally promised liberties," and "[t]he diffusion of power carries with it a diffusion of accountability" (*Free Enterprise Fund*).
- *The Decision of 1789 settled only a default removal power for cases of congressional silence.* Roberts: "The two, however, are one and the same" — removal lies as far outside Congress's control as the pardon or the veto.
- *The Federalist contemplated limits: tenure as a subject of legal regulation (No. 39), and Senate consent "to displace as well as to appoint" (No. 77).* Roberts: "two passing comments" cannot override the logic of The Federalist as a whole.
- *Practice deserves weight, from the First Congress's multimember bodies to the modern for-cause commissions.* Roberts: those early members "were removable by the President at will," and "[w]e have never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power."
- *Courts should review removal limits only for reasonableness, and Morrison "applied and expanded Humphrey's."* Roberts: that principle "is neither limiting nor much of a principle," and *Morrison* abandoned the "quasi" categories rather than extending them.

## Where it sits

**The end of a line that turned on structure.** *Seila Law* (2020) confined Humphrey's to "its own terms" and *Collins* (2021) applied it to another single-director agency, both resting on the single agency head. Slaughter rests on the character of the power instead, so the number of heads no longer matters.

**What survives.** *Morrison* is not overruled (holding an inferior officer can only be removed for good cause), and its rule for inferior officers with limited duties is untouched. *Perkins* (1886) protects inferior officers appointed by department heads. *Wiener* (1958), the only case ever to apply Humphrey's, involved "a solely adjudicatory body," and Part III-B reserves the judges of non-Article III courts.

**The exception rests on banks.** The Federal Reserve is reserved on a historical rationale tied to the First and Second Banks, whose constitutionality [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] upheld in Class 17. [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]], decided the same day, took up the reservation and upheld the Governors' protection.

**Method.** Part II-A argues from the Convention, The Federalist and the First Congress, Part II-B from precedent. These Class 7 readings follow Class 6's [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]], [[The Decision of 1789]], [[The Federalist No. 70]] and [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]].

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the Canvas excerpt.

- **What is left of Humphrey's?** Only its "observation" that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" need not be removable at will. The overruling is conditional in form and total in effect for any agency that enforces a statute.
- **Is the case distinguished from Humphrey's on the facts?** The majority says no: Humphrey's characterization of the 1935 FTC as quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial was always wrong, as *Morrison* n.28 conceded. Sotomayor's reply is the factual one — the 1935 FTC already investigated, made rules and sued — which cuts both ways: if nothing changed, Humphrey's is squarely on point, but the majority's point is precisely that those powers were executive in 1935 too.
- **Whose liquidation?** Both sides argue from settled practice. The majority's is Madison's: the founding-era "regular course of practice" "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" at-will removal. The dissent's is [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s: 140 years of deliberate legislative practice by Congresses and Presidents together "put at rest" Congress's power to limit removal. Baude has written on liquidation, so expect the doctrine's own conditions to be pressed:
	- Three elements needed for liquidation: (1) textual indeterminacy, (2) Deliberate Practice, (3) Constitutional Settlement 
    - Liquidation needs genuine indeterminacy first; the dissent's 1789 point is that the majority cannot claim both that text settled the question and that practice liquidated it.
    - It needs a deliberate course of practice; the majority's answer to the modern commissions is "[w]e have never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory."
    - It needs settlement; *Hennen*'s "much disputed" (1839) is the dissent's evidence that 1789 settled nothing.
    - The open question is whether a later liquidation can displace an earlier one.
- **Is a cause requirement really the same intrusion as Senate participation?** *Myers* involved a statute giving the Senate a role in removal; the FTC Act gives Congress no role and limits only the grounds. Slaughter treats the difference as immaterial, because either way officers exercising "the President's power" are not answerable to him.
- **Why is Chadha not a model?** The majority cites [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]] to show fifty years of practice can be unconstitutional. Sotomayor distinguishes it twice: Chadha struck a practice the Court had never blessed, and it rested on explicit bicameralism and presentment text, whereas no clause mentions removal.
- **Does Morrison still protect anyone?** Its holding for inferior officers with limited duties is untouched. Hypo: Congress protects an FTC administrative law judge from removal by the commissioners. *Morrison* governs, *Free Enterprise Fund* bars two layers, and because the commissioners are now at will, a single layer below them may survive. Sotomayor's warning is that nothing in the "chain of dependence" logic stops at principal officers.
- **Vary the agency.**
    - A commission that only investigates and reports to Congress exercises no executive power ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]], 424 U.S. at 137–138), so it may still be independent.
    - A congressionally chartered private body (the Boy Scouts, Georgetown) exercises no sovereign power and is outside the rule.
    - A purely adjudicatory claims body (*Wiener*) is arguably not executive, but *Arlington v. FCC* n.4 says agency adjudication is executive power, so the reservation needs another rationale.
    - Tax Court and Court of Federal Claims judges are expressly reserved.
    - NLRB and MSPB members enforce statutes and adjudicate, so they are removable at will.
- **Why does history save the Fed when text condemns the FTC?** *Myers* gave "the greatest weight" to early congressional exposition, and the First Congress chartered a bank not under "plenary Presidential control." The conditional "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition" leaves open whether bank supervision counts or only monetary policy. Thomas, who declined to join Part III-B, argues in [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]] that the Banks exercised no executive power.
- **Why would the severance remedy not have worked?** Sotomayor suggests stripping the post-1935 powers instead of the removal protection. The majority's answer is that the original powers were already executive, so severance would leave nothing worth protecting.
- **Formalism or functionalism?** Humphrey's was formalist in method with a functionalist fourth box, *Morrison* was openly functional, and Slaughter is formalist with a single box, though its accountability argument is functional at heart. Sotomayor's values list — continuity, impartiality — is the functionalist rebuttal. Gorsuch shows the cost: presidential control of delegated quasi-legislative power sharpens the nondelegation problem ([[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]]).
- **Memorable lines.**
    - "[T]he foetus of monarchy" (Randolph) against "the best safeguard against" it (Wilson).
    - "[H]e who appoints may remove" (Jefferson).
    - "[W]hom to thank . . . whom to blame—and whom to fire" and "[n]o ifs, ands, or quasis about it" (Slaughter, Roberts).
    - "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President" (Slaughter, Roberts).
    - "[A] power unknown even to the English Crown" and "chaos will follow" (Slaughter, Sotomayor dissenting).
