## What it is

Alexander Hamilton's essay of March 15, 1788, excerpted at pp. 169–172, defending the Constitution's choice of a single President against the proposals for a plural executive or an executive council. It is the founding text of the unitary-executive argument. The Supreme Court cites it for the proposition that a single, accountable President must control those who execute the laws, and the argument runs through the removal cases from [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] to [[Trump v. Slaughter]].

## What to notice

- **The thesis.** "Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government." Energy protects against "foreign attacks," secures "the steady administration of the laws," protects property, and secures liberty "against the enterprises and assaults of ambition, of faction, and of anarchy."
- **The Roman dictator.** Rome "was obliged to take refuge in the absolute power of a single man, under the formidable title of Dictator." Hamilton's point is that a republic without an energetic executive will end up with an unlimited one. It is the same logic as Jackson's Weimar warning in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]], used for the opposite purpose.
- **The syllogism.** "A feeble Executive implies a feeble execution of the government. A feeble execution is but another phrase for a bad execution; and a government ill executed, whatever it may be in theory, must be, in practice, a bad government."
- **The two lists.** Energy requires "first, unity; secondly, duration; thirdly, an adequate provision for its support; fourthly, competent powers." Safety requires "first, a due dependence on the people, secondly, a due responsibility." The essay's move is to show that unity serves both lists at once: it produces energy and it produces responsibility.
- **Unity and energy.** "Decision, activity, secrecy, and despatch will generally characterize the proceedings of one man in a much more eminent degree than the proceedings of any greater number."
- **Two ways to destroy unity.** Vesting power "in two or more magistrates of equal dignity and authority" (the Roman consuls), or vesting it "ostensibly in one man, subject, in whole or in part, to the control and co-operation of others, in the capacity of counsellors to him" (the state executive councils). Hamilton says the second is the more popular proposal and is open to "similar objections."
- **Why dissension is tolerable in the legislature and not in the executive.** In the legislature "promptitude of decision is oftener an evil than a benefit," and disagreement "often promote[s] deliberation and circumspection." Once a law passes, "the opposition must be at an end." In the executive the disadvantages of dissension "are pure and unmixed" and operate "from the first step to the final conclusion." Note the premise: the executive's job begins after deliberation ends. That premise is the root of the idea that execution is a distinct function.
- **The accountability argument, which does the most work in modern doctrine.** "[O]ne of the weightiest objections to a plurality in the Executive . . . is, that it tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility."
  - Responsibility is of two kinds, "to censure and to punishment," and censure "is the more important of the two, especially in an elective office."
  - Plurality lets blame be "shifted from one to another with so much dexterity" that "the public opinion is left in suspense about the real author."
  - The imagined excuse: "I was overruled by my council."
  - Plurality deprives the people of "the restraints of public opinion" and of "the opportunity of discovering with facility and clearness the misconduct of the persons they trust, in order either to their removal from office or to their actual punishment."
  - "[T]he executive power is more easily confined when it is One"; there should be "a single object for the jealousy and watchfulness of the people."
- **From the essay to removal doctrine.** Hamilton's argument is about the constitution of the office itself, not about subordinates. The unitary-executive position extends it one step: an officer the President cannot remove is a counsellor whose "control and co-operation" the President cannot escape, and the President can plead that his commission overruled him. That extension is the premise of Madison's and Ames's arguments in [[The Decision of 1789]] and of Taft's opinion in [[Myers v. United States|Myers]]. The question to press: does Hamilton's objection reach an officer Congress deliberately placed outside the chain of command, or only a council whose concurrence is "constitutionally necessary to the operations of the ostensible Executive"? The quoted phrase suggests the narrower reading. An independent agency acts in its own name; the President need not obtain its consent to anything he does.
- **The accountability argument cuts two ways.** Accountability to "public opinion" can support presidential control (voters can blame one person) or it can support independence (a commission shielded from the President cannot be used as a scapegoat, and the President cannot claim credit for its work). The Justices who defend [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] rely on expertise and neutrality instead; the unitary side answers with this essay.
- **Hamilton's closing appeal to experience.** He "rarely met with an intelligent man from any of the States, who did not admit . . . that the Unity of the executive of this State was one of the best of the distinguishing features of our constitution." New York had a single governor; the argument from state practice is the evidence behind the essay.
