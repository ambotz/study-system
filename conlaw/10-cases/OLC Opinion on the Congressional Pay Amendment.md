## What it is

*Congressional Pay Amendment*, 16 Op. O.L.C. 85 (Nov. 2, 1992), a memorandum opinion for the Counsel to the President signed by Timothy E. Flanigan, Acting Assistant Attorney General, Office of Legal Counsel. It memorializes the advice on which Archivist Don W. Wilson certified the Twenty-seventh Amendment, and it concludes two things: the Congressional Pay Amendment was validly ratified despite 203 years, and the Archivist was required to certify it under 1 U.S.C. § 106b without waiting for Congress.

The casebook prints it directly after [[Dillon v. Gloss]], and the pairing is the assignment: an executive-branch opinion telling the Supreme Court it was wrong, acted on, and never tested in court.

## What to notice

- **The straightforward reading.** Congress proposed the amendment and directed it to the state legislatures (Act of Sept. 23, 1789, ch. 27, 1 Stat. 97). "Three fourths of the several States have now ratified it. By a straightforward reading of Article V, the amendment is now 'valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of th[e] Constitution.'"
- **Text.** "Article V contains no time limits for ratification. It provides simply that amendments 'shall be valid to all Intents and Purposes when ratified.'"
- **Structure, and the best textual argument in the opinion.** Article V *does* address time, once and precisely: no amendment before 1808 affecting the first and fourth clauses of Article I, § 9. "If the Framers had contemplated some terminus of the period for ratification of amendments generally, they would have so stated."
- **History.** The records of drafting and ratification "contain no hint that Article V was intended to contain any implicit time limit," and in later congressional debates "the dominant view has been that the Constitution permits the ratification process to proceed for an unlimited period of time," with a proposal remaining viable "at least until rejected by more than one-fourth of the States."
- **Dillon is dictum, on Hughes's own authority.** In *Coleman v. Miller*, Hughes said Dillon's three considerations were "cogent reasons" for the holding about congressional power but that the discussion "was merely a dictum." OLC takes the concession and runs: the issue in Dillon "was Congress's authority to limit the period for ratification, not a State's authority to ratify a long-dormant proposed amendment."
- **Coleman is not authority either, and its logic cuts the other way.** Having declined to say what an implicit time limit contains, Coleman "leaves open for Congress the conclusion that there is no time limit at all."
- **The three rebuttals, point for point.** Single endeavor "simply assumes its conclusion that the process is to be short rather than lengthy." Proposal-on-necessity confuses two things: "necessity is not the same as emergency," and the states ratifying in the 1990s "evidently consider it to be just as necessary today." Contemporaneous consensus: "Consensus does not demand contemporaneity," and lasting consensus "may just as well be served by a process that allows for extended deliberation in the various states."
- **The methodological argument, which is the deepest one.** An amendment procedure "must provide a clear rule that is capable of mechanical application, without any need to inquire into the timeliness or substantive validity of the consensus achieved," so "any interpretation that would introduce confusion must be disfavored. . . . The alternative to procedural formalism is uncertainty and litigation."
- **Part II: who certifies.** The only obstacle to certification would be the theory that after Coleman "Congress alone among the branches may determine whether an amendment has been constitutionally adopted." OLC reads Coleman as resting on "a political question rationale" about courts, not about the relationship between the political branches: "At most, Coleman stands for the proposition that the validity of a constitutional amendment is a political question," which "has no bearing on the actions of the Archivist, an officer of one of the political branches."
- **Article V's text limits Congress's role.** It lets Congress propose, specify the mode of ratification, and call a convention on the states' application. "Nothing in Article V suggests that Congress has any further role," and amendments are valid "when ratified by" three-fourths of the states.
- **The structural argument against congressional promulgation.** Federalism and separation of powers exist "to ensure that the people, rather than any organ of the government, are sovereign," and giving one branch "ultimate control over the Constitution's very content would be to repudiate the American approach in favor of a return to parliamentary supremacy."
- **The practice argument, and the reductio.** If congressional promulgation were required, "the executive branch would have illegally certified every amendment except the Fourteenth."
- **The closing line to remember.** "The Congressional Pay Amendment — rather, the Twenty-Seventh Amendment — although well aged, is not stale."
- **What the notes press.** Note 1: "This is departmentalism in action. Do you find it troubling? How do you imagine the Court will react if it considers a time-limit question in the future?" There has never been a judicial decision on the Twenty-seventh Amendment "because Congress has not yet attempted to violate it." Note 2: "[I]sn't Dillon right that there is something fishy about adding an amendment without a real consensus . . . ? But isn't the O.L.C. opinion right that Article V does not say that?" Note 3: the last unratified Madison proposal, which would enlarge the House, may already be law if a "forgotten" 1792 Connecticut ratification is real — it would have made 12 of 15 states, so the amendment would have been "part of the Constitution for 224 years, and nobody has known about it!" "Is there some point at which it becomes too late to 'discover' a ratification vote?" The suits to enforce it failed on standing and political-question grounds without reaching the merits: *LaVergne v. Bryson*, 497 F. App'x 219 (3d Cir. 2012), and *LaVergne v. U.S. House of Representatives*, 847 F. App'x 1 (D.C. Cir. 2021) (claim previously litigated in the Third Circuit). Note 4: if anything is a political question, isn't this it — and yet "no branch of government can be trusted with the power to reject amendments, since amendments are a key way for the people to curb the government's power."
- **The stakes, per Notes 5 and 6.** The Fourteenth Amendment was ratified after Congress replaced Southern governments or conditioned readmission on ratification (Amar's Article IV answer), and the denominator question for the Thirteenth and Fourteenth turns on whether seceded states count.
