## Snapshot

Congress required most Americans to carry health insurance or pay the IRS, and expanded Medicaid on pain of losing all existing funds. Twenty-six States, a small-business federation and individuals sued; the circuits split. The Court held the mandate beyond the commerce and necessary-and-proper powers but valid as a tax, and the Medicaid threat unconstitutionally coercive, barring the threat alone. In play: Article I, § 8, cls. 1, 3 and 18, and the Direct Tax Clause.

## Issue

- Does the Anti-Injunction Act bar a pre-enforcement suit?
- Does the Commerce Clause let Congress require individuals to buy insurance?
- If not, is the mandate "proper" under the Necessary and Proper Clause?
- May it be sustained as a tax, and is that tax direct?
- Is the threat to existing Medicaid funds coercive, and what remedy follows?
- **Argued, not decided.** Severability of the mandate.

## Rule

The commerce power presupposes activity, so Congress may not compel individuals into a market, and a law creating the predicate for federal regulation is not a "proper" means. Whether an exaction is a constitutional tax is functional, not nominal. A condition threatening a State's existing grants to force a new program upon it is compulsion; the remedy bars the threat, not the program.

- "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated"; Congress reaches "class[es] of activities," not classes of individuals.
- Creating "the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not "proper," even if "necessary."
- Penalty signals: a "prohibitory" amount, scienter, a non-revenue enforcing agency, "punishment for an unlawful act or omission."
- A condition fails when "pressure turns into compulsion" by threatening "other significant independent grants."

## Operative facts

- **Medicaid has always reserved to Congress the power to amend its terms, and § 1396c lets the Secretary withhold all of a State's federal Medicaid funds for noncompliance.** *The all-or-nothing tool predates the Act; without it no coercion question arises.*
- **The Affordable Care Act of 2010 barred insurers from refusing or surcharging the sick — "guaranteed issue" and "community rating."** *Concededly valid commerce regulation, and the predicate the Necessary and Proper argument needed.*
- **Those reforms invited people to wait until they were sick to buy, so Congress added the mandate, requiring most Americans, healthy people outside the market above all, to keep "minimum essential" coverage.** *Congress created the adverse selection the mandate cures, and the target is inactivity — the Government's counter-framing of the uninsured as "active in the market for health care" is the whole fight.*
- **The only consequence, from 2014, was a "[s]hared responsibility payment" collected by the IRS: a share of household income, capped at the average premium, with no scienter and no criminal prosecution.** *Each feature answers a [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Drexel Furniture]] factor.*
- **Congress called it a "penalty" eighteen times, titled § 5000A "Requirement to maintain minimum essential coverage," and placed it in Title I, not Title IX's revenue provisions.** *Controlling for the Anti-Injunction Act, which follows the label, and the joint dissent's whole case.*
- **The Act expanded Medicaid to all adults below an income threshold, the federal government paying most of the new cost, and § 1396c put every existing Medicaid dollar at risk for a State that refused.** *The hinge of Part IV: old money risked to force a new program.*
- **Medicaid is over 20 percent of the average State's budget, and the money threatened exceeded 10 percent of a State's whole budget.** *[[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] risked under half of one percent of South Dakota's budget; scale is what makes it "a gun to the head."*
- **The Eleventh Circuit held the mandate unconstitutional but severable and upheld the expansion, other circuits split, and the Government pleaded the taxing power in the alternative.** *That alternative is what the saving construction needed; severability was never reached.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (Roberts, C.J., announcing the judgment). Anti-Injunction Act, unanimous. Commerce and Necessary and Proper, five Justices in two opinions — Roberts in Part III-A for himself, and Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Alito jointly — over Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan. Taxing power, 5–4 for the Court in Part III-C: Roberts with Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan. Medicaid coercion, 7–2: Roberts, Breyer and Kagan plus the joint dissenters, over Ginsburg and Sotomayor. Remedy, 5–4: that plurality, with Ginsburg and Sotomayor concurring in the judgment.

- **Anti-Injunction Act: no bar.** The suit may proceed.
- **Commerce and Necessary and Proper: no.** The Clause "authorizes Congress to regulate interstate commerce, not to order individuals to engage in it."
- **Taxing power: yes.** The payment "may reasonably be characterized as a tax" and is no direct tax.
- **Medicaid coercion: yes.** The threat to existing funds is "economic dragooning."
- **Remedy: the threat alone falls.** § 1396c cannot be applied "to withdraw existing Medicaid funds."

**Roberts, C.J., for himself (commerce, necessary and proper)**

- "Regulate" assumes something already there: Congress "has never attempted to rely on that power to compel individuals not engaged in commerce to purchase an unwanted product."
- Implied powers must be "incidental," not "great substantive and independent power[s]" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]); a law outside "the letter and spirit of the constitution" is not "proper."

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court (tax)**

- The payment "looks like a tax in many respects": owed with the return, sited in the Internal Revenue Code, collected by the IRS, and revenue-raising.
- The *Drexel Furniture* factors run the other way, and "Congress did not think it was creating four million outlaws."

**Roberts, C.J., with Breyer and Kagan, JJ. (Medicaid)**

- Coercion follows from anti-commandeering: what Congress cannot command, it cannot compel indirectly. The expansion being a new program in substance, States "must either accept a basic change in the nature of Medicaid, or risk losing all Medicaid funding."

**Ginsburg, J., concurring and dissenting in part**

- The uninsured "as a class" consume billions in care they cannot pay for, which makes forgoing insurance "an economic decision Congress has the authority to address." The markets existed already, so "the 'something to be regulated' was surely there."

**The joint dissent (Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Alito, JJ.)**

- "If all inactivity affecting commerce is commerce, commerce is everything." On the tax: "[t]he issue is not whether Congress had the power to frame the minimum-coverage provision as a tax, but whether it did so." They would strike the whole Act.

**Thomas, J., dissenting**

- Omitted from the casebook. He joined the joint dissent and wrote separately against the "substantial effects" test.

## Arguments rejected

- *The uninsured are all in the health-care market: everyone eventually needs care and shifts its cost.* Roberts: being "active in the market" "has no constitutional significance," and "cars and broccoli are no more purchased for their 'own sake' than health insurance."
- *The mandate is an "integral part of a comprehensive scheme of economic regulation," as in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]].* Roberts: Raich concerned "individual applications of a concededly valid statutory scheme"; what is "necessary" may yet not be "proper."
- *Congress called the payment a penalty, so the Court cannot rewrite it as a tax; and a tax on inactivity is as troubling as a regulation of it.* Roberts: a $50 IRS payment for a house without energy-efficient windows is plainly a tax whatever its name, and "the Constitution does not guarantee that individuals may avoid taxation through inactivity."
- *Medicaid as amended is one program Congress may change, and if the threat is bad the whole Act falls.* Roberts: the expansion and existing Medicaid are not one program "simply because 'Congress styled' them as such," and striking one application "merely enforc[es] the Constitution."

## Where it sits

**Synthesis of the unit.** NFIB applies every earlier reading: the commerce power of [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]], [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]] and [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]; the Necessary and Proper Clause of [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]; the taxing power of [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]; and the conditional spending power of [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|South Dakota v. Dole]].

**A new kind of commerce limit.** [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] limits the power by the kind of activity; NFIB by the existence of activity. Wickard's farmer grew wheat, Raich's grower marijuana; the uninsured did nothing.

**Drexel Furniture reversed.** [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Drexel Furniture]] held that something called a tax was really a penalty; NFIB holds the converse on the same three factors, and confines direct taxes to capitations and property taxes ([[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton]]).

**Coercive spending, and after.** The first holding that a spending condition is coercive: what *New York v. United States* and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] forbid directly, coercive spending does indirectly. Congress zeroed out the payment in 2017, and *California v. Texas* (2021) dismissed the challenge on standing. The sequence begun with [[The Bank Debate]] closes here, before [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]].

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Was there in fact majority support for all of the constitutional conclusions set forth in Chief Justice Roberts's opinion? . . . Does that holding make the Commerce Clause part . . . dictum?"**

- Dictum, says Ginsburg: the tax holding carried the judgment. A necessary step, says Roberts: the saving construction opens only once the straightforward reading fails. Commerce drew five Justices and no opinion of the Court.

**Note 2. "Was this a proper use of the constitutional avoidance doctrine?"**

- The objection is about sequence: avoidance normally lets a court skip the constitutional question; here the Court decided it first. Roberts answers that the test is only whether the tax reading is "fairly possible."

**Note 3. "Is there any good answer to her argument that there is no coherent difference between regulating 'activity' and 'inactivity'?"**

- His answer is textual, not consequentialist: effects do not supply an object of regulation, and a power over whatever affects commerce is the police power renamed.
- He runs text, precedent, history and consequences; Ginsburg purpose and structure ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).

**Note 4. "[D]oes it explain why the framers thought it essential to spell out such powers as taxing and spending . . . but not . . . condemning land through eminent domain?"**

- The "great substantive and independent powers" idea is a middle ground between a clause permitting everything and one adding nothing — Baude for it, Harrison against. No long-exercised power looks less great than the mandate.

**Note 5. "Who needs the Commerce Clause? . . . Is the Taxing Clause power a Trojan horse for federalism?"**

- A $20 IRS surcharge on people who do not buy broccoli is plausibly valid under Part III-C: modest, scienter-free, IRS-collected, a lawful choice. Roberts's limit is thin: the tax power compels only payment.
- Basketball (5b) turns on whether the law declares the conduct unlawful: he reads § 5000A as a price, the dissent as a rule. Labels cannot enlarge the power (5e); press the head-tax reading (5d); zeroed out, it raises nothing (5f).

**Note 6. "What did the Court identify as the essential features . . . that make them coercive? Where is the line?"**

- Both the size and the threat to existing funds matter, and he will not locate the line. A new grant threatening no old money is safe; a 5 percent threat resembles Dole.
- Germaneness does no work, expanding coverage being germane on any loose reading; and the rule, resting on state sovereignty, spares private grantees.

### Further drilling

- **Broccoli ordered, backed by a fine.** Invalid as a regulation of inactivity under Roberts and the joint dissent, and under Ginsburg too, for want of free riding.
- **The payment called a "tax" but set at five times the price of insurance.** Unconstitutional, a prohibitory amount being the first [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Drexel]] factor — yet the label triggers the Anti-Injunction Act, so the challenger pays and sues.
- **Formalism and functionalism change sides.** Roberts is formal on commerce and functional on the tax; Ginsburg the reverse, and on spending she defers to the label he looks past.
- **Memorable lines.** "A gun to the head"; "economic dragooning"; "four million outlaws"; "the broccoli horrible."
