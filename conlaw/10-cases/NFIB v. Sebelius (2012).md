## Snapshot

The Affordable Care Act had four moving parts, and the Court answered each differently. The insurance reforms — guaranteed issue and community rating — were conceded valid commerce regulation. (1) The individual mandate failed the commerce power ("[t]hat Clause authorizes Congress to regulate interstate commerce, not to order individuals to engage in it") and failed the Necessary and Proper Clause, but was saved 5–4 as a tax, because the only thing that happens if you disobey is an IRS payment. (2) The Medicaid expansion was upheld; what fell, 7–2, was the enforcement threat behind it — § 1396c's power to strip a refusing State of its *existing* Medicaid funds, "a gun to the head." No part of Roberts's opinion commanded five votes on every point, which is the case's other lesson.

## Issue

- Does the Anti-Injunction Act bar a pre-enforcement suit?
- Does the Commerce Clause let Congress require individuals to buy insurance?
- If not, is the mandate "proper" under the Necessary and Proper Clause?
- May it be sustained as a tax, and if so, is that tax direct?
- Is the threat to existing Medicaid funds coercive, and what is the remedy?
- **Argued, not decided.** Severability of the mandate from the rest of the Act.

## Rule

The commerce power presupposes activity, so Congress may not compel individuals into a market, and a law creating the predicate for federal regulation is not "proper" however necessary. Whether an exaction is a constitutional tax is functional, not nominal. And a condition that threatens a State's *existing* grants to force a *new* program upon it is compulsion; the remedy bars the threat, not the program.

- **Commerce.** "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated." Congress reaches "class[es] of activities," not classes of individuals "apart from any activity in which they are engaged."
- **Necessary and proper.** Implied powers must be "incidental," not "great substantive and independent power[s]"; creating "the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not proper, even if necessary.
- **Tax.** Three *Drexel Furniture* signals of a penalty: a "prohibitory" amount, a scienter requirement, and enforcement by a non-revenue agency. Backstop definition: "if the concept of penalty means anything, it means punishment for an unlawful act or omission."
- **Direct tax.** Only capitations and taxes on land or personal property; this is neither, being triggered by "specific circumstances."
- **Spending.** A grant condition fails when "pressure turns into compulsion," which is what happens when a condition "take[s] the form of threats to terminate other significant independent grants." The Court declines to "fix the outermost line."

## Operative facts

**The problem Congress was solving**

- **Everyone eventually needs care at an unpredictable time; federal and state law (EMTALA, 42 U.S.C. § 1395dd) require hospitals to treat regardless of ability to pay; hospitals recoup from insurers, who recoup from policyholders.** *Cost-shifting is the Government's entire causal story, and Roberts accepts it as fact while denying it supplies an object of regulation.*
- **A State going it alone would become "bait to the needy and dependent elsewhere," so no State moves first.** *Ginsburg's collective-action framing, borrowed from [[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering v. Davis]] — the structural argument Roberts never answers on its own terms.*

**Part one — the insurance reforms (conceded valid)**

- **"Guaranteed issue" bars insurers from denying coverage for a preexisting condition; "community rating" bars charging the sick higher premiums.** *Indisputably a regulation of commerce. This concession is what makes the Necessary and Proper argument available at all — there must be a valid scheme for the mandate to be "necessary" to.*
- **The reforms "sharply exacerbate" the healthy-person problem: they invite people to wait until they are sick to buy. Seven States tried guaranteed issue and community rating without a mandate, and insurers raised premiums or left.** *Congress manufactured the adverse-selection death spiral the mandate cures — which is simultaneously the best argument for the mandate and Roberts's reason for calling it bootstrapping.*

**Part two — the mandate, 26 U.S.C. § 5000A (invalid under commerce power, valid as a tax)**

- **§ 5000A, titled "Requirement to maintain minimum essential coverage," commands that every "applicable individual *shall* . . . ensure that the individual . . . is covered under minimum essential coverage," beginning in 2014.** *The mandatory verb is why Roberts concedes the "most straightforward reading" is a command — and why he must invoke the saving construction rather than simply read the statute.*
- **It "primarily affects healthy, often young adults," who as an actuarial class incur low costs; that is precisely why they are needed in the pool.** *"If the individual mandate is targeted at a class, it is a class whose commercial inactivity rather than activity is its defining feature." The target's inactivity is not incidental to the design — it is the design.*
- **Some individuals are exempt from the payment who are not exempt from the mandate.** *The joint dissent's sharpest textual point: the asymmetry only makes sense if there is a freestanding command, separate from the exaction.*

	**Part three — the shared responsibility payment** (*Tax; not penalty*)
	
	- **The only legal consequence of noncompliance is a payment to the IRS with your return. No criminal prosecution, no lien or levy of the punitive sort, no other "negative legal consequences."** *This single fact does all the work in Part III-C: the mandate becomes a price rather than a rule.*
	- **The amount is a percentage of household income with a dollar floor and a ceiling at the average annual premium — in 2016, 2.5% of household income, no less than $695, no more than the average premium for a plan covering 60% of the cost of ten specified services.** *Capped by design at less than the price of insurance, so it is never "prohibitory" in the [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Drexel Furniture]] sense. Set it at five times the premium and the case flips.*
	- **It does not apply to those below the income-tax filing threshold, and the amount turns on taxable income, dependents and joint filing status.** *Reads off the income tax return, which is also why it is not a capitation ("taxes paid by every person, without regard to . . . any other circumstance," [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton]]).*
	- **No scienter requirement; collected solely by the IRS "in the same manner as taxes."** *Two of the three Drexel factors, answered.*
	- **It was projected to raise about $4 billion a year by 2017, and roughly four million people a year were expected to pay rather than buy.** *Revenue is "the essential feature of any tax" ([[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]]); and the four million figure yields "Congress did not think it was creating four million outlaws."*

**The labels Congress chose**

- **Congress called it a "penalty" eighteen times in § 5000A and elsewhere, and placed the provision in Title I — the Act's operative core — rather than Title IX, the "Revenue Provisions."** *The joint dissent's whole case ("the nail in the coffin"), and the reason the Anti-Injunction Act does not apply. Roberts's answer: the label controls the AIA because Congress is master of its own statutes, but cannot shrink or enlarge Congress's constitutional powers.*

**Part four — the Medicaid expansion and the § 1396c threat (conditioning of funds such that it's coercive)**

- **Medicaid has always reserved to Congress the power to amend its terms, and § 1396c already let the Secretary declare that "further payments will not be made to the State" for noncompliance.** *The all-or-nothing tool predates the Act. Without it there is no coercion question — and with it, Ginsburg's notice argument ("express statutory warning") gets real traction.*
- **The Act extended coverage to all adults below an income threshold, with the federal government paying nearly all of the new cost, and left § 1396c in place as the enforcement mechanism.** *The hinge of Part IV: old money risked to compel a new program. Note the oddity — the inducement was generous; it is the downside, not the offer, that is unconstitutional.*
- **Medicaid is over 20 percent of the average State's total budget, with federal funds covering 50 to 83 percent of those costs; the threatened loss exceeded 10 percent of a State's whole budget.** *Scale is what makes it "economic dragooning." Compare [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]]: 5% of highway funds, which was "less than half of one percent of South Dakota's budget."*
- **The condition did not govern the use of the expansion funds; it threatened a separate pot of money.** *The doctrinal move that distinguishes a real condition from leverage — "[c]onditions that do not here govern the use of the funds . . . cannot be justified on that basis."*

**Posture**

- **Twenty-six States, the NFIB and individual plaintiffs sued pre-enforcement; the circuits split; the Eleventh Circuit held the mandate unconstitutional but severable and upheld the expansion.** *A pre-enforcement challenge is only possible because the AIA does not apply — the label cuts for the challengers here.*
- **The Government pleaded the taxing power in the alternative, and devoted 21 lines of its reply brief and about 50 words of oral argument to the Direct Tax Clause.** *The alternative pleading is what the saving construction needed; the thin briefing is the dissent's complaint about deciding "a difficult constitutional question of first impression" on "fly-by-night" argument.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (Roberts, C.J., announcing the judgment).

- **Anti-Injunction Act: no bar** (unanimous). Congress called it a penalty, and the label controls for this statute.
- **Commerce and Necessary and Proper: no** (five Justices, two opinions, no opinion of the Court — Roberts in Part III-A for himself alone, plus the four joint dissenters).
- **Taxing power: yes** (5–4, and a true opinion of the Court in Part III-C — Roberts with Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan).
- **Medicaid coercion: yes** (7–2 — Roberts, Breyer and Kagan plus the four joint dissenters, over Ginsburg and Sotomayor).
- **Remedy — the threat alone falls** (5–4 — the Roberts/Breyer/Kagan plurality, with Ginsburg and Sotomayor concurring in the judgment to supply the majority). The Secretary "cannot apply § 1396c to withdraw existing Medicaid funds."

*Only Roberts agreed with the entirety of the reasoning and the judgment. Hold onto that: it drives Note 1 and the dictum problem.*

**Roberts, C.J., for himself (commerce, necessary and proper)** regarding mandate

- *Text.* "Regulate" assumes an existing object. If regulating included creating, "coin Money"/"regulate the Value thereof" and "raise and support Armies"/"make Rules for the . . . Regulation of the land and naval Forces" would be redundant.
- *Precedent.* Every case "uniformly describe[s] the power as reaching 'activity.'" Wickard's farmer grew wheat; Raich's grower grew marijuana.
- *History.* Congress "has never attempted to rely on that power to compel individuals not engaged in commerce to purchase an unwanted product," and "the most telling indication of [a] severe constitutional problem . . . is the lack of historical precedent" ([[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]]).
- *Consequences.* Under the Government's logic, "Congress could address the diet problem by ordering everyone to buy vegetables." The Commerce Clause "is not a general license to regulate an individual from cradle to grave."
- *Necessary and proper.* A law that is not "consist[ent] with the letter and spirit of the constitution" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]) is not proper; the mandate "vests Congress with the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power."

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court (tax)** regarding mandate

- The saving construction standard is low: not "the most natural interpretation" but merely a "fairly possible" one (*Crowell v. Benson*).
- The exaction "looks like a tax in many respects": paid to the Treasury by taxpayers with their returns, sited in the Internal Revenue Code, collected by the IRS, exempting those below the filing threshold, and producing revenue.
- The *Drexel Furniture* factors run the other way on all three. "It may often be a reasonable financial decision to make the payment rather than purchase insurance."
- Regulatory purpose is no objection — tariffs and cigarette taxes are taxes.
- Three limits preserve the distinction: upholding this "does not recognize any new federal power"; an exaction can still become "a mere penalty"; and the taxing power "is limited to requiring an individual to pay money into the Federal Treasury, no more."

**Roberts, C.J., with Breyer and Kagan, JJ. (Medicaid)** expansion

- Spending conditions are "much in the nature of a contract" (*Barnes v. Gorman*), so legitimacy rests on the State "voluntarily and knowingly" accepting the terms (*Pennhurst*).
- The coercion limit descends from anti-commandeering: what Congress cannot command under *[[New York v. United States|New York]]* and *[[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]*, it cannot compel indirectly. States "must either accept a basic change in the nature of Medicaid, or risk losing all Medicaid funding."
- *Remedy.* Invalidating one application and following § 1303's severability instruction is not "rewriting" — "we are merely enforcing the Constitution."

**Ginsburg, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part** (joined in full by Sotomayor; by Breyer and Kagan as to Parts I–IV)

- The uninsured "as a class" consume billions in care annually and drive up prices, so forgoing insurance "is hardly inconsequential or equivalent to 'doing nothing'; it is, instead, an economic decision Congress has the authority to address."
- Proximity is real: "more than 60% of those without insurance visit a hospital or doctor's office each year. Nearly 90% will within five years."
- The markets long predated the Act, so "the 'something to be regulated' was surely there."
- Roberts's rule is unadministrable: "The instruction The Chief Justice, in effect, provides lower courts: You will know it when you see it."
- The opinion is "stunningly retrogressive" and "[t]his Court's former endeavors to impose categorical limits on the commerce power have not fared well."

**The joint dissent (Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Alito, JJ.)**

- *Commerce.* "If all inactivity affecting commerce is commerce, commerce is everything." Wickard is "the *ne plus ultra*"; going further "is to make mere breathing in and out the basis for federal prescription."
- *Structure.* "[The Constitution] enumerates not federally soluble problems, but federally available powers. . . . Article I contains no whatever-it-takes-to-solve-a-national-problem power."
- *Tax.* "The issue is not whether Congress had the power to frame the minimum-coverage provision as a tax, but whether it did so." And: "we have never held — never — that a penalty imposed for violation of the law was so trivial as to be in effect a tax."
- *Remedy and severability.* "The Court today decides to save a statute Congress did not write." They would strike the mandate and the expansion and, both being central, the entire Act.

**Thomas, J., dissenting**

- Omitted from the casebook. He joined the joint dissent and wrote separately to renew his attack on the "substantial effects" test.

## Arguments rejected

- *The uninsured are all "active in the market for health care."* Roberts: the phrase "has no constitutional significance"; "[a]n individual who bought a car two years ago and may buy another in the future is not 'active in the car market' in any pertinent sense."
- *Health insurance is unique, so upholding the mandate implies nothing about cars or broccoli.* Roberts: "cars and broccoli are no more purchased for their 'own sake' than health insurance. They are purchased to cover the need for transportation and food." (Ginsburg's counter is the free-rider asymmetry: at the broccoli counter "she will get no free ride or food, at the expense of another consumer forced to pay an inflated price.")
- *The mandate is an "integral part of a comprehensive scheme of economic regulation," as in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]].* Roberts: Raich concerned "individual applications of a concededly valid statutory scheme," not a "great substantive and independent power." What is necessary may still not be proper.
- *Congress called it a penalty, so the Court may not rewrite it as a tax.* Roberts: a $50 IRS payment for a house without energy-efficient windows would plainly be a tax whatever it were called; reading it as a tax "would give practical effect to the Legislature's enactment."
- *A tax on inactivity is as troubling as a regulation of it.* Roberts: "the Constitution does not guarantee that individuals may avoid taxation through inactivity" — a capitation is "a tax that everyone must pay simply for existing," and capitations are expressly contemplated.
- *Medicaid as amended is one program Congress has always been free to change.* Roberts: existing Medicaid and the expansion are not one program "simply because 'Congress styled' them as such." (Ginsburg: States "have no entitlement to receive any Medicaid funds," and "[f]uture Congresses are not bound by their predecessors' dispositions.")
- *The whole Act must fall with the mandate.* Not reached as to the mandate; rejected as to Medicaid, where severing the application suffices.

## Where it sits

**Synthesis of the unit.** NFIB runs every earlier reading at once: the commerce power of [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]], [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] and [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]]; the Necessary and Proper Clause of [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]; the taxing power of [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Drexel Furniture]] and [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]]; the direct-tax line from [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton]] through Pollock and the Sixteenth Amendment; and conditional spending from [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]] through [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]]. If one case is the capstone of federalism, it is this one.

**A new kind of commerce limit.** [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] limits the power by the *kind* of activity (economic or not); NFIB by the *existence* of activity. The two limits are independent, and a statute must now clear both.

**Drexel Furniture, inverted.** [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Drexel Furniture]] held that something Congress called a tax was really a penalty; NFIB holds the converse on the very same three factors. The factors are symmetric, which is the point — functional analysis cuts both ways.

**The first coercive spending condition.** No grant condition had ever crossed the line before. The rule rests on state sovereignty, which is why its reach beyond States (to private grantees) is doubtful — Note 6's last question. What *[[New York v. United States|New York]]* and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] forbid directly, coercive spending now forbids indirectly.

**Aftermath.** Congress zeroed out the payment in 2017 (budget reconciliation would not permit outright repeal), prompting the argument that a provision raising no revenue can no longer be a tax. *California v. Texas* (2021) disposed of that challenge on standing, leaving the question open. The sequence that opened with [[The Bank Debate]] closes here, before [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]].

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Was there in fact majority support for all of the constitutional conclusions set forth in Chief Justice Roberts's opinion? . . . Does that holding make the Commerce Clause part . . . actually dictum?"**

- Start with the lineups above. Five Justices rejected the commerce argument, but in two opinions and with no opinion of the Court; only the tax holding (Part III-C) is a real majority.
- *Why didn't the dissenters join Part III-A?* Because joining an opinion whose next section upholds the statute would make them party to the judgment. Their agreement is on the reasoning, not the disposition — which is exactly why the vote-counting is hard.
- *Dictum?* Ginsburg says yes: the tax holding carried the judgment, so the commerce discussion was unnecessary. Roberts says no: the saving construction only becomes available once the straightforward reading (via commerce clause) fails, so the commerce holding is a necessary step. Both are defensible; the lower courts have treated it as binding.

**Note 2. "Was this a proper use of the constitutional avoidance doctrine?"**

- The objection is about sequence. Avoidance normally lets a court *skip* the constitutional question; here the Court decided it first and then avoided its consequences. (Marshall-esque in Marbury and walking through the commission and cause of action applicability before saying the statute cannot grant original jurisdiction)
- Roberts's answer is that the only question is whether the tax reading is "fairly possible," and that a court must save a statute if it can. The dissent's answer is that this is not construing but "rewrit[ing]."
- Press the asymmetry: if avoidance can be deployed *after* an adverse constitutional holding, <u>it becomes a vehicle for announcing new limits costlessly</u>.

**Note 3. "Is there any good answer to her argument that there is no coherent difference between regulating 'activity' and 'inactivity'?"**

- Roberts's answer is textual, not consequentialist: effects do not supply an *object* of regulation, and a power over whatever affects commerce is the police power renamed. "To an economist, perhaps, there is no difference . . . [b]ut the distinction between doing something and doing nothing would not have been lost on the Framers."
- The pressure point is line-drawing. Is "self-insurance" activity? Is deciding not to buy a decision or merely a failure? The dissent calls the Government's framing "wordplay"; Ginsburg calls Roberts's "formalistic."
- Argument types: Roberts runs text, precedent, history and consequences; Ginsburg runs purpose, structure and practice ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).

**Note 4. "[D]oes it explain why the framers thought it essential to spell out such powers as taxing and spending . . . but not . . . condemning land through eminent domain?"**

- Understand the Government's N&P logic first: guaranteed issue and community rating are valid; without the mandate they collapse into a death spiral; therefore the mandate is necessary to make a valid regulation work. That is a straight [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]] argument.
- Roberts's answer is that "necessary" is not enough, and that the mandate is a "great substantive and independent power" (forcing to buy insurance) that is not "proper" rather than an incidental one.
- Is that line clear? The honest answer is no — Ginsburg's "you will know it when you see it" lands. But it is a middle ground between a clause permitting everything and one adding nothing (Baude for it, Harrison against), and no long-exercised power looks less "great" than compelled purchase.

**Note 5. "Who needs the Commerce Clause? . . . Is the Taxing Clause power a Trojan horse for federalism?"**

- (a) A modest IRS surcharge on people who do not buy broccoli is plausibly valid under Part III-C: small, scienter-free, IRS-collected, leaving a lawful choice. Roberts's limit is thin — the taxing power compels only payment, never conduct — but it is real: the Government cannot imprison you for the choice.
- (b) *Basketball.* The free throw is a penalty if the rules declare the foul unlawful, a tax if they price it. Roberts reads § 5000A as a price; the dissent reads it as a rule with a sanction. The coach's instinct ("don't foul") is evidence for the dissent — but plenty of real taxes are also meant to stop you.
- (c) Labels. The dissent concedes Congress *could* have framed it as a tax; their whole case is that it didn't. Roberts: if the power exists, the word used is a political fact, not a constitutional one.
- (d) *Direct tax.* The dissent only raises the question; Roberts answers it, confining direct taxes to capitations and property taxes. Press the head-tax reading — it falls on persons for not doing something — but the counter is that capitations attach "without regard to . . . any other circumstance," and this one keys to income and insurance status. Fallback: it is an income tax, so the Sixteenth Amendment saves it.
- (e) The AIA/taxing-power split is not a contradiction: Congress is master of its own statutes but cannot alter the scope of its own constitutional powers by labeling.
- (f) *Zeroed out.* A tax that raises nothing arguably fails Kahriger's revenue requirement. *California v. Texas* never reached it.

**Note 6. "What did the Court identify as the essential features . . . that make them coercive? Where is the line?"**

- It is the *combination*: the sheer size (over 10 percent of a State's budget) and the fact that what is threatened is money from a separate, pre-existing program. Neither alone obviously suffices, and Roberts explicitly refuses to locate the line.
- Safe harbors by implication: a new grant that threatens no old money; a Dole-sized 5 percent; a condition that genuinely governs the *use* of the new funds.
- *Germaneness* does no work here — expanding coverage is germane to Medicaid on any loose reading. O'Connor's stricter Dole test (does the condition make *this* program work better?) would do more, and would be the better tool.
- *Private grantees?* Probably not (confirmed after recent cases). The rule is grounded in state sovereignty and the contract analogy, both of which are about States.

### Further drilling

- **Broccoli ordered, backed by a fine.** Invalid as a regulation of inactivity under both Roberts and the joint dissent — and under Ginsburg too, who distinguishes broccoli on free-riding grounds rather than defending the mandate-anything position.
- **The same payment, set at five times the price of insurance.** Unconstitutional: a prohibitory amount is the first Drexel factor. Note the irony — calling it a "tax" would then trigger the Anti-Injunction Act, so the challenger must pay first and sue for a refund.
- **Medicaid expansion with no § 1396c threat at all.** Plainly valid. The unconstitutionality is entirely in the enforcement mechanism, not the program or the offer.
- **Formalism and functionalism change sides.** Roberts is formalist on commerce (activity/inactivity) and functionalist on the tax (look past the label); Ginsburg is the reverse, and on spending she defers to the label Roberts looks past. Whoever asks "is this consistent?" is asking the right question of both.
- **Memorable lines.** "A gun to the head"; "economic dragooning"; "four million outlaws"; "the broccoli horrible"; "if all inactivity affecting commerce is commerce, commerce is everything"; "Article I contains no whatever-it-takes-to-solve-a-national-problem power."
