## Snapshot

Lincoln blockaded Southern ports after Fort Sumter, before Congress met and without any declaration of war. The owners of four captured vessels appealed decrees of condemnation, and the Court affirmed, 5–4. A war may exist in fact without being declared, so the President, bound to resist force by force, could exercise the belligerent right of blockade, and his judgment that war existed binds the courts. Residence in rebel territory, not allegiance, fixes enemy character.

## Issue

The casebook asks whether Congress "must formally 'declare' 'war' . . . in order to exercise its war-initiating power." The Prize Cases answer no.

- Had the President a right to institute a blockade of ports held by persons in armed rebellion, on the principles of international law?
- Was the property of persons residing within those States a proper subject of capture at sea as "enemies' property"?

## Rule

When war is made on the United States, by foreign invasion or by organized rebellion, the President is bound to resist force by force without waiting for Congress, and his judgment that a state of war exists binds the courts. A war may exist in fact, and carry the full rights of war under the law of nations, without any declaration.

- The President "has no power to initiate or declare a war," but if war is made he is "bound to accept the challenge without waiting for any special legislative authority."
- A civil war "is never solemnly declared; it becomes such by its accidents."
- Whether the resistance has reached the level of war "is a question to be decided by him," and the Court "must be governed by the decisions and acts of the political department."
- A sovereign facing civil war "may exercise both belligerent and sovereign rights."

## Operative facts

- **The Militia Acts of 1795 and 1807 authorized the President to use force to repel invasion and suppress insurrection.** *Standing authority on Grier's reading, which keeps the case out of the hardest category; for Nelson they "did not, and could not under the Constitution," let him declare war.*
- **The South attacked Fort Sumter in April 1861, and the war began before Congress could act.** *The war was thrust on the government. Had Lincoln started it, Grier's premise that the President cannot initiate war would have cut the other way.*
- **Lincoln proclaimed the blockade while Congress was out of session.** *Congress had neither authorized nor forbidden it, which puts the case in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] category two, not three.*
- **Congress never declared war on the Confederacy, and Lincoln refused to recognize it as a sovereign entity.** *Grier says Congress "cannot declare war against a State," so a declaration cannot be the test for civil war, and the refusal to recognize generates the inconsistency charge.*
- **Four vessels were seized: the Amy Warwick, carrying coffee from Rio de Janeiro to Hampton Roads under American colors, whose commander "was ignorant of the war"; the Crenshaw and the British Hiawatha, each carrying Virginia tobacco to Liverpool; and the Brilliante, a Mexican schooner carrying flour from New Orleans to the Yucatán.** *Two foreign owners and two American, which splits the challenge: the neutrals attack the blockade, the domestic owners the "enemies' property" label.*
- **Every capture occurred before July 13, 1861.** *The hinge of the dissent. Under Nelson's rule each seizure is "illegal and void," since the war-making power had not yet spoken.*
- **Congress met in special session on July 4, 1861 and was "wholly employed in enacting laws to enable the Government to prosecute the war," passing the Act of July 13 that approved the President's acts as if done under "previous express authority."** *Grier treats it as a fallback he does not need; Nelson refuses to read it backwards over captures already made.*
- **Prize proceedings condemned the four vessels and their cargoes, and the owners appealed.** *The casebook omits Part III, which applies the law vessel by vessel, so the argument is about propositions alone.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (5–4; Grier for the Court; Nelson dissenting, joined by Taney, C.J., Catron and Clifford)

- **The blockade was lawful.** Once organized rebels held territory and commenced hostilities, "the President had a right, jure belli, to institute a blockade of ports in possession of the States in rebellion."
- **The President decides whether the resistance amounts to war.** That question "is to be decided by him," and the Court "must be governed by the decisions and acts of the political department."
- **Congress ratified in any event.** If legislative sanction were needed, the 1861 special session supplies it.
- **Property of residents of rebel territory is enemies' property.** Liability "does not in any manner depend on the personal allegiance of the owner"; they "are none the less enemies because they are traitors."

**Grier, for the Court**

- Defines war by facts rather than forms: territory held, independence declared, allegiance cast off, armies organized, hostilities commenced, with foreign nations acknowledging it by declaring neutrality.
- Congress "alone has the power to declare a national or foreign war" but cannot declare war on a State; the Militia Acts supply what the President needs against insurrection.
- Once war is made on the nation, the President "must determine what degree of force the crisis demands." This rebellion sprang forth "a Minerva in the full panoply of war."
- On property, the claimants' theory would let the rebels exercise belligerent rights while denying them to the government; citizens owe a qualified allegiance to their State, and south of the line "is enemies' territory."

**Nelson, dissenting (joined by Taney, C.J., Catron and Clifford)**

- A state of war is a legal status, not merely a fact, and its consequences are sweeping: intercourse becomes unlawful, contracts suspended, enemy property capturable. So "the right of making war belongs exclusively to the supreme or sovereign power of the State."
- A civil war can carry belligerent rights, but only once "recognized or declared by the war-making power of the Government." The Militia Acts could not confer "the power of declaring war against a State of this Union."
- Until Congress acts, no citizen may be punished in person or property except for an offense against a prior law: "[t]he penalty of confiscation for the acts of others with which he had no concern cannot lawfully be inflicted."
- The Act of July 13, 1861 "recognized a state of civil war" and made it territorial, which makes every capture before that date "illegal and void."

## Arguments rejected

- *Only Congress can put the nation in a state of war; until it acts the country is legally at peace, and a blockade is a belligerent right the President cannot reach.* Grier: war is a fact, not a status conferred by declaration. "The President was bound to meet it in the shape it presented itself, without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name."
- *The government called the conflict an insurrection and refused to recognize the Confederacy, so it cannot claim belligerent rights.* Grier: the law of nations knows "no such anomalous doctrine" that rebels "are not enemies because they are traitors."
- *Southern residents remain citizens owed the protection of municipal law, so their property may be confiscated only after conviction.* Grier: that would make the commerce funding the rebellion immune because capture is "'unconstitutional!!!'" Capture at sea is a "milder mode of coercion."
- *Many Southerners are loyal and were coerced, as Lincoln's own proclamation admits.* Answered by the territorial rule: residence and trade inside the line "marked by lines of bayonets" fixes enemy character.

## Where it sits

**Who may begin a war.** The source of the defensive-war doctrine, which runs from Madison's "repel sudden attacks" in [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]] to the War Powers Resolution's "national emergency created by attack upon the United States." The defensive power is a power to respond, so every later claim of unilateral authority stretches it.

**Youngstown mapping.** Read with [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] the case sits in category two or one, never three: Congress was silent ex ante, the Militia Acts arguably authorized force against insurrection in advance, and the 1861 ratification moved the operation into category one. Jackson's widest latitude is force turned outward, which the blockade was though the enemy was domestic.

**The earlier cases point the other way.** *Talbot v. Seeman* (1801), *Bas v. Tingy* (1800), *Little v. Barreme* (1804) and *Brown v. United States* (1814) all emphasize congressional control, and Little is Clark's authority in Youngstown: where Congress prescribes the method, the President must follow it.

**The formalist pole.** Nelson's dissent is to this unit what Black's opinion is to Youngstown, returning in the Resolution's demand for "specific statutory authorization" and in the concurrent-resolution problem after [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|INS v. Chadha]]. Lincoln's other unilateral acts of 1861 are [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]], and the war-measure theory extends to [[The Emancipation Proclamation]].

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "The key issue that divides the majority and the dissent is whether congressional authorization is required for Lincoln's actions. . . . there is a third logically possible position: That Lincoln's actions are forbidden even if Congress authorizes them."**

- Majority: none is required; war is a fact the President must meet, and his recognition binds the courts. Dissent: it is required, because war is a legal condition only the war-making power can create.
- Both agree he may use force against insurrection; the fight is over belligerent rights against property.
- The third runs through secession: coercing a State exceeds federal power, and Congress "cannot declare war against a State." Nobody takes it, because it assumes secession was lawful.

**Note 2. "Was Lincoln being inconsistent? Was the Civil War unconstitutional?"**

- Inconsistent in appearance. He called the conflict a rebellion and would not recognize the Confederacy, yet claimed a belligerent right.
- Grier's answer is that the sovereign holds both sets of rights at once, treating rebels as enemies at sea and traitors at home.
- So the war was not unconstitutional; the double posture is a feature of civil war.

**Note 3. "If the president can sometimes use force without congressional authorization, when? . . . What do The Prize Cases say?"**

- The holding covers war made on the nation by invasion or organized rebellion, and no further.
- A foreign attack on United States territory is easier still, and an attack on forces abroad is probably covered, as the Resolution's § 2(c)(3) agrees.
- A grave threat without an attack falls outside it; the national-interest theory in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] needs more than this case gives.

**Note 4. "The Court's earliest decisions on war powers emphasized congressional control over the use of force rather than presidential power to respond to the use of force by others."**

- These are prize and confiscation cases too, which makes them the right comparison: each asks whether a capture was authorized, and each looks to a statute for the answer.
- Talbot puts it broadest: "[t]he whole powers of war being . . . vested in congress, the acts of that body can alone be resorted to as our guides."
- None involved an attack on the United States, and the Quasi-War statutes had already spoken, so they do not contradict Grier. *Durand v. Hollins* (1860) is the contrast, and only a circuit decision.

### Further drilling

- **Why is this not category three?** Congress had not spoken against the blockade; it was out of session and then ratified, unlike Youngstown, where seizure had been rejected.
- **Does ratification cure a capture made before authority existed?** Grier uses it only as a fallback, and retroactive forfeiture raises its own ex post facto problem.
- **How far does "a question to be decided by him" reach?** It is deference on whether the facts amount to war, not a holding that the President defines his own war powers.
- **Memorable lines.** "It becomes such by its accidents." "Without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name."
