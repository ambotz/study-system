## Snapshot

Congress chartered a national bank; Maryland taxed the notes of its Baltimore branch and sued the branch's cashier for the statutory penalties. The state courts gave judgment for Maryland, and the Supreme Court reversed, unanimously, on both questions: Congress may incorporate a bank, and a State may not tax the operations of a federal instrumentality. In play are the Necessary and Proper Clause, the fiscal powers it carries into execution, and the Supremacy Clause.

## Issue

The casebook asks "what principles does Marshall lay down for the scope of the enumerated powers of the national government?" — a question this case answers twice, once for Congress and once against the States.

- Has Congress power to incorporate a bank, and may the Bank itself select where its branches are fixed?
- May Maryland, "without violating the constitution, tax that branch"?

## Rule

Congress is not confined to the means the Constitution names: where the end is legitimate and within the enumerated powers, any appropriate means plainly adapted to it is constitutional, and the degree of necessity is for Congress, subject only to prohibition and pretext. Because federal law is supreme, a State may not tax or otherwise control a federal instrumentality's operations, though nondiscriminatory taxes on its real property and on residents' shares survive.

- "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."
- "[T]he States have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress."

## Operative facts

- **No enumerated power mentions a bank or a corporation, and no clause excludes incidental powers as the Articles had.** *The charter can rest only on implied powers; the Tenth Amendment's omission of "expressly" is the hinge.*
- **Congress chartered the First Bank in 1791 for twenty years, after the principle was "opposed with equal zeal and ability" in debate and in Washington's cabinet.** *Both the practice Marshall credits and the argument he is adjudicating, Hamilton against Jefferson and Madison.*
- **The charter was allowed to expire in 1811, a recharter bill having failed under Madison's presidency.** *A lapse cuts both ways: Jackson later counts 1791 and 1816 against 1811 and 1815 to call the precedents "equal."*
- **The War of 1812 brought financial crisis, and the embarrassments of doing without a Bank "convinced those who were most prejudiced against the measure of its necessity."** *Necessity shown by events rather than by reasoning, which is why Marshall must disclaim reliance on it.*
- **Madison vetoed an 1815 bank bill on policy grounds alone, waiving the constitutional question as "precluded" by recognition in all three branches, then signed the Second Bank's charter in 1816.** *The leading opponent of 1791 conceding the point, which Marshall turns against Maryland.*
- **Maryland's 1818 statute taxed banks the State had not chartered at one to two percent, which in practice meant only the Bank of the United States; Kentucky and Ohio went further, at $60,000 and $50,000 a year on each branch.** *The tax singled the Bank out, but Marshall does not rest on discrimination. Note 6 asks whether he should have.*
- **The tax fell on the Bank's operations — its notes — and not on its real estate or on Marylanders' shares.** *This lets Marshall win while preserving nondiscriminatory taxes on both, the limit the modern doctrine grew from.*
- **The Bank itself, not the charter, chose to put a branch in Baltimore.** *This forces a sub-holding that Congress may leave the siting to the Bank, since the charter prescribes duties and "those duties require branches."*
- **Maryland sued James McCulloch, cashier of the Baltimore branch, for the penalties after he issued notes without paying the tax, and the state courts gave judgment for Maryland.** *A penalty suit in the State's own courts, so the Bank's immunity arrives as a defense on writ of error.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (unanimous — "We are unanimously of opinion"; Marshall, C.J., for the Court; reversed)

- **Congress may incorporate a bank.** The act "is a law made in pursuance of the constitution, and is a part of the supreme law of the land."
- **The branches are valid.** The Bank "may, we think, be safely trusted with the selection of places where those branches shall be fixed."
- **Maryland's tax is "unconstitutional and void,"** which is "the unavoidable consequence of that supremacy which the constitution has declared."

**Marshall, C.J., for the Court**

- **Practice.** "[A] bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long acquiesced in, but a "doubtful question" about the powers of equal representatives of the people "ought to receive a considerable impression from that practice." He then disclaims reliance: the result would be the same "were the question entirely new."
- **The people, not the States.** The Constitution "derives its whole authority" from the ratifying conventions, and the people's act "could not be negatived" by the State governments. The government is one "of enumerated powers," yet "supreme within its sphere of action."
- **A constitution, not a code.** A document detailing every means "would partake of the prolixity of a legal code," so "only its great outlines should be marked"; Article I, § 9's restrictions would be pointless if nothing were implied. "[W]e must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding."
- **"Necessary and proper."** "[N]o word conveys to the mind, in all situations, one single definite idea"; Article I, § 10 says "absolutely necessary" when it means that, and "proper" can only "qualify that strict and rigorous meaning." The Clause sits "among the powers of Congress, not among the limitations," so "if it does not enlarge, it cannot be construed to restrain."
- **Application and judicial role.** Incorporation is "never used for its own sake," and a bank is "a convenient, a useful, and essential instrument" of the government's "fiscal operations." Once the means is appropriate, "the degree of its necessity . . . is to be discussed in another place."
- **The tax.** Supremacy is "so interwoven with [the Constitution's] web . . . as to be incapable of being separated from it": a power to create implies a power to preserve, and "that authority which is supreme must control." The check on taxation is that "the legislature acts upon its constituents"; federal means, "given by all, for the benefit of all," have none.

## Arguments rejected

- *The Constitution is the act of sovereign and independent States; federal powers are delegated by the States.* The people ratified in conventions, and though they met in their States their act remained "the measures of the people themselves."
- *Creating a corporation is a sovereign power not expressly granted, so it cannot be implied.* Incorporation is no "great substantive and independent power" but "a means by which other objects are accomplished."
- *"Necessary" means indispensable; and in any event the Clause only confirms that Congress may legislate.* Ordinary usage often makes "necessary" mean "convenient, or useful, or essential," and the second is self-refuting.
- *Taxation is an absolute sovereign power, and the States can be trusted not to use it to destroy.* "[T]he power to tax involves the power to destroy," and "the magic of the word Confidence" will not do.
- *Taxing power is concurrent, so if Congress may tax state banks the States may tax the national bank.* The difference is "the action of the whole on a part, and the action of a part on the whole": Maryland taxes "institutions created, not by their own constituents."

## Where it sits

**The 1791 debate, decided.** McCulloch resolves [[The Bank Debate]] judicially, adopting Hamilton's framework almost whole and rejecting Jefferson's "indispensable." [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] gives Madison's account of the retreat Marshall turns against Maryland, and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] denies it authority over a President in his legislative capacity.

**The implied-powers foundation.** The first holding anchors the modern enumerated-powers cases: Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]] builds on it, and *United States v. Comstock* (2010) applies its rational-relation reading. [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] give "proper" independent force, and the pretext passage is the ancestor of [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]].

**Intergovernmental immunity, narrowed.** *Osborn v. Bank of the United States* (1824) reaffirmed the second holding against Ohio's raid; *Graves v. New York ex rel. O'Keefe* (1939) and *South Carolina v. Baker* (1988) abandoned the broad immunity for nondiscriminatory taxes. What survives bars taxing or regulating the United States directly and discriminating against it.

**The Banks in Article II.** [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) reserved the Federal Reserve as following "the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks," and [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] (2026) upheld the Governors' protection on that ground. Next in the casebook is *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824).

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "What does Chief Justice Marshall say about Jefferson's arguments? About Hamilton's? About Madison's?"**

- Jefferson's "indispensable" reading of "necessary" is rejected.
- Hamilton's ordinary meaning and corporation-as-means are adopted almost verbatim. Madison's 1791 position is rejected, his conversion is evidence that statesmen "have yielded those opinions."

**Note 2. "Does he consider the practice dispositive? What kinds of questions does Marshall think can and cannot be settled by practice?"**

- No. Practice gets "a considerable impression" on a "doubtful question" about the division of power, but cannot legitimate "a bold and daring usurpation."
- Structure dominates: it carries the tax holding, which has no text at all. Compare [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette|Madison]] and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]]; see [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].

**Note 3. "Does Marshall think the Necessary and Proper Clause restricts the powers of the national government?"**

- He says only that it cannot restrict: "if it does not enlarge, it cannot be construed to restrain."
- Confirmation: as "A Friend of the Constitution" he agreed with Hampden that the Clause added nothing to Congress's incidental powers.

**Note 4. "What, exactly, does Marshall say that 'necessary' means? What work does he think 'proper' does in the clause? . . . Is 'Let the end be legitimate' a good paraphrase?"**

- "Necessary" admits degrees; "proper" only qualifies its strictness, making the phrase a unity — Bray's hendiadys reading, which [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] abandon.
- Imperfect: it drops "necessary" and adds "consist with the letter and spirit," which is where "proper" lives.

**Note 5. "Does Marshall say which enumerated powers the incorporation of the Bank would be 'necessary and proper' to carry into execution? Did Hamilton?"**

- No single power: taxing, borrowing, commerce, war, and armies, with the Bank an instrument of "fiscal operations." So did Hamilton.
- A means serving many powers at once starts to look like a freestanding power.

**Note 6. "What, exactly, was the problem with the tax? . . . Should these questions . . . be resolved by constitutional interpretation?"**

- The first: Marshall disclaims any inquiry into "what degree of taxation is the legitimate use." Maryland did single the Bank out, but he never rests there; modern doctrine does.
- *Johnson v. Maryland* (1920) extends it to regulation: no state license for a mail carrier, though perhaps rules on "turning at the corners of streets."
- The closing question is the live one: "In the legislature of the Union alone, are all represented" points to Congress — [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] runs the same logic in reverse.

### Further drilling

- **Hypothetical: a one-percent tax on every Maryland bank.** Discrimination is gone, but the tax still falls on the Bank's "operations."
- **Is a pretext inquiry manageable?** He reserves it while forbidding any weighing of necessity; [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] never reaches motive.
- **Compact or people?** One State's people cannot confer a sovereignty over what all the States created. See [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]].
- **Memorable lines.** "[I]t is a constitution we are expounding." "[A] splendid bauble."
