## What it is

The House of Representatives' debate of December 6–8, 1791 (3 Annals of Congress 229–41) on the
bill establishing the Post Office. Representative Sedgwick moved to strike the list of roads in
the bill and substitute "by such route as the President of the United States shall, from time to
time, cause to be established." The motion failed.

The casebook sets it next to [[Gundy v. United States]] as the founding-era case study on
delegation. Every modern argument about the nondelegation doctrine appears in it, made by members
of the First and Second Congresses with no precedent to rely on.

## What to notice

- **The textual hook.** Article I, § 8 gives Congress power "To establish Post Offices and post
  Roads." Livermore: "it is as clearly their duty to designate the roads as to establish the
  offices; and he did not think they could with propriety delegate that power, which they were
  themselves appointed to exercise."
- **Sedgwick's theory of delegation.** "[I]t [is] sufficient that the House should establish the
  principle, and then leave it to the Executive to carry it into effect." Later: "the
  establishment of principles was the peculiar province of the former, and the execution of them,
  that of the latter." Note 1 asks whether Sedgwick thought Congress could delegate legislative
  power, or thought that choosing routes was not legislative at all. The second reading is the
  better one, and it is the root of the intelligible principle.
- **Sedgwick's reductio.** Congress may borrow money and coin money. If no part of its power were
  delegable, Congress would have to "go in a body to borrow every sum" and "turn coiners, and work
  in the Mint themselves," and even "act the part of executioners."
- **Bourne's argument from practice.** The House had already let the President mark out excise
  districts. If that delegation was valid, so is this one. The earliest example of the argument
  from practice in the course.
- **Arguments from institutional competence cut both ways.** Sedgwick and Barnwell say members
  are biased by local interest and the executive, "responsible to the people," will decide with
  more care. Hartley, White and Page say ten Virginia representatives know Virginia's roads better
  than one man.
- **The anti-delegation argument is about political corruption, not efficiency.** White: a
  Postmaster could tell a town "If you do not send such a man to Congress, you shall have no post
  office." Hartley notes England bars post office officials from interfering in elections and the
  bill has no such guard. Vining: to a good President the power is a burden, to a bad one a
  dangerous weapon.
- **Page's slippery slope.** If the House can leave the post office to the President, "it may
  leave to him any other business of legislation," and adjourn.
- **Hartley's middle position.** A sunset: limit the law to three to five years and see. If
  Congress proves incompetent, delegate, "or, if the Constitution will not allow such a
  delegation, submit the article for amendment." Bourne picks up the sunset as a safeguard.
- **Madison's position.** Sedgwick's arguments "admit of such construction as will lead to
  blending those powers so as to leave no line of separation whatever." The subject "is expressly
  committed to Legislative determination by the Constitution"; alienating it "would be a violation
  of the Constitution."
- **Benson's observation undercuts the whole debate.** The bill as written designates no post
  offices at all. Congress was already delegating the offices while arguing about the roads.
- **What precedent does the vote set?** Note 2. Madison's side won. But a House vote declining to
  delegate is not a holding that delegation is unconstitutional; members voted on expediency and
  constitutionality together. Be ready to say how much weight an early congressional vote deserves
  as "liquidation" of meaning.
- **Private rights versus government property.** Note 3. Post roads concern the government's own
  operations, not private conduct. Gorsuch's definition of legislative power in [[Gundy v. United States|Gundy]] is rules governing private conduct, which would put post roads outside the
  nondelegation doctrine altogether. The debate is harder for Gorsuch than it looks.
