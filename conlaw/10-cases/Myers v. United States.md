## Rule

Article II's grant of "the executive power" and the Take Care Clause give the President the power to remove the executive officers he appoints with the Senate's consent, so Congress may not make that removal depend on the Senate's consent.

## Facts

- **Myers was appointed a first-class postmaster at Portland, Oregon, by the President with the Senate's consent, for a four-year term.** *The holding reaches officers the President appoints with Senate consent. Where Congress vests an inferior officer's appointment in a department head, the Court accepted (citing Perkins) that Congress may restrict the head's power to remove that officer.*
- **The Act of 1876 provided that such postmasters "shall be appointed and may be removed by the President by and with the advice and consent of the Senate."** *The Senate's role in removal is the fact later cases seize on: [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] distinguishes Myers as "an attempt by Congress itself to gain a role in the removal of executive officials."*
- **The Postmaster General removed Myers at the President's direction in 1920, and the Senate never consented.** *The statute was squarely violated, so the Court was "confronted by the constitutional question and cannot avoid it."*
- **A postmaster performs only executive functions.** *Taft did not rest on this fact, but [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] later made it decisive: a postmaster is "restricted to the performance of executive functions."*
- **The 1876 removal clause was an appropriation rider that the President signed.** *Explained away: the President's approval reflected "the otherwise valuable effect of the legislation approved," not acquiescence in the Senate's claim.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (6–3; Taft, C.J., for the Court; Holmes, McReynolds and Brandeis, JJ., dissenting)

- The 1876 provision "by which the unrestricted power of removal of first-class postmasters is denied to the President is in violation of the Constitution and invalid."
- Article II grants the President "the general administrative control of those executing the laws, including the power of appointment and removal of executive officers," a conclusion "confirmed by his obligation to take care that the laws be faithfully executed."
- The President must execute the laws through subordinates, so he must be able to remove "those for whom he cannot continue to be responsible."
- "The power of removal is incident to the power of appointment, not to the power of advising and consenting to appointment."
- The [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]] adopted this construction, "all branches of the Government" acquiesced in it for seventy-four years, and the First Congress's view carries special weight because its leaders included members of the Convention.
- The Tenure of Office Act of 1867, "in so far as it attempted to prevent the President from removing executive officers who had been appointed by him by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, was invalid."
- Congress may let department heads appoint inferior officers and may regulate the heads' removal of them, but may not "draw to itself, or to either branch of it, the power to remove or the right to participate in the exercise of that power."

**Rejected**

- *The Senate shares in appointment, and Hamilton in Federalist No. 77 read that share to require the Senate's consent to removal as well (Brandeis).* Taft: the Senate's check on appointment was meant to stop the President from filling too many offices from the larger states, and a Senate veto on removal is "a much more serious blending of the legislative with the executive, than a rejection of a proposed appointment."
- *Congress creates the office, fixes its pay and may abolish it, so Congress may also fix its tenure; the President's duty "does not go beyond the laws" (Holmes), and his power to remove from statutory inferior offices "comes immediately from Congress" (Brandeis).* Taft: leaving removal to Congress under the Necessary and Proper Clause "would be a delegation by the convention to Congress of the function of defining the primary boundaries of another of the three great divisions of government."
- *A postmaster is an inferior officer, and the Decision of 1789 concerned only superior officers.* Taft: "The power to remove inferior executive officers, like that to remove superior executive officers, is an incident of the power to appoint them."
- *Congress and the President have required Senate consent to such removals for fifty-eight years, and "[a] persistent legislative practice" of that kind "should be deemed tantamount to judicial construction" (Brandeis, citing Midwest Oil).* Taft: the Reconstruction Congress reversed the settled construction "during a heated political difference of opinion," which deserves less weight than the construction reached by the First Congress "during a political calm."

## Context

Myers is the Court's adoption of the Madison-Ames reading of [[The Decision of 1789]], and it resolved the Johnson-era fight by declaring the Tenure of Office Act invalid ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]). Nine years later, [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] confined Myers to "purely executive officers" and treated the rest of Taft's opinion as dicta. Frankfurter, concurring in [[United States v. Lovett (1946)]], put the reversal plainly: "Within less than a decade an opinion of fifteen pages largely qualified what the Myers case had apparently so voluminously settled." [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] called Myers "undoubtedly correct in its holding," but replaced its categories with the question whether a removal limit impedes the President's constitutional duty.

The dissents have their own afterlife. Frankfurter's [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] concurrence adopts Holmes's line that the President's duty "does not go beyond the laws," and Kagan's dissent in [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] says Taft's history "has held up even worse than Myers' holding (which was mostly reversed)." The majority in Seila Law nonetheless built its general rule on Myers, and [[Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) restored Myers's reasoning for every officer who exercises executive power. [[Trump v. United States]] cites Myers for the removal power as an example of the President's exclusive constitutional authority. See [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]], and for the Tenure of Office Act and Johnson's impeachment, [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].
