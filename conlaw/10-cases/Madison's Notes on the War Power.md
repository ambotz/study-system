## What it is

Madison's record of the Convention debate of August 17, 1787 on the draft clause giving the Legislature power "To make War." Madison and Gerry moved to substitute "declare" for "make," "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks," and the motion carried. The casebook prints the entire debate and frames it with an opening survey (Starting War, Text, Historical Context) that opens the war-powers unit (Assignment 14).

The reading is short, and every later dispute in the unit comes back to it: the President's power to repel attacks, what a "declaration" is, and whether practice since 1787 has moved the line.

## What to notice

- **The casebook's framing questions.** Scope of "declare War"; scope of "Commander in Chief"; who may initiate war; defensive action against attack; who conducts war; what happens where the powers overlap; "What is 'war'—is it any use of military force?"; and whether actual practice "has been consistent with the Constitution's allocation of war powers." Every later reading in the class answers one of these.
- **The text divides the war power.** Congress gets clause 11 ("To declare War, grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal, and make Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water"), the power to raise and support armies (appropriations capped at two years), to provide a navy, to make rules for the forces, and to call forth and govern the militia, plus taxing, spending and Necessary and Proper. The President gets "[t]he executive Power," the Commander in Chief Clause, the Take Care Clause and the oath.
- **The historical baseline.** In the background English understanding, "executive power" arguably included the prerogatives of war and peace. The casebook's point is that the Constitution "relocates to the legislative branch the power 'to declare War' and to raise and support armies and navies," leaving the President the command role "and whatever else remains of the 'executive power.'" The argument about how much remains is the Vesting Clause debate from Class 6 in a war setting.
- **The four positions on the floor.**
  - Pinkney: vest war in the Senate. The Legislature is "too slow," meets "but once a year," and the House is too numerous; the Senate is "more acquainted with foreign affairs."
  - Butler: vest it in the President, "who will have all the requisite qualities, and will not make war but when the Nation will support it."
  - Gerry: "never expected to hear in a republic a motion to empower the Executive alone to declare war."
  - Mason: "for clogging rather than facilitating war; but for facilitating peace."
- **What the change from "make" to "declare" was meant to do.** The motion's stated purpose was "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks." Sherman's summary: "The executive shd be able to repel and not to commence war." The change carved out defensive force; it was not a transfer of the power to begin war.
- **King's gloss changed Connecticut's vote.** "Make" war "might be understood to 'conduct' it which was an Executive function." On that reading "declare" separates the decision to go to war (Congress) from the conduct of war (the Commander in Chief). The vote went from 7–2 with Massachusetts abstaining to 8–1 once Connecticut switched.
- **Sherman's worry cuts the other way.** He thought "declare" narrowed the congressional power "too much." The Yoo reading in Note 1 builds on exactly this: if "declare" means only a formal announcement, the President keeps power to wage undeclared war, limited by the purse.
- **Peace was left out on purpose.** Ellsworth: "It shd be more easy to get out of war, than into it." Butler's motion to give the Legislature the power of peace failed ten states to none. Peace runs through the treaty power instead, which is the problem [[Terminating War]] takes up.
- **Note 1, the majority reading.** Most scholars (Ramsey, Lofgren) read the materials to give Congress the decision "to take the nation into a condition of war." That is the better reading of the debate: every speaker who objected to executive war-making prevailed, and the only executive power named is repelling attacks. The Yoo reading has to treat the words "commence" and "clogging" as beside the point.
- **Note 2, repelling sudden attacks today.**:
  - An attack on U.S. territory already under way: within the carve-out on any reading.
  - An attack on U.S. forces abroad: within its logic; [[The Prize Cases]] and WPR § 2(c)(3) both treat attack on "armed forces" as sufficient.
  - An attack believed imminent: the carve-out stretches to anticipatory self-defense only if "repel" includes preempt. Technology is the pressure the note names.
  - A strike to punish or deter after the attack is over: no longer "repelling"; this is the Syria 2017/2018 and Soleimani question in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]].
- **Practice and liquidation.** The casebook asks whether practice "'liquidate[s]' the meaning of the Constitution, as Madison said in The Federalist No. 37," or whether "a great deal of the nation's practice with respect to war powers has been unconstitutional." Liquidation in Madison's sense settles ambiguity through deliberate, repeated, accepted practice; it cannot override clear text. The debate here makes the initiation question look less ambiguous than the practice suggests, which is why the practice argument is hard.
- **Only five formal declarations.** 1812, Mexico, Spain, World War I, World War II (six with the Declaration of Independence). The casebook asks whether Congress must use "those magic words." The Convention's concern was who decides, not what the decision is called, which points toward treating a statutory authorization as enough (see [[Declarations of War]]).
- **The Youngstown connection.** Jackson gives the Commander in Chief his widest latitude when force is turned against the outside world and refuses it when force is turned inward. The debate adds a limit on the outward side too: the command power was designed to conduct war and repel attacks, not to begin war. See [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]].
