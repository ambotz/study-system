## What it is

Alexander Hamilton's essay of May 28, 1788, the first of The Federalist's papers on the judiciary, answering the Anti-Federalist critique made in [[Brutus No. 11]]. It defends tenure during good behaviour and gives the classic pre-ratification argument for judicial review. [[Marbury v. Madison]] follows it closely, fifteen years later.

## What to notice

- **"The least dangerous" branch.** The Executive "holds the sword," the legislature "commands the purse" and prescribes the rules; the judiciary "has no influence over either the sword or the purse" and "can take no active resolution whatever." "It may truly be said to have neither Force nor Will, but merely judgment; and must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments." Brutus's answer in advance: an unaccountable body is dangerous whatever its tools.
- **Weakness justifies independence.** The judiciary "is beyond comparison the weakest of the three departments," so "all possible care is requisite to enable it to defend itself against their attacks." Tenure during good behaviour is the care. [[Stuart v. Laird]] is the test: the weakest branch, attacked by the Repeal Act, did not defend itself.
- **Judicial review protects a "limited Constitution."** Limits such as the bans on bills of attainder and ex post facto laws "can be preserved in practice no other way than through the medium of courts of justice, whose duty it must be to declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void." Two things to notice: the examples are limits on Congress protecting individuals, and "manifest tenor" suggests courts strike down only clear violations. The second point is the root of later arguments for deference to Congress.
- **The agency argument.** "[E]very act of a delegated authority, contrary to the tenor of the commission under which it is exercised, is void." To deny it "would be to affirm, that the deputy is greater than his principal; that the servant is above his master; that the representatives of the people are superior to the people themselves." Marshall's "original right" of the people to establish fundamental principles is the same argument.
- **Why the legislature is not the judge of its own powers.** Legislative finality "cannot be the natural presumption, where it is not to be collected from any particular provisions in the Constitution." Hamilton's argument for judicial review is a presumption from structure, not a textual argument; he cites no clause. Marshall adds the text (the arising-under clause, the oath, the Supremacy Clause).
- **Courts as "an intermediate body between the people and the legislature."** "The interpretation of the laws is the proper and peculiar province of the courts. A constitution is, in fact, and must be regarded by the judges, as a fundamental law." Where the two conflict, "the Constitution ought to be preferred to the statute, the intention of the people to the intention of their agents." Compare Marbury: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
- **Constitutional supremacy, not judicial supremacy.** "Nor does this conclusion by any means suppose a superiority of the judicial to the legislative power. It only supposes that the power of the people is superior to both." This is the passage the departmentalist reads in Class 13: courts disregard unconstitutional statutes because the Constitution binds them, not because their reading binds everyone else.
- **The conflict-of-laws analogy.** When two statutes clash, "it is the province of the courts to liquidate and fix their meaning and operation," and the later statute prevails. That rule is "not derived from any positive law, but from the nature and reason of the thing." For a superior and a subordinate authority, the same reasoning reverses the rule: "the prior act of a superior ought to be preferred to the subsequent act of an inferior and subordinate authority." Judicial review is presented as ordinary legal reasoning, not a special power.
- **Hamilton's answer to Brutus's fear of judicial will.** Courts might "substitute their own pleasure to the constitutional intentions of the legislature," but that risk exists in every statutory case: "if they should be disposed to exercise Will instead of Judgment," the result is the same. "The observation, if it prove any thing, would prove that there ought to be no judges distinct from that body." The answer concedes the risk and denies it is special to constitutional cases.
- **What Hamilton does not answer here.** Brutus's point that no power can correct the Court's errors goes unanswered in the excerpt. The answer comes in The Federalist No. 81, quoted in [[A Map of Article III]]: impeachment is "alone a complete security."
- **Casebook's question: what should a reader have expected?** From Hamilton, a court that declares void only acts contrary to the Constitution's "manifest tenor," in the cases before it, as an application of ordinary rules for resolving conflicts between laws. From Brutus, a court that construes the Constitution by its "reason and spirit" with no one to correct it. Marbury's reasoning is Hamilton's almost point for point; whether practice has followed Hamilton or Brutus is the Class 12–13 question.
