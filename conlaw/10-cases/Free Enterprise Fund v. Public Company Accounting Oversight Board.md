## Rule

Article II forbids insulating an officer from the President by two layers of for-cause removal protection, a new situation to which the Court declined to extend its earlier approval of single for-cause limits.

## Facts

- **Members of the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board could be removed by the Securities and Exchange Commission only for cause.** *Standing alone, one for-cause layer resembles what [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] had sustained.*
- **The SEC commissioners who held that removal power could themselves be removed by the President only for cause.** *The second layer is what made the case "a new situation not yet encountered by the Court"; with at-will commissioners, the Board members would sit behind only one layer.*
- **The double-layer structure had no historical precedent.** *The Court treated "[a] lack of historical precedent" as "[p]erhaps the most telling indication of [a] severe constitutional problem."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Roberts, C.J., for the Court)

- The two layers of for-cause protection violate Article II.
- "Since 1789, the Constitution has been understood to empower the President to keep these officers accountable—by removing them from office, if necessary."
- The President possesses "the authority to remove those who assist him in carrying out his duties."
- Allowing officers to "execute the laws" beyond the President's control "is contrary to Article II's vesting of the executive power in the President."
- Without the removal power, the President cannot "be held fully accountable" for the exercise of the executive power.
- The exceptions of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] were left in place, not extended.

**Rejected**

- *The Court had already sustained for-cause limits in Humphrey's Executor and Morrison, so each layer here is a limit of a kind already approved.* Roberts: those precedents do not extend to "a new situation not yet encountered by the Court," an official insulated by two layers of for-cause protection.

## Context

Free Enterprise Fund sits between [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] and [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] in the casebook's doctrinal history. It supplied Seila Law's method: treat Humphrey's Executor and Morrison as exceptions and refuse to extend them to a new situation, weigh the absence of historical precedent heavily, and guard against insulated agencies that "slip from the Executive's control, and thus from that of the people." Thomas's opinion in Seila Law says Free Enterprise Fund's reasoning "created further tension (if not outright conflict) with Humphrey's Executor." The novelty line travelled beyond removal: Roberts borrowed it in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] against the individual mandate, *NLRB v. Noel Canning* cited Free Enterprise Fund for the President's control of staffing and political accountability, and Gorsuch's [[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]] dissent cited it as an instance of the Court policing "legislative efforts to control executive branch officials."

[[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) relies on Free Enterprise Fund and names it with [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] and Seila Law among the precedents the dissent would discard. The double-layer rule still matters after Slaughter: commissioners who exercise executive power are now removable at will, so one for-cause layer below them is the surviving configuration and a second layer remains barred.
