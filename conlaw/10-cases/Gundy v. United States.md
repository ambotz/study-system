## Rule

Nothing new in doctrine. The Court reaffirms the **intelligible principle** test from [[J. W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States|J. W. Hampton]] and reaches it through statutory interpretation. The case matters for the votes: a plurality of four, Alito concurring only in the judgment and inviting reconsideration, and three dissenters proposing a replacement test, with Kavanaugh not participating.

## Facts

- **The delegation.** SORNA § 20913(d) gives the Attorney General "the authority to specify the applicability" of its requirements to pre-Act offenders. *For them, that sentence is the whole statute.*
- **The plurality reads "specify the applicability" narrowly.** It means *how* to apply SORNA during transition, not *whether*, and requires registration "as soon as feasible." *This reading decides the case; the dissent takes the government's reading in [[Reynolds v. United States|Reynolds]]: "all of the statute's requirements, some of them, or none of them."*
- **Successive Attorneys General applied SORNA to pre-Act offenders in different measures.** *The plurality sees transition; the dissent sees policymaking discretion.*
- **The delegation governs private conduct backed by criminal penalties.** Gundy was prosecuted under § 2250 for failing to register. *Gorsuch's test turns on this; delegations over government property, like the post roads, or within Article II power would differ.*
- **About half a million people are affected.** *For the dissent, a criminal code by the Attorney General; for the plurality, a problem justifying delegation.*
- **Eight Justices sat.** *Alito would reconsider the doctrine with a willing majority; a ninth Justice could have changed the result.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (5–3 judgment; Kagan for a plurality of four; Alito concurring in the judgment)

- A delegation is constitutional if Congress "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform."
- A nondelegation inquiry "always begins (and often almost ends) with statutory interpretation."
- Read with its text, context, purpose and history, § 20913(d) confines the Attorney General to transitional feasibility questions and requires registration of all pre-Act offenders as soon as feasible. That delegation is "distinctly small-bore" and "easily passes muster."
- The Court has invalidated delegations only twice, both in 1935, and has upheld "public interest," "fair and equitable," and "requisite to protect the public health" standards.

**Rejected**

- *§ 20913(d) gives the Attorney General unguided power to decide whether SORNA applies to half a million people at all, and that is lawmaking.* The plurality answers by construction: the statute does not mean that, and [[Reynolds v. United States|Reynolds]] already said so.
- *The intelligible principle test has no basis in the original meaning and should be replaced.* Only three Justices would do so. Alito would support reconsideration but not "single out the provision at issue here for special treatment." The test survives by default.
- *(Implicitly) Congress should have to make the retroactivity choice itself.* The plurality answers with necessity: "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional," because "Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives" (quoting [[Mistretta v. United States|Mistretta]]).

## Context

Gundy is the modern bookend to [[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]]. The 1935 cases ([[Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan|Panama Refining]] and [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]]) are the only two invalidations; Gundy is the moment four Justices signalled that the doctrine might revive. Read it with [[The Post Roads Debate]], which is the founding-era version of the same argument. Sedgwick's line that Congress should "establish the principle, and then leave it to the Executive to carry it into effect" is the ancestor of the intelligible principle; Madison's objection is the ancestor of Gorsuch's.

**Gorsuch's three categories of permissible delegation**, which he draws from founding-era cases:

- Congress makes the policy and lets the executive "fill up the details" ([[Wayman v. Southard]]).
- Congress prescribes the rule and makes its application depend on executive fact-finding ([[Cargo of Brig Aurora v. United States|The Brig Aurora]]).
- Congress assigns non-legislative responsibilities, or discretion over matters already within the executive's own power, such as foreign affairs.

Gorsuch's "hydraulic pressures" point is worth taking seriously. He argues the Court still polices delegation under other names: the **major questions doctrine**, **void for vagueness**, and the structural cases, including [[INS v. Chadha]] (delegation to one House) and [[Clinton v. City of New York]] (the line-item veto), which are the next class.

**What happened after the 4th edition.** Two later decisions bear directly on this case. [[Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo|Loper Bright]] (2024) overruled *Chevron* deference, which removes one way agencies expanded delegated authority without a nondelegation ruling. [[FCC v. Consumers' Research]] (2025) then took up the nondelegation question with a full Court and **upheld** the Universal Service Fund 6–3 (Kagan), applying the intelligible principle test, with Gorsuch dissenting. The revival that Alito's concurrence seemed to promise has not come. Check whether the 5th edition or Baude's Canvas materials add Consumers' Research.

Alito's position is the question Note 2 presses and a real problem for any constitutional court: when should first principles override precedent, and should a Justice who doubts a precedent still apply it until a majority is ready? See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Criminal conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 2250 for failing to register; Gundy moved to dismiss on nondelegation grounds. The District Court and the Second Circuit rejected the claim, as had all eleven courts of appeals to consider it.
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari anyway and affirmed. Kavanaugh, confirmed after argument, did not participate.

### Facts

The Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act of 2006 requires a sex offender to register in every state where he lives, works or studies, to keep the registration current, and to report in person periodically for fifteen years to life. The general rule in § 20913(b) requires registration before the offender completes his prison sentence, or within three business days of sentencing if he is not imprisoned, and so assumed the offender would still be in custody. Pre-Act offenders were the problem: many had never registered and many had already been released, so they could not comply with subsection (b). Subsection (d) gives the Attorney General authority over them. Herman Gundy pleaded guilty to a sexual assault of a minor under Maryland law the year before SORNA was enacted; after his release in 2012 he moved to New York. The casebook condenses the plurality's discussion of the statute.

### Issue

- Does § 20913(d), which authorizes the Attorney General to "specify the applicability" of SORNA's requirements to pre-Act offenders, unconstitutionally delegate legislative power?
- (Raised by the dissent and Alito.) Should the intelligible principle test be retained?

### Holding

- No violation. Construed to require the Attorney General to register all pre-Act offenders as soon as feasible and to allow only transitional delay, the statute supplies an intelligible principle far more definite than standards the Court has upheld.
- Left open. Five Justices apply the existing test, but only four endorse it; Alito would reconsider it with a full Court, and three dissenters would replace it now.

### Rule

- Congress may not transfer to another branch "powers which are strictly and exclusively legislative" ([[Wayman v. Southard]]).
- A delegation is valid if Congress supplies an "intelligible principle" to which the delegee must conform ([[J. W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States|J. W. Hampton]]), or makes clear "the general policy" and "the boundaries of [the] authority" (American Power & Light).
- The inquiry begins with statutory interpretation.

### Reasoning

**(Kagan, J., for a plurality)**

- The doctrine exists but the standard is "not demanding"; the Court has "almost never felt qualified to second-guess Congress" on the degree of policy judgment left to those executing the law (quoting Scalia's dissent in [[Mistretta v. United States|Mistretta]]).
- "Specify the applicability" read in context means specify how, not whether. The definition of "sex offender" includes pre-Act offenders, and the Act demands a "comprehensive" system.
- Feasibility standards are "ubiquitous in the U.S. Code."
- Necessity: "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional."

**(Alito, J., concurring in the judgment)**

- Since 1935 the Court has upheld "extraordinarily capacious standards."
- He would support reconsideration if a majority were willing. Absent one, it would be "freakish" to strike this statute alone. He joins neither the plurality's constitutional nor its statutory analysis.

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Gorsuch, J., with Roberts, C.J., and Thomas, J., dissenting)**

- The legislative power is the power to adopt "generally applicable rules of conduct governing future actions by private persons." The people vested it in Congress alone, and "not even Congress" can alter that.
- Delegation defeats the purposes of bicameralism and presentment: laws would be fewer, slower, more consensual, more stable and more accountable if Congress had to make them. It invites "finger-pointing."
- The permissible categories are details, fact-finding and non-legislative or overlapping executive powers. SORNA fits none of them: it lets the Attorney General choose all, some or none of the requirements for half a million people.
- The intelligible principle test was "plucked" from J. W. Hampton, where it meant something narrower, and has become a "misadventure" under which "many 'less discerning readers [have been able only to] find gibberish.'"
- The Court still polices delegation through major questions and vagueness. It should do so openly.
- Respecting the separation of powers "forecloses no substantive outcomes"; Congress can still delegate details, fact-finding and study.
- "It would be easy enough to let this case go." The rule that protects sex offenders protects everyone else.

### Cold-call notes

- **What is the holding, and how many Justices joined it?** The judgment is 5–3. The plurality is four. There is no majority opinion. Getting this wrong is the most common error about Gundy.
- **Why does the plurality spend most of its opinion on statutory interpretation?** Because the constitutional question "all but answers itself" once the statute is read narrowly. The plurality makes the case easy by reading the delegation small. Be ready to argue that this is constitutional avoidance doing the real work.
- **State Gorsuch's test in his three categories.** Fill up the details; condition on fact-finding; non-legislative or overlapping executive power. Then apply it: where does the post roads question fall? It concerns government property, not private conduct, which Note 3 to the Post Roads Debate suggests may allow broader delegation.
- **Is delegation an exception to the Vesting Clause, or inherent in executing the law?** Note 1 presses this. Sedgwick's view: execution necessarily involves discretion, so the executive part of the business belongs to executive officers. The necessity view: the modern state cannot function otherwise, which the notes call "a constitutional fudge."
- **What is Alito doing, and is it defensible?** He believes the doctrine is wrong but will not act alone. The notes quote Ed Levi: "constitution worship . . . gives great freedom to a court." Be ready to say whether a Justice who doubts a precedent should apply it in the meantime.
- **Should it matter what power is delegated?** Note 3 in the Gundy notes asks whether Congress could delegate the power to declare war, tax, appropriate or impeach. Tie this to Class 9 on war and Class 5 on appropriations.
- **Know the post-2021 history.** [[FCC v. Consumers' Research]] (2025): 6–3, Kagan, upheld under the intelligible principle test. [[Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo|Loper Bright]] (2024) ended *Chevron*. If asked whether Gundy started a revival, the answer so far is no in the nondelegation doctrine itself, and yes through statutory interpretation doctrines such as major questions.
- **Memorable detail.** "One good year, and 211 bad ones" (Sunstein, 2000). The plurality's "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional." Gorsuch's "passed the potato to the Attorney General."
