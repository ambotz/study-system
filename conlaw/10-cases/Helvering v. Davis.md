## Rule

The Hamiltonian reading of the spending power is "now settled by decision," and the line between general and particular welfare belongs to Congress unless its choice is "clearly wrong, a display of arbitrary power, not an exercise of judgment."

## Facts

- The Social Security Act provided for the payment of Old Age Benefits.
- The problem the Old Age Benefits addressed was, in the Court's words, "plainly national in area and dimensions."
- The case came one year after [[United States v. Butler]], whose restrictive half barred taxing and spending to accomplish what Congress could not regulate directly. *The Court did not discuss that restrictive half.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Congress may spend money in aid of the "general welfare."
- The conception of the spending power "advocated by Hamilton and strongly reinforced by Story has prevailed over that of Madison."
- The line between general and particular welfare "cannot be known through a formula in advance of the event."
- "The discretion, however, is not confided to the courts. The discretion belongs to Congress, unless the choice is clearly wrong, a display of arbitrary power, not an exercise of judgment."
- "[T]he concept of welfare or the opposite is shaped by Congress."
- Old Age Benefits serve the general welfare because the problem is national, and a single state's program would be "bait to the needy and dependent elsewhere, encouraging them to migrate and seek a haven of repose."

**Rejected**

- *Madison's reading: Congress may spend only in aid of its other enumerated powers.* "There have been great statesmen in our history who have stood for other views. We will not resurrect the contest. It is now settled by decision."
- *Because a line between general and particular welfare must exist, courts must draw it.* The Court conceded that "[t]he line must still be drawn" but held that the drawing is for Congress.

## Context

Helvering is the half of the 1937 pair that settles the scope of spending; [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis]], decided the same year, addresses inducements to the states. The casebook reads Helvering as affirming only part of [[United States v. Butler]]: after Helvering, "the Court had settled on a broad, Hamiltonian view of the spending power." That is the reading of [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]] over [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]].

[[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|South Dakota v. Dole]] disposed of the general-welfare limit by quoting Helvering that "the concept of welfare or the opposite is shaped by Congress," which is why that limit is the weakest in [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]]. Madison had predicted that no court would police the general/local line, and after Helvering none has; compare the founding-era vote in [[The Savannah Fire Debate]]. Ginsburg, in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]], used Helvering's "bait to the needy" passage to argue that states cannot solve the problem of the uninsured on their own.
