## Rule

The Commerce Clause reaches activity, not inactivity, and a law that creates "the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not a "proper" means under the Necessary and Proper Clause (Roberts, with the four joint dissenters agreeing separately); but whether an exaction is a constitutional "tax" turns on function, not label, so a modest, IRS-collected payment for going without insurance is a valid tax (5–4). A spending condition that threatens a State's existing grants to force it into a new program is unconstitutional coercion (7–2), remedied by barring the threat rather than striking the program (5–4).

## Facts

- **The mandate ordered people to buy a product they were not already buying.** The Act required most Americans to maintain "minimum essential" coverage, aimed mostly at healthy people outside the insurance market. *This is what made the case "inactivity"; the Government's "active in the market for health care" reframing is the whole Commerce Clause fight.*
- **The only consequence of not buying insurance was a payment to the IRS.** The "[s]hared responsibility payment" was collected with income taxes, scaled to income, capped at the price of insurance, carried no scienter requirement and no criminal prosecution. *Each feature answers a [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Drexel Furniture]] factor and turns the "penalty" into a tax.*
- **Congress called the payment a "penalty," not a "tax."** *Controlling for the Anti-Injunction Act, which follows Congress's label; irrelevant to the constitutional question, which does not.*
- **The Medicaid expansion was enforced by the threat to cut off all Medicaid funds.** A State refusing the expansion could lose its existing federal Medicaid money, not just the new money. *Roberts's coercion holding depends on the threat reaching "existing" funds for what he treats as a new program.*
- **The money at stake was enormous.** Medicaid is over 20 percent of the average State's budget, with federal funds covering 50 to 83 percent of it. *[[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]] involved less than half of one percent of South Dakota's budget; size converts "encouragement" into "a gun to the head."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (fractured; Roberts, C.J., announcing the judgment; for the Court only in Parts I, II and III-C)

- **Anti-Injunction Act (Part II, for the Court).** The Act does not bar the suit, because Congress labeled the payment a "penalty"; the statutory label does not control the constitutional question. All nine Justices agree the suit may proceed.
- **Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses (Part III-A, Roberts alone).** "The individual mandate cannot be upheld as an exercise of Congress's power under the Commerce Clause. That Clause authorizes Congress to regulate interstate commerce, not to order individuals to engage in it." Nor is the mandate a "proper" means of carrying the insurance reforms into execution. The four joint dissenters reach the same conclusion in their own opinion, so five Justices reject the commerce theory, but in no single opinion.
- **Saving construction (Part III-B, Roberts alone).** Because the "most straightforward reading" is unconstitutional, the Court asks whether reading the mandate as a tax on going uninsured is "fairly possible." It is.
- **Taxing power (Part III-C, for the Court, 5–4).** The payment "may reasonably be characterized as a tax," within Art. I, § 8, cl. 1; it is not a direct tax requiring apportionment. Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan join.
- **Medicaid coercion (Part IV-A, Roberts, Breyer and Kagan).** Threatening existing Medicaid funds is "economic dragooning that leaves the States with no real option but to acquiesce." The four joint dissenters agree the expansion is coercive, making seven votes.
- **Remedy (Part IV-B, same plurality).** The Secretary "cannot apply § 1396c to withdraw existing Medicaid funds for failure to comply with the requirements set out in the expansion"; the rest of the Act stands under its severability clause. Ginsburg and Sotomayor concur in the judgment on this point, making five votes against the joint dissent's call to strike the whole Act.

**Rejected**

- *The uninsured are all in the health-care market, since everyone eventually needs care and the uninsured shift its cost to others; the mandate merely regulates how they pay.* Roberts: "active in the market" "has no constitutional significance"; a person who "bought a car two years ago and may buy another in the future is not 'active in the car market' in any pertinent sense." Precedent recognizes power over "class[es] of activities," not classes of individuals.
- *Health insurance is unique, so upholding the mandate implies nothing about cars or broccoli.* Roberts: "cars and broccoli are no more purchased for their 'own sake' than health insurance"; insurance and care "involve different transactions, entered into at different times, with different providers."
- *The mandate is an "integral part of a comprehensive scheme of economic regulation," as in [[Gonzales v. Raich|Raich]], so the Necessary and Proper Clause carries it.* Roberts: Raich concerned "individual applications of a concededly valid statutory scheme"; the mandate "vests Congress with the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power," and "[e]ven if the individual mandate is 'necessary' to the Act's insurance reforms, such an expansion of federal power is not a 'proper' means."
- *Congress called the payment a penalty and framed a command, so the Court cannot rewrite it as a tax (joint dissent).* Roberts: labels do not control; the windows hypothetical shows that "[n]o one would doubt that this law imposed a tax," and that conclusion "should not change simply because Congress used the word 'penalty' to describe the payment."
- *A tax on inactivity is as troubling as a commerce regulation of inactivity.* Roberts: "the Constitution does not guarantee that individuals may avoid taxation through inactivity"; a capitation is "a tax that everyone must pay simply for existing"; and a tax "leaves an individual with a lawful choice to do or not do a certain act."
- *Medicaid, as amended, is one program; Congress reserved the right to change it and has not threatened funds "earmarked for any other program" (Ginsburg).* Roberts: that "begs the question"; existing Medicaid and the expansion are not "all one program simply because 'Congress styled' them as such."
- *The coercive threat taints the whole expansion and, with the mandate, the whole Act (joint dissent).* Roberts: invalidating an unconstitutional application is "not 'rewriting' the statute; we are merely enforcing the Constitution," and § 1303 tells the Court to leave the remainder unaffected.

## Context

NFIB is the casebook's "Synthesis" case for the federal-powers unit: the Commerce Clause of [[Wickard v. Filburn]], [[United States v. Lopez]] and [[Gonzales v. Raich]]; the Necessary and Proper Clause of [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]; the taxing power of [[Hylton v. United States]] and [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]; and the conditional spending power of [[South Dakota v. Dole]]. Each piece of the opinion is an application of one earlier reading, and each piece reaches a different vote.

The **commerce holding** adds a limit the earlier cases never needed. [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] and [[United States v. Morrison|Morrison]] limit the commerce power by the *kind* of activity (economic or not); NFIB limits it by the *existence* of activity. Wickard's farmer grew wheat; Raich grew marijuana; the uninsured did nothing. Roberts reads [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]'s "great substantive and independent power" language as a limit on implied powers and borrows "proper" from [[Printz v. United States]], where laws inconsistent with the "letter and spirit of the constitution" are "merely acts of usurpation."

The **tax holding** turns [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Drexel Furniture]] around. Drexel held that something called a "tax" was really a penalty; NFIB holds that something called a "penalty" may really be a tax, using the same three factors (size, scienter, enforcing agency). The **direct-tax** analysis confines the category to capitations and taxes on land or personal property, relying on [[Hylton v. United States|Hylton]] and noting that [[Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co|Pollock]] was overturned by the Sixteenth Amendment.

The **spending holding** is the first time the Court held a spending condition unconstitutionally coercive. [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]] and [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis]] had only mentioned the possibility that "pressure turns into compulsion." Roberts ties the coercion limit to anti-commandeering: [[New York v. United States]] and [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] forbid Congress to order States to regulate, and coercive spending does "indirectly" what they forbid directly.

Post-casebook: in the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 Congress set the shared responsibility payment at $0 (effective 2019) without repealing the mandate. Texas and other plaintiffs argued that a payment raising no revenue could no longer be a tax under [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] and that the whole Act fell with it. In [[California v. Texas]] (2021) the Court, 7–2 (Breyer, J.), dismissed for lack of standing: an unenforceable mandate caused no traceable injury. The merits question in Note 5f was never reached.

NFIB leads into the Class 24 materials on the nature of the Union ([[Texas v. White]]) and closes the enumerated-powers sequence that began with [[The Bank Debate]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Suits by 26 States, the National Federation of Independent Business and individuals challenging the individual mandate and the Medicaid expansion. The Eleventh Circuit held the mandate unconstitutional but severable and upheld the Medicaid expansion; other circuits split on the mandate.
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the Anti-Injunction Act, the mandate, severability and the Medicaid expansion, and affirmed in part and reversed in part.

### Facts

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010 sought near-universal coverage. Its "guaranteed-issue" and "community-rating" provisions barred insurers from refusing or surcharging people with preexisting conditions. Those reforms invite people to wait until they are sick to buy insurance, so Congress added the individual mandate, which requires healthy people to join the risk pool or make a "shared responsibility payment" to the IRS with their taxes. Separately, the Act expanded Medicaid, the joint federal–state program for the poor, to all adults under a set income threshold, with the federal government paying most of the new cost. A State that refused the expansion could lose all of its federal Medicaid funds under § 1396c. States, a small-business federation and individuals sued, challenging both the mandate and the expansion.

### Issue

- Does the Commerce Clause authorize Congress to require individuals to purchase health insurance?
- If not, does the Necessary and Proper Clause authorize the mandate as an integral part of the Act's insurance reforms?
- May the mandate be sustained as an exercise of the taxing power, and if so is it an unapportioned direct tax? (Threshold: does the Anti-Injunction Act bar the suit?)
- Is the Medicaid expansion an unconstitutionally coercive exercise of the spending power?
- If the expansion is coercive, what is the remedy: strike the expansion, the whole Act, or only the threat?

### Holding

- **Commerce Clause: no** (Roberts in Part III-A for himself; the joint dissent separately; five votes). The power to "regulate" commerce "presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated"; the mandate compels activity from those who are inactive. Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan would uphold it.
- **Necessary and Proper Clause: no** (same five). Even if "necessary," a power to create the predicate for federal regulation is not "proper": it is a "great substantive and independent power," not an incidental one.
- **Taxing power: yes** (Part III-C for the Court, 5–4: Roberts, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan). Read as a tax on going uninsured (a "fairly possible" reading, Part III-B, Roberts alone), the payment is a tax by function under the [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Drexel Furniture]] factors; it is not a capitation or a tax on property, so it need not be apportioned. The Anti-Injunction Act does not apply because Congress labeled the payment a "penalty" (Part II, for the Court; no Justice disagrees).
- **Medicaid coercion: yes** (7–2: Roberts, Breyer, Kagan in Part IV-A plus the four joint dissenters; Ginsburg and Sotomayor dissent). Threatening all existing Medicaid funds, over 10 percent of a State's overall budget, to force acceptance of what is in substance a new program crosses from encouragement into compulsion.
- **Remedy: strike only the threat** (5–4: Roberts, Breyer, Kagan in Part IV-B; Ginsburg and Sotomayor concurring in the judgment). The Secretary may not withdraw existing funds from States that refuse the expansion; States may accept or decline the new money, and the rest of the Act survives. The joint dissent would have struck the entire Act.

### Rule

- "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" (Roberts). Congress may regulate "class[es] of activities," not classes of individuals defined by inactivity.
- A law that gives Congress "the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not "proper," even if "necessary" (Roberts).
- If a statute has two possible meanings, one unconstitutional, the Court adopts the other if it is "fairly possible" (Roberts, citing [[Crowell v. Benson]]).
- Whether an exaction is a tax for constitutional purposes is a "functional" question: the label controls statutory questions like the Anti-Injunction Act, not constitutional power. Signals of a penalty: a "prohibitory" amount, a scienter requirement, enforcement by a non-revenue agency, and "punishment for an unlawful act or omission."
- Direct taxes requiring apportionment are capitations and taxes on land or personal property; a tax "triggered by specific circumstances" is neither.
- Congress may attach conditions governing the use of new funds, but when "pressure turns into compulsion," by threatening "other significant independent grants," the condition is unconstitutional (Roberts plurality, with the joint dissent).
- The remedy for an unconstitutional spending threat is to bar the threat, not the program.

### Reasoning

**(Roberts, C.J., Part I and introduction, for the Court)**

- The federal government is "one of enumerated powers"; "[t]he enumeration presupposes something not enumerated" ([[Gibbons v. Ogden]]). The States hold the "police power," and federalism "secures to citizens the liberties that derive from the diffusion of sovereign power."
- Commerce and tax powers "must be read carefully to avoid creating a general federal authority akin to the police power."
- Deference on policy: "It is not our job to protect the people from the consequences of their political choices." But "[o]ur deference in matters of policy cannot, however, become abdication in matters of law."

**(Roberts, C.J., Part III-A, for himself: Commerce Clause)**

- **Novelty.** Congress "has never attempted to rely on that power to compel individuals not engaged in commerce to purchase an unwanted product." Borrowing [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]]: "the most telling indication of [a] severe constitutional problem . . . is the lack of historical precedent."
- **Text.** "Regulate" assumes something already exists. The Constitution separately grants power to "coin Money" and to "regulate the Value thereof," and to "raise and support Armies" and to regulate the forces; if "regulate" included "create," those grants would be superfluous.
- **Precedent.** Every commerce case describes the power as reaching "activity." "It is nearly impossible to avoid the word when quoting them."
- **Wickard reductio.** Price can be supported by increasing demand as well as limiting supply, so the Government's logic would let Congress "command that those not buying wheat do so." The farmer in [[Wickard v. Filburn|Wickard]] "was at least actively engaged in the production of wheat."
- **Broccoli.** Poor diets raise health costs more than the uninsured do; "Under the Government's theory, Congress could address the diet problem by ordering everyone to buy vegetables."
- **Framers.** "The Framers gave Congress the power to regulate commerce, not to compel it." Accepting the Government's theory "would give Congress the same license to regulate what we do not do, fundamentally changing the relation between the citizen and the Federal Government."
- **Future activity.** "The Commerce Clause is not a general license to regulate an individual from cradle to grave, simply because he will predictably engage in particular transactions." Wickard and Raich "involved preexisting economic activity."

**(Roberts, C.J., Part III-A-2, for himself: Necessary and Proper Clause)**

- The Clause covers powers "incidental to the [enumerated] power, and conducive to its beneficial exercise," not "great substantive and independent power[s]" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]).
- The Court is "very deferential" on "necessary" ([[United States v. Comstock|Comstock]]), but laws not "consist[ent] with the letter and spirit of the constitution" are not "proper," quoting [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] on "acts of usurpation."
- Earlier Necessary and Proper cases (civil commitment, bribery of federal-fund recipients, tolling) were "derivative of, and in service to, a granted power." The mandate instead lets Congress "draw within its regulatory scope those who otherwise would be outside of it."
- [[Gonzales v. Raich|Raich]] is distinguished as a challenge to "individual applications of a concededly valid statutory scheme."

**(Roberts, C.J., Part III-B, for himself: saving construction)**

- "The most straightforward reading of the mandate is that it commands individuals to purchase insurance." Only because that reading fails under the Commerce Clause does the Court turn to the tax reading: "Under our precedent, it is therefore necessary to ask whether the Government's alternative reading of the statute—that it only imposes a tax on those without insurance—is a reasonable one."
- The test is not the most natural reading but a "fairly possible" one. Justice Story: no court should adopt a construction that "should involve a violation, however unintentional, of the constitution."

**(Roberts, C.J., Part III-C, for the Court: taxing power)**

- The payment "looks like a tax in many respects": paid by "taxpayer[s]" with returns, not owed below the filing threshold, computed from taxable income, dependents and filing status, housed in the Internal Revenue Code, collected by the IRS, and producing revenue (about $4 billion a year by 2017), "the essential feature of any tax" ([[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]]).
- The "penalty" label is "fatal to the application of the Anti-Injunction Act" but does not control constitutional power: "It is up to Congress whether to apply the Anti-Injunction Act to any particular statute," but its choice "does not, however, control whether an exaction is within Congress's constitutional power to tax."
- **Drexel factors reversed.** (1) The amount "will be far less than the price of insurance, and, by statute, it can never be more"; paying may be "a reasonable financial decision," unlike Drexel's "prohibitory" 10 percent of net income. (2) No scienter requirement. (3) Collected "solely by the IRS through the normal means of taxation," without criminal prosecution.
- Regulatory taxes are old: early protective tariffs, cigarette taxes, taxes on marijuana and sawed-off shotguns.
- A penalty is "punishment for an unlawful act or omission"; failing to buy insurance "need not be read to declare that failing to do so is unlawful." Four million people a year expected to pay: "Congress did not think it was creating four million outlaws."
- **Windows hypothetical.** A $50 IRS payment for owning a house without energy-efficient windows, adjusted by income and paid with the return, is plainly a tax; calling it a "penalty" should not change that.
- **Direct Tax Clause.** [[Hylton v. United States|Hylton]] suggested only capitations and land taxes are direct; [[Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co|Pollock]] added taxes on personal property and its income. The payment is not a capitation, which is paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance," and not a tax on property.
- **Tax on inactivity.** Three answers: the Constitution has never promised escape from taxation through inactivity (capitations; Franklin's "death and taxes"); the taxing power has limits, since a tax can become "a mere penalty with the characteristics of regulation and punishment" ([[Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch|Kurth Ranch]], quoting Drexel); and the taxing power gives less control than the commerce power, being "limited to requiring an individual to pay money into the Federal Treasury, no more."
- "Upholding the individual mandate under the Taxing Clause thus does not recognize any new federal power. It determines that Congress has used an existing one."

**(Roberts, C.J., Part IV, with Breyer and Kagan, JJ.: Medicaid)**

- Spending legislation is "much in the nature of a contract," legitimate only if the State "voluntarily and knowingly accepts the terms" ([[Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman|Pennhurst]]).
- The coercion limit follows from anti-commandeering: "[T]he Constitution simply does not give Congress the authority to require the States to regulate," whether directly ([[New York v. United States]], [[Printz v. United States|Printz]]) or by indirect coercion.
- Conditions on the use of the funds themselves are how Congress ensures money is spent on its view of the "general Welfare." Conditions threatening "other significant independent grants" are "a means of pressuring the States to accept policy changes."
- **Dole compared.** In [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]], 5 percent of highway funds, under half of one percent of the State's budget: "relatively mild encouragement." Here, all Medicaid funds, over 10 percent of a State's overall budget: "it is a gun to the head."
- The expansion is in substance a new program; Congress's decision to title it an amendment is "irrelevant" if it "is not properly viewed as a modification of the existing Medicaid program." The summary: States "must either accept a basic change in the nature of Medicaid, or risk losing all Medicaid funding."
- No need to "fix the outermost line": "wherever that line may be, this statute is surely beyond it."
- Remedy: § 1396c is unconstitutional as applied to existing funds; § 1303's severability instruction preserves the rest.

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Ginsburg, J., with Sotomayor, J., and with Breyer and Kagan, JJ., as to Parts I–IV; concurring in part, concurring in the judgment in part, dissenting in part)**

- Joins Parts I, II and III-C of Roberts's opinion; would hold, alternatively, that the Commerce Clause authorizes the mandate, and would uphold the Medicaid expansion "exactly as Congress enacted it."
- **Collective action.** States cannot solve the uninsured problem alone; a generous State becomes "bait to the needy and dependent elsewhere" ([[Helvering v. Davis]]). The Commerce Clause "was the Framers' response to the central problem that gave rise to the Constitution itself."
- **Rational basis.** The uninsured "as a class" consume billions in care, cannot pay for much of it, and shift costs to others; forgoing insurance is "an economic decision Congress has the authority to address," not "doing nothing."
- **Everyone is in the market.** More than 60 percent of the uninsured see a doctor or hospital each year, nearly 90 percent within five years. Wickard and Raich already let Congress "direct the conduct of an individual today" because of "a prophesied future transaction."
- **Broccoli answered.** The health-care market is unique: need is "inevitable yet unpredictable," and emergency care is guaranteed; the broccoli buyer "will be obliged to pay at the counter." Calls it "the broccoli horrible" and quotes Bork: judges "are not supposed to ski it to the bottom."
- **"Regulate" answered.** The insurance and health-care markets already existed, so "the 'something to be regulated' was surely there." [[Wickard v. Filburn|Wickard]]: "The stimulation of commerce is a use of the regulatory function quite as definitely as prohibitions or restrictions thereon."
- **Formalism.** The activity/inactivity line repeats the abandoned "production" and "indirect" formulas; Roberts "plows ahead with his formalistic distinction."
- **Limits remain.** [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] still bars regulating noneconomic conduct with attenuated effects; the Bill of Rights and "the democratic process" check purchase mandates.
- **Necessary and Proper.** Guaranteed issue and community rating would produce "an adverse-selection death-spiral" without the mandate, so the mandate is an "essential par[t] of a larger regulation of economic activity" under [[Gonzales v. Raich|Raich]] and Scalia's Raich concurrence. "Proper" was violated in [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] and [[New York v. United States|New York]] because those laws commandeered States; the mandate acts "directly upon individuals." Roberts's test tells lower courts: "You will know it when you see it."
- **Commerce discussion unnecessary** (editors' summary of Part IV): having upheld the mandate as a tax, the Court had no need to reach the Commerce Clause.
- **Medicaid (Part V, Ginsburg and Sotomayor only).** Medicaid is "a single program with a constant aim"; Congress reserved the right to amend it, and States have "no entitlement to receive any Medicaid funds." Unlike [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]], the condition relates solely to Medicaid and induces nothing Congress could not do directly, since it "could operate its own health-care program for poor persons."

**(Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Alito, JJ., jointly dissenting)**

- **Commerce Clause (agreeing with Roberts's result, in their own opinion).** Wickard is "the ne plus ultra of expansive Commerce Clause jurisprudence"; to reach the failure to grow wheat "is to make mere breathing in and out the basis for federal prescription." "If all inactivity affecting commerce is commerce, commerce is everything." "The Constitution is not that. It enumerates not federally soluble problems, but federally available powers." "Article I contains no whatever-it-takes-to-solve-a-national-problem power."
- **Taxing power.** "The issue is not whether Congress had the power to frame the minimum-coverage provision as a tax, but whether it did so." "[W]e have never held—never—that a penalty imposed for violation of the law was so trivial as to be in effect a tax." Evidence of a mandate: § 5000A is titled "Requirement to maintain minimum essential coverage"; the exaction is called a "penalty" eighteen times; some are exempt from the payment but not the mandate; and it sits in Title I, not the revenue provisions of Title IX ("the nail in the coffin"). "[T]o say that the Individual Mandate merely imposes a tax is not to interpret the statute but to rewrite it."
- **Direct tax.** The tax reading raises a hard Direct Tax Clause question that got "the lick-and-a-promise" and "fly-by-night briefing and argument." They do not decide it.
- **Medicaid and remedy** (editors' summary). They agree the expansion is coercive but would strike both the mandate and the expansion and, as central to the Act, the whole Act.
- **Summation.** "The Court today decides to save a statute Congress did not write." "In the name of restraint, it overreaches. In the name of constitutional avoidance, it creates new constitutional questions. In the name of cooperative federalism, it undermines state sovereignty." Structural protections are "less romantic" than the Bill of Rights, but "[t]he fragmentation of power produced by the structure of our Government is central to liberty."

**(Thomas, J., dissenting)**

- Omitted from the casebook. Thomas joined the joint dissent and wrote separately to repeat his view that the "substantial effects" test is inconsistent with the original understanding of the Commerce Clause (see his opinions in [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] and [[Gonzales v. Raich|Raich]]).

### Cold-call notes

- **Count the votes on each issue (Note 1).** Anti-Injunction Act: unanimous that the suit proceeds. Commerce and Necessary and Proper: 5–4 against the mandate, but Roberts writes for himself and the joint dissent concurs only in its own opinion. Tax: 5–4 for the mandate, for the Court. Medicaid coercion: 7–2 (Roberts, Breyer, Kagan plus the joint four). Remedy: 5–4 (the plurality plus Ginsburg and Sotomayor). Only Roberts is in the majority on every question.
- **Why didn't the joint dissenters join Part III-A?** They would have struck the whole Act, so joining Roberts would have meant joining an opinion whose judgment they rejected; they may also have wanted a broader rationale (no "whatever-it-takes" power) than Roberts's. Be ready to say that five Justices agreeing on a proposition across two opinions is not the same as an opinion of the Court.
- **Is the Commerce Clause discussion holding or dictum? (Note 1)** Dictum argument (Ginsburg's Part IV): once the mandate stands as a tax, the commerce analysis did not affect the judgment. Holding argument (Roberts): the saving construction is available only if the "most straightforward reading" is unconstitutional, so the commerce ruling is a necessary step ("it is therefore necessary to ask whether the Government's alternative reading . . . is a reasonable one"). Middle position: five Justices reached the proposition, and lower courts have treated it as binding in practice. Note that Roberts's closing summary states the commerce conclusion flatly ("The individual mandate cannot be upheld as an exercise of Congress's power under the Commerce Clause"), but Part III-A is not an opinion of the Court.
- **Hypo: Congress orders everyone to buy broccoli, backed by a fine.** Commerce: no under Roberts and the joint dissent (inactivity). Ginsburg: no as well, because the broccoli market lacks free-riding and guaranteed service, and the inference chain to health costs fails [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]]. Same result, different reason.
- **Hypo: a $20 IRS surcharge on those who don't buy broccoli (Note 5a).** Under Part III-C, plausibly a valid tax: modest, no scienter, IRS-collected, lawful choice to pay. That is the "Trojan horse" worry: the taxing power reaches inactivity the commerce power cannot. Roberts's answers: the tax power gives no power to compel beyond payment, and past a point a tax "becomes a mere penalty."
- **Hypo: Congress labels the mandate payment a "tax" but sets it at five times the cost of insurance.** Constitutional: the label does not help; a "prohibitory" amount is the first [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Drexel]] factor, so it is a penalty and needs the commerce power, which fails. Statutory twist: because the label is "tax," the Anti-Injunction Act would likely bar a pre-enforcement suit, so challengers would have to pay and sue for a refund.
- **Hypo: the payment is small but applies only to those who "willfully" go uninsured and is enforced by HHS with criminal fines.** Two or three Drexel factors now point to a penalty; the tax reading is no longer "fairly possible."
- **Is the label rule coherent? (Note 5e)** The same exaction is a "penalty" for the Anti-Injunction Act and a "tax" for Article I. Roberts: Congress controls its own statutes, so its label governs a statutory question; it cannot expand or shrink its constitutional powers by labels. The joint dissent's reply: the question is what Congress did, and what it did was impose a penalty for violating a legal command.
- **Basketball fouls (Note 5b).** Roberts's line is whether the law declares the conduct unlawful. A free throw after a foul is a penalty because the foul breaks a rule; Roberts reads § 5000A as imposing no rule, just a price. The joint dissent's evidence ("Requirement," "shall," "penalty") is that Congress wrote a rule.
- **Is the mandate a direct tax? (Note 5d)** Roberts: not a capitation (triggered by circumstances) and not a property tax. Pressure points: it falls directly on persons, not transactions; is it a head tax on the uninsured? If it is an income tax because it runs through the income-tax system and exempts those below the threshold, the Sixteenth Amendment removes the apportionment problem. The joint dissent only raises the question.
- **Hypo: Congress creates the expansion as a separate, new grant program, with no threat to existing funds.** Valid under Part IV-B: "Nothing in our opinion precludes Congress from offering funds . . . and requiring that States accepting such funds comply with the conditions on their use."
- **Hypo: Congress modestly changes existing Medicaid (adds a covered service, adjusts eligibility slightly) and threatens all funds for non-compliance.** Probably valid: a "modification of the existing Medicaid program" is within the reserved power to amend, and the notice problem disappears. The line is Roberts's "basic change in the nature of Medicaid," and it is contested: Ginsburg says Medicaid is "a single program with a constant aim."
- **Hypo: the expansion threatens only 5 percent of existing Medicaid funds.** Closer to [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]]'s "relatively mild encouragement." Roberts relies on both the threat to existing funds and its size; Note 6 asks which is essential. Answer: both appear necessary to his reasoning, and he declines to "fix the outermost line."
- **Apply O'Connor's Dole dissent (Note 6).** Germaneness asks whether the condition relates to how the funds are used. Expanding Medicaid to more poor adults is germane to Medicaid in a loose sense; the coercion holding therefore does not rest on germaneness but on the new-versus-old program distinction and size.
- **Does the coercion rule protect private grant recipients? (Note 6)** Roberts grounds it in State sovereignty and anti-commandeering ([[New York v. United States]], [[Printz v. United States|Printz]]); nothing in the opinion extends it to private recipients.
- **Necessary and Proper, McCulloch, and the professor (Note 4a).** Roberts uses [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]'s "great substantive and independent power" to say some powers are too important to be incidental. The casebook cites William Baude, *Rethinking the Federal Eminent Domain Power* (2013), for this reading of the Clause: expect questions on it. Pressure: Congress has long exercised criminal law, conscription, eminent domain and legal tender without enumeration; are those less "great" than a purchase mandate?
- **Is NFIB consistent with Comstock and Raich? (Note 4d)** [[United States v. Comstock|Comstock]] upheld civil commitment of those already in federal custody, so the predicate (federal custody) already existed. [[Gonzales v. Raich|Raich]] regulated existing activity (growing marijuana). NFIB says Congress cannot manufacture the predicate. Tension: Congress's own insurance reforms created the adverse-selection problem the mandate fixed. Scalia's Raich concurrence ("reasonably adapted") is quoted by Ginsburg against the joint dissent Scalia joined.
- **Ginsburg's reading of "proper."** She reads [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] as a rule about commandeering States; the mandate acts on individuals, as the Framers intended. Roberts reads "proper" as a broader structural limit. Which reading later cases adopt is open.
- **Formalism and functionalism reversed.** On commerce, Roberts is formalist (activity/inactivity) and Ginsburg functionalist (real effects, "not . . . nomenclature"). On tax, Roberts is functionalist (substance over label) and the joint dissent formalist ("whether it did so"). On spending, Roberts looks through Congress's label ("Congress styled" it as one program) and Ginsburg defers to it.
- **Text and original meaning vs. doctrine.** Roberts's best textual argument is the coin-Money / regulate-the-Value pairing. Ginsburg's is structural and historical: the Articles of Confederation's collective-action failure. Thomas's view (omitted) would go further and reject substantial effects altogether. Ask which is closest to [[Gibbons v. Ogden]]'s "commerce" and to the Framers' "regulate."
- **Types of argument (Note 3).** Roberts: text, precedent (every case says "activity"), history (no precedent for mandates), consequences (broccoli). Ginsburg: purpose and structure (collective action), precedent (Wickard, Raich), consequences (the democratic check). Joint dissent: structure ("fragmentation of power . . . is central to liberty") and text of the statute.
- **The zeroed-out penalty (Note 5f).** At $0 the payment raises no revenue, which [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] calls the essential feature of a tax; so is the mandate now an unconstitutional command? And if so, is it severable? In [[California v. Texas]] (2021) the Court (7–2) held the plaintiffs lacked standing because a mandate with no penalty inflicts no traceable injury, and never reached either question.
- **Memorable lines.** Roberts: "a gun to the head"; "economic dragooning"; "Congress could address the diet problem by ordering everyone to buy vegetables"; "four million outlaws"; "It is not our job to protect the people from the consequences of their political choices." Ginsburg: "the broccoli horrible"; "stunningly retrogressive"; "You will know it when you see it." Joint dissent: "commerce is everything"; "no whatever-it-takes-to-solve-a-national-problem power"; "save a statute Congress did not write."
