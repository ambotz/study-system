## What it is

Jefferson Davis's remarks in the Senate Chamber on January 21, 1861, announcing that Mississippi had declared her separation from the United States and that his functions as her senator were therefore at an end. He had represented Mississippi since 1857 and had been Secretary of War under Pierce; less than three weeks later he was chosen provisional president of the Confederacy.

The speech is assigned with [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]] and [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]] as the third position on secession, and it is the only one of the three that defends secession as lawful. It is short, and it is a legal argument rather than a political manifesto: Davis says explicitly that "[t]he occasion does not invite me to go into argument," and then makes four — on state sovereignty, on the difference between nullification and secession, on coercion, and on the Declaration of Independence.

Read it as a primary source for the Southern constitutional case. The last of the four arguments is an argument that the Declaration's equality principle does not extend to enslaved people, and it is set out here because the course needs the position stated accurately, not because it is defensible.

## What to notice

- **The premise is state sovereignty, and everything else follows from it.** "It is known to Senators who have served with me here, that I have for many years advocated, as an essential attribute of State sovereignty, the right of a State to secede from the Union." Secession is not a remedy for a particular wrong but an attribute of what a State is.
- **Allegiance runs to the State, not to the Union.** Davis says that even if he thought Mississippi were "acting without sufficient provocation, or without an existing necessity," he "should still, under my theory of the Government, because of my allegiance to the State of which I am a citizen, have been bound by her action." This is the practical content of the compact theory: the State is the political community whose judgment binds its citizens.
- **Nullification and secession are opposites, not degrees.** "Nullification and secession, so often confounded, are indeed antagonistic principles." Nullification "is a remedy which it is sought to apply within the Union, and against the agent of the States," available when "the agent has violated his constitutional obligation," and it works by an appeal "to the other States of the Union for a decision." Calhoun advocated it "because it preserved the Union."
- **Secession begins where nullification has failed.** "[W]hen the States themselves, and when the people of the States, have so acted as to convince us that they will not regard our constitutional rights, then, and then for the first time, arises the doctrine of secession in its practical application." The trigger is not a single unconstitutional act but a judgment that the other parties will no longer be bound.
- **The agency metaphor is the whole theory in one sentence.** "Secession... is to be justified upon the basis that the States are sovereign... each State is a sovereign, and thus may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever." The federal government is an agent holding delegated powers; a principal may revoke a delegation. [[Texas v. White]] answers exactly this: admission "was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body."
- **Why Davis needs the distinction between nullification and secession.** Jackson's Nullification Proclamation and Force Bill were the precedent for coercion, and Davis's separation move is what disarms them. "The phrase 'to execute the laws,' was an expression which General Jackson applied to the case of a State refusing to obey the laws while yet a member of the Union. That is not the case which is now presented."
- **The no-coercion argument, and why it is stronger than Buchanan's.** "[T]here are no laws of the United States to be executed within the limits of a seceded State." [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] argued that no enumerated power authorizes war on a State; Davis argues that there is nothing left to execute, which meets [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln's]] reframing of coercion as ordinary law enforcement against individuals head-on. Its weakness is that it is entirely parasitic: it holds only if the secession was valid, and Lincoln's answer is that the ordinance is void, so the laws still run.
- **War, not enforcement, is the only honest description.** "You may make war on a foreign State. If it be the purpose of gentlemen, they may make war against a State which has withdrawn from the Union." Davis concedes that the Union may use force; he denies that force can be called execution of the laws. That concession is worth noticing, because it means the dispute with Lincoln is not about whether force is possible but about what the seceded State has become.
- **The symmetry argument, offered as proof of good faith.** Davis recalls the demand to coerce Massachusetts after a fugitive slave was rescued in Boston and says his answer was the same: "it is her right to go, and I will neither vote one dollar nor one man to coerce her back." The rhetorical work is to present secession as a neutral structural principle rather than a sectional device.
- **The costs of leaving are accepted, and used as an argument.** A seceding State "surrenders all the benefits, (and they are known to be many,) deprives herself of the advantages, (they are known to be great,) severs all the ties of affection, (and they are close and enduring,)" and "thus divesting herself of every benefit, taking upon herself every burden, she claims to be exempt from any power to execute the laws of the United States within her limits." The argument is that the exit is a bargain, not a free option — which is his answer to Lincoln's charge that secession makes the Union "a rope of sand," in [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan's]] phrase.
- **The stated cause is the Declaration of Independence, read as an attack on slavery.** "She has heard proclaimed the theory that all men are created free and equal, and this made the basis of an attack upon her social institutions; and the sacred Declaration of Independence has been invoked to maintain the position of the equality of the races."
- **Davis's reading of the Declaration limits it to political communities.** "That Declaration of Independence is to be construed by the circumstances and purposes for which it was made." The colonists meant "that no man was born—to use the language of Mr. Jefferson—booted and spurred to ride over the rest of mankind; that men were created equal—meaning the men of the political community; that there was no divine right to rule; that no man inherited the right to govern; that there were no classes by which power and place descended to families." On that reading the Declaration is about hereditary aristocracy, not about slavery, and "[t]hey have no reference to the slave."
- **His two textual arguments for that reading.** First, the grievance against George III that he "endeavored to do just what the North has been endeavoring of late to do—to stir up insurrection among our slaves": if the Declaration had freed them, the charge would make no sense. Second, the Constitution treats the same class "as property" and for representation discriminates against them "as a lower caste, only to be represented in the numerical proportion of three fifths."
- **Note what Davis actually quotes.** He says "all men are created free and equal," which is not the Declaration's language; the Declaration says "all men are created equal." The interpolated "free" is the Massachusetts constitution's phrase, and the slip matters because the whole argument turns on what the sentence says.
- **This is Taney's reading of the Declaration, put to a different use.** [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] gave the same construction — that the language of the Declaration appears to embrace the whole human family, but that the men who framed it did not intend to include enslaved people — to establish that Black Americans could not be citizens. Davis uses it to establish a cause for secession. [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] contain Lincoln's answer: the Declaration's authors set up a standard maxim to be approximated over time, and it applies to all.
- **The closing move sits badly with everything before it.** "[W]hen you deny them, and when you deny to us the right to withdraw from a Government which thus perverted threatens to be destructive of our rights, we but tread in the path of our fathers when we proclaim our independence, and take the hazard." The path of the fathers is revolution, and [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] had said that "Secession is neither more nor less than revolution." Davis has just spent the speech insisting that secession is a constitutional right rather than revolution; the ending concedes that if the constitutional argument fails the revolutionary one is held in reserve.
- **What is missing is any text.** Davis cites no clause of the Constitution. The argument runs entirely on the premise that "the States are sovereign," which is what Lincoln attacked in the July 4, 1861 message quoted in the casebook's Note 3: "Much is said about the 'sovereignty' of the States; but the word, even, is not in the national Constitution," and no State "ever having been a State out of the Union."
- **Davis is the radical position that Note 4 distinguishes.** The casebook separates "moderate" secessionists, who justified secession as a response to an egregious violation of the South's constitutional rights, from radicals who held that "states could secede for whatever reason they chose." Davis is in the second camp: he would have been bound by Mississippi's decision even if he thought it unjustified. That matters, because the moderate argument survives Lincoln's refutation of state sovereignty and Davis's does not.
- **Apply Lincoln's contract argument to Davis.** Lincoln concedes the compact premise and still wins: "One party to a contract may violate it—break it, so to speak—but does it not require all to lawfully rescind it?" Davis's only answer is that the States are not ordinary parties but sovereigns, and that a sovereign's delegation to an agent is revocable at will. The exchange is the cleanest statement in the course of what turns on the locus of sovereignty, which is [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell's]] thesis.
- **Hypothetical: Mississippi secedes and then asks federal courts in Mississippi to keep sitting to enforce her citizens' contracts.** On Davis's theory there are no federal laws to execute there and no federal courts; the State cannot claim the Union's protections while denying its authority. This is the mirror of the question Note 4 of the [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln]] material puts to the Union — whether a State that has been denied the benefits of the Constitution may leave it.
- **Hypothetical: Congress accepts Mississippi's withdrawal by statute.** Davis wins on his own terms and does not need the sovereignty argument at all; [[Texas v. White]] expressly reserves revocation "through... consent of the States." That is worth saying in class, because it shows how much of the 1861 dispute was about unilateralism rather than about exit.
- **Where the compact theory came from.** The Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions of 1798, which the casebook's Note 2 to Lincoln's inaugural asks you to connect to secession. Davis's own answer is that they do not connect: nullification preserves the Union and secession dissolves it, and the two are "antagonistic." The counter is that both rest on the State's authority to judge the compact for itself, and once the State judges that the compact has failed, Davis's step follows.
- **Memorable lines.** "Nullification and secession, so often confounded, are indeed antagonistic principles." "[E]ach State is a sovereign, and thus may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever." "[T]here are no laws of the United States to be executed within the limits of a seceded State." "[W]e but tread in the path of our fathers when we proclaim our independence, and take the hazard."
