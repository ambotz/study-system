## What it is

The passage on the spending power from Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures, transmitted to the House on December 5, 1791 — written shortly after the Report on Public Credit that produced the assumption plan, and in the same season as his opinion on the Bank. The casebook prints it (4th ed., pp. 612–614) as the direct answer to [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill|Madison]]: the "general Welfare" clause is a distinct power to appropriate, limited only by the three express tax limitations and by a requirement that the object be general rather than local. It is the reading the Court adopted in [[United States v. Butler]] and has applied ever since.

## What to notice

- **The argument is built from the express limits, not from the grant.** Hamilton quotes the clause, then quotes the three qualifications the Constitution states — uniformity of "duties, imposts and excises," apportionment of "capitation or other direct tax," and the ban on taxing "articles exported from any state" — and reasons: "These three qualifications excepted, the power to raise money is plenary, and indefinite; and the objects to which it may be appropriated are no less comprehensive, than the payment of the public debts and the providing for the common defence and 'general Welfare.'" The Constitution took the trouble to state limits on this clause; the enumerated limits are therefore the limits. Compare the same inference running the other way in [[Express Limits on the Power to Tax]].
- **The surplusage canon, pointed the opposite way from Madison's.** "The terms 'general Welfare' were doubtless intended to signify more than was expressed or imported in those which Preceded; otherwise numerous exigencies incident to the affairs of a Nation would have been left without a provision." Madison says the broad reading makes the enumeration nugatory; Hamilton says the narrow reading makes "general Welfare" nugatory next to "debts" and "common defence." Both are arguments from redundancy, and they cancel — which is why neither side won on text alone.
- **Indefiniteness is the point, not a defect.** "The phrase is as comprehensive as any that could have been used; because it was not fit that the constitutional authority of the Union, to appropriate its revenues shou'd have been restricted within narrower limits than the 'General Welfare' and because this necessarily embraces a vast variety of particulars, which are susceptible neither of specification nor of definition." A power to meet unforeseen national needs cannot be enumerated in advance.
- **The judgment is Congress's.** "It is therefore of necessity left to the discretion of the National Legislature, to pronounce, upon the objects, which concern the general Welfare, and for which under that description, an appropriation of money is requisite and proper." Madison's fourth objection in the Bonus Bill veto — that general-welfare questions are "unsusceptible of judicial cognizance and decision" — is not a refutation of Hamilton but a description of him.
- **The specific objects Hamilton names are the whole New Deal in miniature.** "[W]hatever concerns the general Interests of learning of Agriculture of Manufactures and of Commerce are within the sphere of the national Councils as far as regards an application of Money." Agriculture is the object at stake in [[United States v. Butler]]; manufactures and commerce cover most of what the Commerce Clause cases fight over in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] through [[Wickard v. Filburn]].
- **The one real limit: general, not local.** "The only qualification of the generallity of the Phrase in question, which seems to be admissible, is this—That the object to which an appropriation of money is to be made be General and not local; its operation extending in fact, or by possibility, throughout the Union, and not being confined to a particular spot." Note how weak "or by possibility" makes it: an object that could in principle operate throughout the Union qualifies, even if it does not.
- **Spending is not regulating.** "A power to appropriate money with this latitude which is granted too in express terms would not carry a power to do any other thing, not authorised in the constitution, either expressly or by fair implication." Note 1 asks why Hamilton says this. The answer is that a power to give money away is not a power to command: Congress may fund a national university without acquiring power to tell anyone what to teach. That answer is the founding-era ancestor of [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]]'s holding that conditions on funds are judged by standards "less exacting" than direct regulation — and it is also why conditional spending turned out to be the hole in the argument. Hamilton assumed appropriation and command were different things; Dole's conditions make appropriation a way of commanding.
- **Note 1's Articles question.** Madison read "general welfare" as carrying its meaning from Article VIII of the Articles of Confederation. Hamilton never engages that, and the evidence is thin either way: the Articles' phrase sat in a requisition scheme in which Congress could not levy anything, so it never had to function as a limit on an operative taxing power. Be ready to say what would count as proof — drafting history, state ratification debates, contemporaneous usage — and that none of it is decisive.
- **The First Congress's record supports nobody.** It funded the Cape Henry lighthouse (Madison defending it under the commerce power), then mooted the general/local problem by taking over the expense of every lighthouse "from Maine to Georgia" — a local object made general by aggregation, which is the trick Hamilton's "or by possibility" invites. The same Congress rejected a voyage to Baffin's Bay to study the magnetic pole, a national university or subsidies to existing schools, and a loan to a struggling Maryland glass factory. "In each case the scope of the spending power was debated, but not resolved."
- **Madison's evasions are evidence about what he thought the stakes were.** The 1792 codfish subsidy was recharacterized as tax relief; the 1793 St. Domingo refugee relief was recharacterized as partial repayment of the French debt, avoiding "a right to Congress of expending, on objects of benevolence, the money of their constituents." A politician who needs a characterization is conceding that the substance is outside his own rule.
- **What are the limits of the Hamiltonian view?** Note 1 presses this, and the honest answer is: (i) the three express tax limits; (ii) general rather than local objects, unenforced in practice; (iii) independent constitutional prohibitions, which is where [[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]]'s fourth limit comes from; and (iv) no power to regulate acquired by the power to appropriate. Only the first and third have ever been enforced by a court.
