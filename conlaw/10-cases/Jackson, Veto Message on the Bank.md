## What it is

Andrew Jackson's message of July 10, 1832 returning to the Senate the bill "to modify and continue" the Second Bank's charter. The Bank's charter ran to 1836, but the Bank sought a twenty-year renewal in an election year; Jackson vetoed the bill on the ground that it was unconstitutional, and then won re-election on the veto.

The casebook prints the veto immediately after [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]], as the opposite answer to a single question: whether a President must take a constitutional question as settled against his own view. Its interest lies in its premise, not in banking — the casebook flags that this was a veto "on constitutional grounds that had been previously rejected by all three branches of the national government," and asks: "Should he have accepted that the Bank was constitutional because Presidents Washington and Madison had? Because the Supreme Court had in McCulloch v. Maryland? Or was he right to stick to his convictions about the Constitution?"

## What to notice

- **The oath argument, which is the whole of departmentalism in one sentence.** "Each public officer who takes an oath to support the Constitution swears that he will support it as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others." The Article VI oath runs to the Constitution, not to the Court's reading of it, so the duty it imposes is a duty of independent judgment. Everything else in the veto's constitutional section is an application of that premise.
- **Co-ordinate construction, symmetrically stated.** "The Congress, the Executive, and the Court must each for itself be guided by its own opinion of the Constitution." Jackson does not claim the President outranks the Court; he claims parity — "The opinion of the judges has no more authority over Congress than the opinion of Congress has over the judges, and on that point the President is independent of both."
- **The limiting phrase that keeps this short of defiance: "when acting in their legislative capacities."** "The authority of the Supreme Court must not, therefore, be permitted to control the Congress or the Executive when acting in their legislative capacities, but to have only such influence as the force of their reasoning may deserve." Jackson claims independence in deciding whether to *make* law, not a licence to disobey a judgment. Nothing in the veto says he would refuse to execute a decree against the United States, and that distinction is what makes the position defensible.
- **Precedent is counted, not weighed.** "Mere precedent is a dangerous source of authority, and should not be regarded as deciding questions of constitutional power except where the acquiescence of the people and the States can be considered as well settled." Jackson then tallies: "One Congress, in 1791, decided in favor of a bank; another, in 1811, decided against it. One Congress, in 1815, decided against a bank; another, in 1816, decided in its favor." Conclusion: "the precedents drawn from that source were equal."
- **The States supply a second count.** "If we resort to the States, the expressions of legislative, judicial, and executive opinions against the bank have been probably to those in its favor as 4 to 1." Note what this does to the argument from practice: Jackson accepts the *criterion* — settled acquiescence of people and States — and disputes only the tally. His disagreement with Madison is empirical before it is theoretical.
- **The narrowing of McCulloch.** "But in the case relied upon the Supreme Court have not decided that all the features of this corporation are compatible with the Constitution." Even conceding the judgment its full force, Jackson says it validated *a* bank, not *this* charter with these privileges. The veto therefore does not have to overrule McCulloch to reach its result.
- **McCulloch turned against itself, which is the cleverest move in the message.** Marshall said that where a law "is not prohibited and is really calculated to effect any of the objects intrusted to the Government, to undertake here to inquire into the degree of its necessity would be to pass the line which circumscribes the judicial department and to tread on legislative ground." Jackson reads that as a grant to him: "The principle here affirmed is that the 'degree of its necessity,' involving all the details of a banking institution, is a question exclusively for legislative consideration," so "it is the exclusive province of Congress and the President to decide whether the particular features of this act are necessary and proper." Judicial deference on degree of necessity becomes presidential authority over degree of necessity, because the President acts legislatively when he vetoes.
- **He then applies Marshall's own test to the details.** Many of the act's privileges "can not be supposed necessary for the purpose for which it is proposed to be created, and are not, therefore, means necessary to attain the end in view, and consequently not justified by the Constitution." The foreign-stockholder provision is the example: far from being "necessary and proper," it is "calculated to convert the Bank of the United States into a foreign bank, to impoverish our people in time of peace, to disseminate a foreign influence through every section of the Republic, and in war to endanger our independence."
- **The objection is to privilege, not to banking.** "A bank of the United States is in many respects convenient for the Government and useful to the people." What he denies is that the charter's particular grants are authorised: they are "unauthorized by the Constitution, subversive of the rights of the States, and dangerous to the liberties of the people." The concession matters for the cold call — Jackson is not a strict-construction purist about all national institutions.
- **The monopoly-valuation argument.** "Every monopoly and all exclusive privileges are granted at the expense of the public, which ought to receive a fair equivalent." He prices it: "The present value of the monopoly . . . is $17,000,000, and this the act proposes to sell for three millions." This is a takings-flavoured fairness argument dressed as constitutional analysis, and it shows how far the veto's reasoning ranges beyond Article I.
- **Equal protection of privilege, a generation before the Fourteenth Amendment.** "Many of our rich men have not been content with equal protection and equal benefits, but have besought us to make them richer by act of Congress." The consequence: legislation has "arrayed section against section, interest against interest, and man against man, in a fearful commotion which threatens to shake the foundations of our Union." His programme is to "take a stand against all new grants of monopolies and exclusive privileges, against any prostitution of our Government to the advancement of the few at the expense of the many."
- **The states'-rights theme is structural, not merely rhetorical.** "Nor is our Government to be maintained or our Union preserved by invasions of the rights and powers of the several States. In thus attempting to make our General Government strong we make it weak." The positive picture: the Government's "true strength consists in leaving individuals and States as much as possible to themselves—in making itself felt, not in its power, but in its beneficence; not in its control, but in its protection; not in binding the States more closely to the center, but leaving each to move unobstructed in its proper orbit." Set this against Marshall's supremacy and representation arguments in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]: the same Union, described as an orbit rather than a hierarchy.
- **Who resolves the disagreement on Jackson's view? The electorate.** "A general discussion will now take place, eliciting new light and settling important principles; and a new Congress, elected in the midst of such discussion, and furnishing an equal representation of the people according to the last census, will bear to the Capitol the verdict of public opinion." Departmentalism does not leave the question permanently open; it routes it to elections instead of to the Court. That is why the 1832 and 1834 results matter constitutionally and not just politically — see [[The Death of the Second Bank]].
- **The contrast with Cooper v. Aaron.** In [[Cooper v. Aaron]] the Court held that its own constitutional interpretation binds state officials as supreme law, and rested that on the Article VI oath — the same clause Jackson uses to reach the opposite conclusion. Two readings of one oath: swearing to support the Constitution means supporting it as the Court expounds it (Cooper), or as the officer himself understands it (Jackson). The distinguishing fact is who is resisting and how: Arkansas defied a judgment binding on parties before the Court, while Jackson declined to enact a new law. Cooper's rule survives that distinction; Jackson's veto does not offend it.
- **The parallel with Lincoln.** In [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] Lincoln accepts that [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] binds the parties while denying that it settles the political question of slavery in the territories, and argues that the political branches may work to have the decision overruled. That is Jackson's distinction — obedience to the judgment, independence in the legislative capacity — carried into a case where the stakes are much higher. Lincoln, unlike Jackson, concedes that a question genuinely settled over time does bind, which puts him between Jackson and [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette|Madison]].
- **Hypothetical: Congress re-passes the identical charter McCulloch upheld, word for word, and Jackson vetoes again.** The narrowing move ("have not decided that all the features") is unavailable, so the veto stands or falls on the oath argument alone. That is the pure case, and the casebook's questions are aimed at it.
- **Hypothetical: Jackson sits as a judge in a suit to enjoin a state tax on the Bank.** Nothing in the veto lets him ignore McCulloch there; he claims independence only for the legislative capacity. Ask whether a President may go further and decline to *enforce* a statute he thinks unconstitutional — that is [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] territory, and the veto does not reach it.
- **Hypothetical: the tally comes out the other way.** If every Congress since 1791 had voted for the Bank and the States had run 4 to 1 in its favour, Jackson's own test — "where the acquiescence of the people and the States can be considered as well settled" — would bind him. His departmentalism is thus weaker than it first appears: it is a theory about *unsettled* questions, which is exactly Madison's theory of liquidation with a stricter threshold.
- **Types of argument.** Text (the Article VI oath; "necessary and proper" applied to the charter's details); structure (three co-ordinate branches, each with the same duty; the States in their "proper orbit"); precedent and practice (the 1791–1816 tally and the 4-to-1 state count); consequences (a foreign-owned bank, sectional commotion, monopoly at public expense). What is missing is any argument from original meaning of the sort Jefferson and Madison made in [[The Bank Debate]] — see [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
