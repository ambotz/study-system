## What it is

The opening of Chapter 2 (The Separation of the National Powers), which introduces the three vesting clauses and catalogs the checks each branch holds against the others. It leads into [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]].

## What to notice

- **The three vesting clauses, side by side.**
  - Art. I, § 1: "All legislative Powers **herein granted** shall be vested in a Congress."
  - Art. II, § 1: "The executive Power shall be vested in a President."
  - Art. III, § 1: "The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish."
  - The asymmetry: only Article I says "herein granted." The difference is the textual root of the argument over whether Article II vests a general executive power. Justice Jackson rejects that reading in Youngstown.
- **The separation is "not absolutely pure."** Some powers, such as lawmaking, are allocated to two branches acting together. The shared allocations still serve checking.
- **Congress's checks on the President.** The power of the purse (it can end a war by cutting funds); Senate refusal to confirm nominees or ratify treaties; impeachment.
- **Congress's checks on the courts.** Altering their size, jurisdiction or budget; proposing amendments that overturn the Court's constitutional rulings (Amendments XI, XIV, XVI, XXVI); and impeachment of judges.
- **The President's checks on Congress.** The veto; setting enforcement priorities different from Congress's; large power in foreign affairs.
- **The President's checks on the courts.** Appointment of judges; control over which issues reach court, including by settling suits against the executive.
- **The courts' one check.** Holding that the other branches have acted unlawfully. "[B]ut it is a check that constrains every action those branches can take."
- **The setup for Youngstown.** Truman's seizure was unilateral, with no statutory authorization, so the case poses the question whether the President has an inherent legislative power or a power to act in emergencies.
