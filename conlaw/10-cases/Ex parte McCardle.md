## Rule

Congress may withdraw the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over a class of cases under the Exceptions Clause of Art. III, § 2, even while a case in that class is pending and has been argued, and the Court will not inquire into Congress's motive; the withdrawal reached only the statutory route it repealed, so other routes to the Court (the 1789 Act, original habeas) survived.

## Facts

- **McCardle, a civilian newspaper editor, was held by the military for trial before a military commission for publishing articles "alleged to be incendiary and libellous."** *A merits ruling threatened military Reconstruction itself; that is why Congress acted.*
- **The Circuit Court denied habeas corpus, and McCardle appealed under the Habeas Corpus Act of February 5, 1867, which gave an appeal to the Supreme Court.** *No other jurisdictional basis was invoked.*
- **The Court denied a motion to dismiss and heard argument on the merits.** *The withdrawal hit a pending, argued case, not only future ones.*
- **Before the Court decided, Congress repealed the 1867 appeal provision, including "the exercise of any such jurisdiction by said Supreme Court, on appeals which have been, or may hereafter be taken."** *The express reach to pending appeals left no room to read the repeal as prospective only.*
- **The repeal touched only appeals under the 1867 Act.** *Other habeas routes survived, which [[Ex parte Yerger]] then used.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (8–0; Chase, C.J., for the Court)

- The Court's appellate jurisdiction is conferred by the Constitution but "'with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make.'"
- The repealing act is a valid exception: "It is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception."
- A court whose jurisdiction has been withdrawn cannot decide the merits: "Without jurisdiction the court cannot proceed at all in any cause."
- The repeal applies to the pending case, because a repealed statute is treated "as if it never existed," "except as to transactions past and closed."
- The repeal withdraws only appeals under the 1867 Act and "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised."
- Appeal "DISMISSED FOR WANT OF JURISDICTION."

**Rejected**

- *Appellate jurisdiction comes from the Constitution itself, not from Congress, so a statute cannot take it away once the Court has lawfully acquired a case.* Chase: the jurisdiction is indeed "not derived from acts of Congress" and is "strictly speaking, conferred by the Constitution," but the same clause confers it only "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make," and "the power to make exceptions to the appellate jurisdiction of this court is given by express words."
- *Congress's purpose was to prevent a ruling on the constitutionality of Reconstruction in this very case, and a withdrawal aimed at one pending case is an attack on the judicial power, not an "exception."* Chase: "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature. We can only examine into its power under the Constitution."
- *The Court had already taken jurisdiction and heard argument, so the repeal came too late to affect this case.* Chase: under the rule of [[Norris v. Crocker]] and [[Insurance Company v. Ritchie]], "no judgment could be rendered in a suit after the repeal of the act under which it was brought and prosecuted."
- *Giving the repeal effect would deny the whole appellate power of the Court in habeas cases.* Chase: counsel "seem to have supposed" so, "[b]ut this is an error"; the repeal excepts only "appeals from Circuit Courts under the act of 1867."

## Context

McCardle is the leading case for the congressional power to strip jurisdiction, and it is the Class 15 companion to the other two checks on the Court: impeaching Justices ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]) and changing the Court's size ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]). The repeal was passed over President Johnson's veto on March 27, 1868, while his impeachment trial was under way ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]), and the Court postponed decision once the repeal passed.

Two sequels mark the limits. [[Ex parte Yerger]] (1869), decided months later, held that the Court still had appellate habeas jurisdiction through the 1789 Judiciary Act (habeas with certiorari), exactly the route McCardle's last paragraph reserved; so the stripping in McCardle closed one door and left another open. [[United States v. Klein]] (1872) held that Congress may not use a jurisdictional form to dictate the outcome of pending cases: Congress had directed courts to treat a pardon as proof of disloyalty and to dismiss for want of jurisdiction. Chase, writing again, distinguished McCardle: "If it simply denied the right of appeal in a particular class of cases, there could be no doubt that it must be regarded as an exercise of the power of Congress." But this was "to prescribe a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way," and Congress "has inadvertently passed the limit which separates the legislative from the judicial power."

Modern cases have read Klein narrowly. [[Bank Markazi v. Peterson]] (2016) upheld a statute changing the law for identified pending cases, and a plurality in [[Patchak v. Zinke]] (2018) upheld a statute directing courts to dismiss suits about a particular parcel of land. [[Felker v. Turpin]] (1996) upheld the habeas restrictions of AEDPA partly because, as in [[Ex parte Yerger|Yerger]], the Court's original habeas jurisdiction remained. The theoretical debate (Henry Hart's view that exceptions may not destroy the Court's essential role; Akhil Amar's reading of Art. III's "all Cases" language) is over whether McCardle's broad language holds when Congress closes every route, which McCardle itself did not face.

## Class layer

### Posture

- The Circuit Court for the Southern District of Mississippi denied habeas corpus and remanded McCardle to military custody; he appealed under the Act of February 5, 1867.
- The Supreme Court denied a motion to dismiss at the prior term and heard argument; Congress then repealed the appeal provision (Act of March 27, 1868); the Court then took up the effect of the repeal and dismissed on April 12, 1869.

### Facts

William McCardle, a Mississippi newspaper editor and not a member of the military, was arrested by federal military authority under the Military Reconstruction Acts and held for trial before a military commission on charges based on articles he published attacking Reconstruction. He sought habeas corpus in federal circuit court, lost, and appealed under the 1867 Habeas Corpus Act, a Reconstruction statute that expanded federal habeas and gave an appeal to the Supreme Court. After the Court denied the Government's motion to dismiss and heard the merits, Congress, fearing the Court would use the case to hold military Reconstruction unconstitutional, repealed the part of the 1867 Act authorizing Supreme Court appeals, expressly including appeals already taken. The Court held the case over and then decided only the jurisdictional question.

### Issue

- Does Congress have power under Art. III, § 2 to withdraw the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over habeas appeals under the 1867 Act?
- Does the repeal apply to an appeal already pending and argued?
- Does the repeal take away all of the Court's appellate jurisdiction in habeas cases?

### Holding

- Yes. Appellate jurisdiction exists "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make," and it is "hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception" than the repeal; motive is not examined.
- Yes. A repealed jurisdictional statute is treated as if it never existed except as to closed transactions, and the Act expressly reached pending appeals; the only remaining function is "announcing the fact and dismissing the cause."
- No. The repeal excepts only appeals from Circuit Courts under the 1867 Act and "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised."

### Rule

- The Exceptions Clause lets Congress remove classes of cases from the Court's appellate jurisdiction.
- A withdrawal of jurisdiction applies to pending cases, and the court must dismiss: "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law," and once it is gone "the only function remaining to the court is that of announcing the fact and dismissing the cause."
- Courts examine Congress's "power under the Constitution," not its "motives."
- Jurisdictional repeals are read to reach only what they name; other statutory routes survive.

### Reasoning

**(Chase, C.J., for the Court)**

- **Jurisdiction first.** "The first question necessarily is that of jurisdiction," for if the repeal takes the jurisdiction away, "it is useless, if not improper, to enter into any discussion of other questions."
- **Source of the jurisdiction.** Appellate jurisdiction is "not derived from acts of Congress" and is "strictly speaking, conferred by the Constitution," but it is conferred "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make."
- **The negative pregnant of the Judiciary Acts.** Following [[Durousseau v. United States]], Congress has "described affirmatively, its jurisdiction, and this affirmative description has been understood to imply a negation" of jurisdiction not described.
- **The repeal.** "The provision of the act of 1867 affirming the appellate jurisdiction of this court in cases of habeas corpus is expressly repealed." "It is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception."
- **Motive.** "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature. We can only examine into its power under the Constitution; and the power to make exceptions to the appellate jurisdiction of this court is given by express words."
- **Pending cases.** "When an act of the legislature is repealed, it must be considered, except as to transactions past and closed, as if it never existed"; [[Norris v. Crocker]] and [[Insurance Company v. Ritchie]] held "that no judgment could be rendered in a suit after the repeal of the act under which it was brought and prosecuted."
- **Judicial duty.** "[J]udicial duty is not less fitly performed by declining ungranted jurisdiction than in exercising firmly that which the Constitution and the laws confer."
- **The saving paragraph.** The repeal does not deny "the whole appellate power of the court, in cases of habeas corpus"; "The act of 1868 does not except from that jurisdiction any cases but appeals from Circuit Courts under the act of 1867. It does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised."

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous; no separate opinions.

### Cold-call notes

- **What exactly did Congress take away?** One statutory route: appeals from circuit courts under the 1867 Act. It did not touch the 1789 Act's habeas-with-certiorari route or the Court's original habeas power, and the Court said so. That is why McCardle is weaker authority for total stripping than its language suggests.
- **Distinguishing fact from [[Ex parte Yerger]].** Yerger, another military prisoner, came up through the route McCardle reserved (the 1789 Act), and the Court took jurisdiction. Same Court, same year, same subject: the difference is only the statutory door. McCardle plus Yerger means Congress had not actually cut off Supreme Court review of military detention.
- **Distinguishing fact from [[United States v. Klein]].** McCardle's statute removed a class of cases and left the Court to decide nothing; Klein's statute left the cases in court but told the Court how to decide them (pardon = proof of disloyalty) and which way to rule. Chase wrote both and drew the line himself: denying appeal in "a particular class of cases" is valid; "prescrib[ing] a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way" is not.
- **Hypo: Congress strips all federal jurisdiction, including the Supreme Court's and the lower courts', over challenges to a statute.** McCardle's text ("given by express words") supports it; Yerger and the saving paragraph show the Court did not have to face it. The arguments against are that Art. III says the judicial power "shall extend" to "all Cases" arising under federal law, that a constitutional claim would then have no forum (a due process problem if the claimant is detained, and a Suspension Clause problem for habeas; see [[Ex parte Merryman]] and [[Boumediene v. Bush]]), and that state courts would be left as final interpreters of federal law.
- **Hypo: Congress strips jurisdiction only over cases where the claimant challenges Reconstruction and wins.** One-sided stripping dictates outcomes, so it looks like Klein, not McCardle. The line is between removing a class of cases and removing the Court's ability to rule one way.
- **Hypo: Congress repeals the appeal provision after the Court has decided but before the mandate issues.** "[T]ransactions past and closed" are untouched, and after decision the case is closed as to the judgment; the repeal-of-jurisdiction rule reaches only undecided cases. Compare [[Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm]], which forbids Congress to reopen final judgments.
- **Motive.** The Court refuses to look at motive although everyone knew the motive. Compare the court-packing debate: the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] condemns the 1937 bill precisely for its purpose ("a change in the decisions of the Court"). A court applying McCardle could not reach that objection; the Senate could. The motive objection is a political constraint, not a judicial one.
- **Departmentalism from Congress's side.** [[Marbury v. Madison]] and [[Cooper v. Aaron]] make the Court's judgments binding, but McCardle shows Congress can decide which cases the Court gets. Jurisdiction stripping does not overrule the Court's view of the Constitution; it removes the occasion for stating it. Ask whether that is more or less aggressive than the 1802 repeal of the circuit judgeships, which [[Stuart v. Laird]] upheld without inquiring into Congress's purpose either.
- **The original-meaning argument.** The "exceptions and regulations" clause was, on one reading, about fact review (juries, the Seventh Amendment debate) and routine housekeeping, not about removing constitutional questions. [[The Federalist No. 78|Federalist No. 78]] and [[Brutus No. 11]] assume the Court will pass on the constitutionality of laws. McCardle's text-first answer ("express words") is the formalist reading; the "essential functions" view is structural.
- **Memorable lines.** "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law"; "It is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception"; "judicial duty is not less fitly performed by declining ungranted jurisdiction."
