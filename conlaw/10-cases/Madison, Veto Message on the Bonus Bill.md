## What it is

President Madison's veto message of March 3, 1817 — the last day of his presidency — rejecting the "Bonus Bill," which would have funded roads, canals and other "internal improvements." Madison first concluded that the bill exceeded the commerce power; the passage the casebook prints argues that it therefore exceeded the spending power as well. The casebook sets the message inside its introduction to the spending power (4th ed., pp. 608–612), which runs from the textual puzzle about where the power comes from, through the 1790 Assumption Controversy, to the framing of the two rival readings of the "general Welfare" clause. Madison's is the narrow reading: Congress may spend only in aid of a power enumerated elsewhere. Read against [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]], it poses the question [[United States v. Butler]] answered and [[South Dakota v. Dole]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] inherited.

## What to notice

- **The clause may not confer a spending power at all.** The casebook's first move is textual: "It is generally assumed that Article I, Section 8, Clause 1 empowers Congress not only to collect money through taxes but also to spend money ('for the common Defence and general Welfare'). But read it carefully. Does the clause actually say that? It reads like a limitation on the purposes for which taxes may be imposed." The clause grants a power "To lay and collect Taxes"; "to pay the Debts and provide for the common Defence and general Welfare" reads as the purpose limitation on that grant, not as a second grant.
- **Three candidate sources, each with a cost.** If the "general Welfare" clause is the source, it is odd that it never says "spend," and the power would not obviously cover money from non-tax sources such as sales of western land. If the Necessary and Proper Clause is the source, "Congress could spend money only in service of its other enumerated powers; there would be no free-standing power to spend" — Madison's position, reached by a different route. If Article IV, § 3's power "to dispose of . . . Property belonging to the United States" is the source (David Engdahl), the spending power is not in Article I at all. The casebook concludes that "the widespread assumption, both at the Founding and today, is that the basis for the spending power is indeed the 'general Welfare' clause."
- **The modern stakes are conditions, not objects.** "For modern purposes, the most significant question involving the spending power is the scope of conditions that Congress can attach to appropriations." Put bluntly: "may Congress tax the people of all fifty states, and then 'give' the money back only to those states that toe the federal line?"
- **The easy case and the hard case are set side by side.** Nobody doubts Congress may require that money appropriated to train state police be used to train state police. But "could Congress say that citizens of Arkansas will not get their Social Security checks unless the legislature repeals its religious freedom law? Or no Social Security checks for Coloradans, until the state repeals its recreational marijuana law?" The germaneness question in [[South Dakota v. Dole]] is the line between the two.
- **Conditional grants are not gifts.** "[F]ederal grants to states are not gifts or freebies, like money on the Free Parking square; the people of every state are coerced to pay taxes, and then get some of their money back in the form of federal spending." This is the premise behind the coercion inquiry: the state is not choosing whether to pay, only whether to get its citizens' money back.
- **The Assumption Controversy came first — a year before the Bank.** Hamilton's 1790 plan had the national government take over the states' unpaid Revolutionary War debts, financed by notes backed by national excise taxes on coffee, tea, wine and spirits, with the aim of securing "the economic and political supremacy of the national government." The objections were political (it rewarded speculators who had bought the notes "at pennies on the dollar from impoverished veterans"), sectional (Virginia had paid most of its debt and objected to "a bailout for the other states") and constitutional.
- **Stone's constitutional objection is the slippery-slope form of Madison's argument.** Representative Michael Stone of Maryland: "It does not appear to me that the debts of the particular States are the debts of the United States." And: "If Congress say they want money for some purpose which they conceive to be salutary to the United States, but which, at the same time, is not a constitutional object of their power, have they a right to levy duties for such a purpose? And if they have, where is the limit to which they may not go? Or where is the boundary by which they are restrained[?]"
- **Sherman's answer collapses the constitutional question into the policy question.** Roger Sherman split the issue into whether Congress was "authorized to assume the debts" and whether "prudence, policy, and justice, dictate the proposed measure," then argued that "Congress, by the Constitution, is authorized to levy money in all instances where, in their opinion, the expenditure shall be for the 'general welfare'; an answer, therefore, to the second of these questions would depend on the decision of the first: if prudence, policy, and justice dictated the assumption of the State debts, it must be for the general welfare that they should be assumed." That is the Hamiltonian reading stated as a syllogism — and it is exactly why Madison thought the reading unjudgeable.
- **The controversy was settled by a deal, not by a doctrine.** The House first voted assumption down; after the meeting of Hamilton, Madison and Jefferson, most state debts were assumed, and Virginia got reimbursement and the national capital. The constitutional question was never resolved, which is the pattern for the whole founding-era record.
- **Madison's compressed statement of the narrow view, from the Bank speech.** "No argument . . . could be drawn from the terms 'common defence and general welfare.' The power as to these general purposes was limited to acts laying taxes for them; and the general purposes themselves were limited and explained by the particular enumeration subjoined." Two moves in one sentence: "general Welfare" governs only the laying of taxes, and its content is fixed by the enumeration that follows.
- **The veto message's first argument is the redundancy canon.** Reading the power to the "general Welfare" clause "would be contrary to the established and consistent rules of interpretation, as rendering the special and careful enumeration of powers which follow the clause nugatory and improper." If "general Welfare" is a power, clauses 2 through 17 do no work.
- **Its second argument is that the reading proves too much.** It would give Congress "a general power of legislation instead of the defined and limited one hitherto understood to belong to them, the terms 'common defense and general welfare' embracing every object and act within the purview of a legislative trust."
- **Its third argument is about supremacy, and it is the federalism argument.** A general legislative power plus the Supremacy Clause means "subjecting both the Constitution and laws of the several States in all cases not specifically exempted to be superseded by laws of Congress." The enumeration is what keeps state law standing; dissolve the enumeration and you dissolve the states' reserved field. Compare [[McCulloch v. Maryland]], where Marshall accepts the enumeration and expands what counts as a means of executing it.
- **Its fourth argument is about justiciability, and it is the sharpest.** The broad reading would exclude "the judicial authority of the United States from its participation in guarding the boundary between the legislative powers of the General and the State Governments, inasmuch as questions relating to the general welfare, being questions of policy and expediency, are unsusceptible of judicial cognizance and decision." Madison predicts the result: "courts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress" ([[South Dakota v. Dole|Dole]]) and "[t]he discretion belongs to Congress" ([[Helvering v. Davis]]). The general-welfare limit became the least constraining of Dole's four limits precisely because Madison was right about who could enforce it.
- **The last paragraph answers the redundancy objection to Madison's own position.** If "general Welfare" were confined "to cases which are to be provided for by the expenditure of money," that would "still leave within the legislative power of Congress all the great and most important measures of Government, money being the ordinary and necessary means of carrying them into execution." A spending-only reading is no real limit, because almost everything government does costs money. Madison is arguing that a limit which bites only on non-monetary action is not a limit at all — a point that cuts against anyone who would make the spending power broad and the regulatory powers narrow.
- **Madison's practice was more flexible than his theory.** He justified the Cape Henry lighthouse appropriation under the commerce power, got the 1792 codfish subsidy recharacterized as "relief from federal taxes the industry would otherwise have had to pay," and recast aid to the St. Domingo refugees as partial repayment of the French debt — so that Congress could "relieve the sufferers" without "establishing a dangerous precedent" that would allow "a right to Congress of expending, on objects of benevolence, the money of their constituents." Each workaround concedes that the underlying object was outside the enumeration.
- **Note 1's hardest question is about the Articles.** Article VIII of the Articles of Confederation used "general welfare" too, and Madison assumed it carried the same meaning into the Constitution. Ask what evidence would settle that: the Articles' clause appears in a requisition provision where the states, not Congress, raised the money, so the phrase had no occasion to operate as a grant. That makes Madison's inference plausible on the Articles' own terms and weak as a claim about a document that replaced requisitions with direct taxation.
