## What it is

James Madison's essay of November 22, 1787, on controlling faction, reprinted with four casebook Notes (pp. 462–468). The casebook introduces it under "The Political Theory of American Federalism" as "The starting point for thinking about America's constitutional federalism." It is not named on the syllabus but sits inside the Class 16 page range. It argues that a large republic protects rights better than a small one, which turns the Anti-Federalist case for small republics on its head.

## What to notice

- **The problem is faction, and especially majority faction.** A faction is "a number of citizens, whether amounting to a majority or minority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community." A minority faction is beaten "by regular vote." The real danger: "When a majority is included in a faction, the form of popular government on the other hand enables it to sacrifice to its ruling passion or interest, both the public good and the rights of other citizens."
- **The complaint Madison answers is about the state governments.** Measures are "decided, not according to the rules of justice, and the rights of the minor party; but by the superior force of an interested and over-bearing majority." The target is the state legislatures of the 1780s: debtor relief, paper money, tender laws.
- **The causes of faction cannot be removed.** Destroying liberty is a cure "worse than the disease": "Liberty is to faction, what air is to fire." Giving everyone the same opinions is impossible while reason is "fallible." "The latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man."
- **Property is the main source.** "But the most common and durable source of factions, has been the various and unequal distribution of property." The protection of men's unequal faculties "is the first object of Government," so faction follows from good government itself.
- **Legislators are judges in their own cause.** "[W]hat are many of the most important acts of legislation, but so many judicial determinations, not indeed concerning the rights of single persons, but concerning the rights of large bodies of citizens[?]" Private debts, protective tariffs and tax apportionment are his examples; on taxes, "Every shilling with which they over-burden the inferior number, is a shilling saved to their own pockets."
- **Statesmen won't save you.** "Enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm." Relief lies "only ... in the means of controling its effects."
- **Pure democracy fails.** Citizens who "assemble and administer the Government in person" share passions and can act at once; such democracies "have in general been as short in their lives, as they have been violent in their deaths."
- **Representation is the first cure.** It will "refine and enlarge the public views, by passing them through the medium of a chosen body of citizens." Madison admits it can be "inverted" by men "of factious tempers." Large districts help: more "fit characters" to choose from, and it is harder for "unworthy candidates to practice with success the vicious arts."
- **Size is the second and main cure.** "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens." Even if a majority shares a motive, it is harder "to discover their own strength, and to act in unison."
- **The punchline is an argument for the Union over the states.** The advantage of a republic over a democracy "is enjoyed by a large over a small Republic—is enjoyed by the Union over the States composing it." Faction may "kindle a flame within their particular States, but will be unable to spread a general conflagration"; "a rage for paper money, for an abolition of debts, for an equal division of property" is less likely to "pervade the whole body of the Union."
- **The division of labor.** "The Federal Constitution forms a happy combination in this respect; the great and aggregate interests being referred to the national, the local and particular, to the state legislatures." This one sentence is the textual bridge from the extended-republic theory to enumerated powers.
- **Federalist 10 is a theory of national power, but not of unlimited national power.** It explains why a national government is safer for rights than the states, which is the premise behind a strong Congress, the Reconstruction Amendments and national civil-rights law. But the "happy combination" sentence keeps "local and particular" matters with the states. The same Madison argued in 1791 that the enumeration forbade the Bank ([[The Bank Debate]]). Extended-republic theory tells you why to have a national government; it does not tell you how far that government's powers reach.
- **The tension with modern federalism doctrine.** [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] and [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] defend the states as laboratories and as sites of accountability. Federalist 10 says the states are where faction does its worst work. A student defending strong national power has Madison of 1787 on her side; one defending enumerated limits has Madison of 1791.
- **Note 1: human nature.** Madison occupies the middle ground the Note describes: he is neither utopian (faction is "sown in the nature of man," so abolishing property would not produce angels) nor Hobbesian (he rejects destroying liberty). His answer is institutional design, not moral reform, because "neither moral nor religious motives can be relied on as an adequate control."
- **Note 2: rejected solutions.** He rejects (a) destroying liberty, (b) uniformity of opinion, (c) enlightened statesmen, (d) moral and religious restraint, and (e) pure democracy. The rejections hold up, but the essay never considers judicial enforcement of rights as a cure, which is the answer the twentieth century chose.
- **Note 3: the Electoral College.** Requiring a winner to assemble support across many states is the extended-sphere logic applied to elections: a concentrated regional majority cannot win alone. Lincoln in 1860 is the exception, made possible by a four-way race. Push back: the winner-take-all allocation, not the College itself, does most of the work.
- **Note 4: size.** Madison treated the thirteen states as a large republic relative to the states "composing it." On his logic today's United States should be even safer from majority faction; the counterpoint is that modern communication weakens his claim that distance makes it hard for a majority "to discover their own strength." A world federation would stretch the other side of his "mean": representatives "too little acquainted" with local circumstances.
