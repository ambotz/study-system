## What it is

Two short casebook notes closing the Bank unit (pp. 506–508). "The Death of the Second Bank" narrates the Bank War from the 1832 veto to the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. "The Bank and Constitutional Interpretation" is the unit's review note: it runs the Bank question back through the five types of constitutional argument (p. 41) and then poses the question the whole Assignment has been building to — "Who interprets the Constitution?"

Read the second note as the syllabus's own outline of what the exam wants from Classes 16–17, and the first as the historical fact that makes the question bite: the Bank lost, after it had won in court.

## What to notice

- **The sequence after the veto.** "President Jackson's veto set off a titanic struggle with the Senate." Re-elected, Jackson ordered the Treasury Secretary to withdraw the federal deposits "(to destroy it)"; the Secretary refused; Jackson "fired him for insubordination," installing Attorney General Roger Taney, "a fiercely partisan ally whom President Jackson would later appoint to be chief justice." Taney withdrew the deposits and the Senate censured the President.
- **The removal fight is an Article II episode hiding inside a federalism unit.** Jackson removed a Cabinet officer for refusing to exercise a statutory discretion the way the President wanted, and the Senate's censure was the institutional response. That is [[The Decision of 1789]]'s question in a harder form — not whether the President may remove, but whether removal may be used to control a subordinate's own judgment — and it is the direct ancestor of the removal cases in [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]], [[Trump v. Slaughter]] and [[Trump v. Cook]]. Note the irony for Class 7: the two Banks are the historical tradition Slaughter invoked to shield the Federal Reserve, and the Second Bank was killed by exactly the removal power those cases were about.
- **Elections did the work Jackson said they would.** "The Bank tried to save itself by triggering a financial panic, but President Jackson succeeded in persuading the public that the panic was not his fault but the Bank's." The question went "to the public in the 1834 midterm elections," Jackson's allies won, and the new Senate expunged the censure from its records. This is the mechanism the veto message predicted — the "verdict of public opinion" delivered by "a new Congress" — actually operating. Departmentalism with an electoral tiebreaker, and it worked.
- **The result held for seventy years, and not because a court said so.** "Although Congress twice passed bills recreating the Bank in the 1840s, President John Tyler vetoed them on the same grounds of unconstitutionality that had been cited by President Jackson." Then: "No institution remotely resembling the Bank of the United States would be created until the passage of the Federal Reserve Act in 1913." Two Presidents' constitutional objections, sustained by one-third of one chamber each time, settled national banking policy for three generations.
- **The casebook's central observation, and the best single line to have ready.** "Today, its main legacy is Chief Justice Marshall's opinion in McCulloch v. Maryland and the precedent it set of broad national power. Yet this is not how the matter seemed to be settled in the nineteenth century. President Jackson won the Bank War, killing the Bank for the next seventy years. The resolution seemed to reaffirm the Jeffersonian view of more limited national government powers."
- **What it means that the Bank died politically rather than judicially.** [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] settled the legal question — Congress *may* charter a bank — and settled nothing about whether it *would*. A holding that a power exists leaves entirely open the practice of exercising it, and the practice is decided by the actors who control whether a bill becomes law. For seventy years the operative constitutional answer in the country was Jackson's, not Marshall's, even though Marshall's was the law. Constitutional meaning as lived is made outside the courts at least as much as inside them.
- **This cuts against reading McCulloch as liquidating the question.** [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette|Madison]] thought practice through 1816 had fixed the meaning by "a construction put on the Constitution by the Nation." The Bank War shows that a liquidation can come unstuck: a nation that had acquiesced for forty years withdrew its acquiescence, and did so through elections. Ask whether liquidation is therefore a claim about settlement at a moment, or a claim that can be defeated by later practice. The same doubt reaches [[The Decision of 1789]], where early practice is doing much heavier lifting.
- **Two senses of "settled" that the unit keeps sliding between.** Legally settled (a precedent no court will revisit) and politically settled (a question no actor reopens). The Bank was the first after 1819 and never the second. Sort every claim in the unit — Marshall's, Madison's, Jackson's, and the casebook's closing questions — into one bucket or the other before answering.
- **Textual argument and the dictionary problem.** (First review question.) Johnson's *Dictionary of the English Language* (6th ed. 1785) gives *necessary* "1. Needful; indispensably requisite," and *proper* "1. Peculiar; not belonging to more; not common. . . . 3. One's own. . . . 5. Fit; accommodated; adapted; suitable; qualified." The note's own objection: dictionaries may "introduce too much indeterminacy and subjectivity, because the interpreter has to choose which dictionary to consult and which meaning to use." Johnson helps both sides — "indispensably requisite" is Jefferson's reading, "fit; suitable" is Hamilton's and Marshall's — which is the point.
- **The historical-context argument.** Jefferson's claim that "the Philadelphia Convention considered giving Congress a power to charter corporations and deliberately decided not to." The standard answers: the Convention record is silent on *why* it was dropped, the rejection may have concerned a general incorporation power rather than incidental incorporation, and unsuccessful proposals are weak evidence of meaning. The note pairs it with the better question — "What is the relationship of the Necessary and Proper Clause to the background principle of incidental powers?"
- **The four structural arguments, worth memorising as a set.** (1) the Clause "cannot decrease the powers of Congress because it appears with the list of congressional powers in Article I, Section 8 instead of with the restraints in Article I, Section 9"; (2) "'Necessary' cannot mean 'absolutely necessary,' because Article I, Section 10 uses the phrase 'absolutely necessary'"; (3) "The omission of the word 'expressly' in the Tenth Amendment, given its inclusion in the Articles of Confederation, indicates that Congress has implied powers under the Constitution"; (4) "The clause cannot be read to justify most of what the national government does, or there would be no point in enumerating its powers." Arguments (1)–(3) are Marshall's and are genuinely strong but narrow — each defeats a maximalist reading without fixing a standard. Argument (4) is the only one pushing the other way, and it is the ancestor of the limits in [[United States v. Lopez]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]. The note's own question is the right one: "Do any lead inexorably to a conclusion about the Bank's constitutionality?" None does.
- **"Should McCulloch have simply affirmed in a few sentences because of settled practice?"** Marshall could have. He did not, and the elaborate opinion is what survived the political defeat — which is the answer to the question. An opinion resting only on practice would have been worth nothing once practice reversed in 1832.
- **Consequences, both directions.** A narrow reading would "hobble the national government's ability to solve problems the states could not solve by themselves"; a broad one would "risk concentrating all governmental power in the national government." Add the institutional question the note appends: "Once Congress and the president have determined that an institution such as the Bank is 'necessary,' should the courts defer to their expertise?" Marshall's answer (degree of necessity is legislative) is deference; Jackson's use of that answer shows how deference to the political branches can become a weapon against the Court's own judgment.
- **The note's framing of the whole doctrinal question.** "[I]s it really possible that 'necessary and proper' could be limited to laws that are absolutely necessary, The One Thing We Must Do Or The Sky Will Fall? On the other hand, is it really possible that it allows Congress to do anything 'convenient' or 'useful' for exercising an enumerated power? And if the answer lies somewhere between those extremes, where is it exactly?" Have a formulation ready: a means that is genuinely incidental to an enumerated end — carrying that power into execution rather than constituting a new great power — and not barred by the letter or spirit of the Constitution.
- **The closing questions are the unit's thesis.** "Who interprets the Constitution?" Courts obviously must, because Article III gives them "the judicial Power" over cases "arising under this Constitution." "But are courts the only government actors who must interpret the Constitution? How should each branch respond to the interpretations made by other branches? And when does a decision by the Supreme Court finally settle a constitutional question? The various actors in the controversy over the First and Second Banks had to answer these important questions for themselves, and they often disagreed."
- **Line up the four answers the unit supplies.** Marshall: the Court decides, and practice is at most a makeweight on a doubtful question. Madison: the Nation decides by settled practice, and an officer defers to it against his own view. Jackson: each officer decides for himself in his own capacity, and elections break the tie. [[Cooper v. Aaron|Cooper]] (1958): the Court's exposition binds other officials as supreme law. Be able to say which of these the Bank War vindicated in fact (Jackson's) and which the law later adopted (Cooper's).
- **Hypothetical: Congress charters a Third Bank in 1841 and Tyler signs it.** Nothing in McCulloch or the veto tradition prevents it; the constitutional question was open in the only sense that mattered — whether anyone with a veto would object. The lesson for the exam: identifying the holding of McCulloch tells you almost nothing about what the government will actually be permitted to do.
- **Hypothetical: a modern President vetoes an appropriation for an agency the Court has upheld, giving only her own constitutional objection.** Entirely lawful on Jackson's theory and on ordinary veto practice, and no court could review it. Judicial supremacy has never constrained the veto, which is the enduring practical truth behind departmentalism.
