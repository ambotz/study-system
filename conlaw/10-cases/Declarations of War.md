## What it is

Two joint resolutions of Congress printed for comparison: the Declaration of War Against Japan
(December 8, 1941), the day after Pearl Harbor, and the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (August 7,
1964), cited as the authority for the Vietnam War. The casebook's notes add Korea (no
declaration, no authorization), Vietnam litigation, three unauthorized uses of force (Cuba 1962,
Iran 1980, Panama 1989), covert operations, and the lead-in to the War Powers Resolution.

The casebook's framing question (pp. 313–314): if "declare War" gives Congress control over
initiating sustained hostilities, "what counts as a legally effective authorization"? Must
Congress "formally and literally 'declare' war," or does a statute authorizing force suffice?

## What to notice

- **The Japan declaration does three things.** It recognizes a war already begun ("the state of
  war . . . which has thus been thrust upon the United States is hereby formally declared"); it
  authorizes and directs the President "to employ the entire naval and military forces"; and it
  pledges "all of the resources of the country." Recognition, authorization and commitment are
  separable functions, and later resolutions keep only some of them.
- **"Thrust upon" echoes the Prize Cases.** Congress declares a war it did not start, just as the
  1846 Mexico act recognized war "existing by the act of the Republic of Mexico." Note 1 asks
  whether the declaration was legally necessary to fight Japan after Pearl Harbor. On
  [[The Prize Cases]], no: the President was "bound to resist force by force." What the
  declaration adds is authority for full-scale offensive war to "a successful termination," and
  the domestic and international legal incidents of a formal state of war (enemy property, trade,
  treaties) that Nelson's dissent catalogues.
- **Tonkin § 1 is an approval, not a grant.** Congress "approves and supports the determination
  of the President, as Commander in Chief, to take all necessary measures to repel any armed
  attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression." It
  presupposes that the President already has the power and endorses his use of it.
- **Tonkin § 2 is the open-ended part.** The United States is "prepared, as the President
  determines, to take all necessary steps, including the use of armed force," to assist any
  SEATO member or protocol state "requesting assistance." The words "as the President
  determines" leave the choice of war to the President, which is the same drafting feature the
  casebook presses in the 2001 AUMF ("he determines").
- **Tonkin § 3: the President decides when it ends.** It expires when the President determines
  that peace and security are "reasonably assured," or earlier "by concurrent resolution of the
  Congress." After [[INS v. Chadha]] a concurrent-resolution termination would be invalid,
  leaving termination to the President or to a veto-proof statute.
- **Declaration versus authorization: is there a constitutional difference?** The better answer
  is no difference in the power to initiate. What the Convention placed in Congress was the
  decision, and a statute authorizing force is Congress's decision (see [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]). The differences are elsewhere:
  - Scope. A declaration commits the nation to general war; an authorization can be limited, as
    the Quasi-War statutes were ([[Bas v. Tingy]]; [[Talbot v. Seeman]]: Congress may authorize
    "general hostilities" or "partial hostilities").
  - Legal incidents. A formal declaration triggers the status of war in domestic and
    international law; an authorization may not.
  - Specificity. An authorization that delegates the choice of enemy or theatre ("as the
    President determines") raises a nondelegation question that a declaration against a named
    enemy does not.
  - Politics. Congress may prefer an authorization precisely because it avoids the formal
    commitment and diffuses responsibility, which is the casebook's parenthetical question.
- **Korea (Note 2).** Truman sent forces without a declaration or an authorization; the Security
  Council approved (the Soviet Union was boycotting); Congress kept appropriating. The questions:
  - Does U.N. approval substitute for Congress? No on the text: the U.N. treaty does not itself
    commit members to use force, and a treaty is made by President and Senate, not the House.
    The casebook's parenthetical asks whether a treaty committing the nation to war would even be
    consistent with the Constitution, since it would bypass the House.
  - Does funding count as authorization? That is the weakest form of category-one support.
    Congress cannot easily defund troops already in the field, so appropriations are poor evidence
    of a decision to go to war. WPR § 8(a)(1) later rejects inference from appropriations.
  - Youngstown link: Truman's troops in Korea are the war Truman invoked to justify the steel
    seizure. The Court held the seizure unlawful without deciding whether the war was lawful.
- **Vietnam (Note 3).** Tonkin plus years of appropriations plus the eventual funding cutoff.
  [[Orlando v. Laird]] (2d Cir. 1971) found the challenge justiciable but upheld the war because
  "[t]he Congress and the Executive have taken mutual and joint action." That is a category-one
  analysis built from cumulative congressional acts. The Supreme Court never decided the merits.
- **Is funding Congress's real war power?** Note 3 asks it directly. The funding cutoff, not the
  1971 repeal of Tonkin, ended American involvement in Vietnam. The better view is that the purse
  is an enforcement tool for the power to authorize, not a substitute for it; otherwise the
  Declare War Clause would do no work beyond the Appropriations Clause.
- **Three unauthorized actions (Note 4).** Run each through the Convention carve-out and the
  Youngstown categories:
  - Cuba 1962 "quarantine": a blockade, the same belligerent measure as in [[The Prize Cases]],
    but with no attack on the United States. Hard to justify as repelling; the argument for it
    has to be that the missiles were an imminent threat and the quarantine a limited response.
  - Iran 1980 rescue raid: protection of citizens held hostage by a foreign state. The strongest
    of the three for unilateral action because the objective was rescue, not the defeat of an
    enemy.
  - Panama 1989 invasion: ground troops, regime removal, seizure of a head of state. Closest to
    "commencing" war, defended on protection of Americans in the Canal Zone.
- **Covert operations (Note 5).** The Intelligence Authorization Act of 1991 permits covert
  action where the President finds it "necessary to support identifiable foreign policy
  objectives" and "important to the national security," in writing within 48 hours, reported to
  the intelligence committees (or to eight members). That is a statutory framework for
  category-one authority with a reporting check; it assumes rather than resolves whether covert
  lethal force is "war."
- **Political question.** Note 3 closes by asking whether war-powers disputes are nonjusticiable.
  The casebook's answer by implication: where a proper case turns on the allocation of powers,
  as in [[The Prize Cases]], there is no bar in principle. The lower courts split on ripeness and
  political question in the Gulf War cases (see [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]]).
