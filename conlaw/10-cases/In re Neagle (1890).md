## Snapshot

A deputy marshal assigned by the Attorney General to guard Justice Field on circuit shot and killed David Terry, who had assaulted Field on a railway platform. California charged him with murder; a federal circuit court discharged him on habeas before any state trial, and the Supreme Court affirmed. No statute authorized the bodyguard, but "law of the United States" in Rev. Stat. § 753 covers obligations inferrible from the Constitution, which the Take Care Clause reaches. Article II, § 3 and Article VI carry the holding.

## Issue

- Does "an act done . . . in pursuance of a law of the United States" in Rev. Stat. § 753 reach an act taken under executive authority that no statute confers?
- Was Neagle's killing of Terry within that authority and necessary to it?
- May California try him, or does the writ discharge him before any state proceeding?

## Rule

A federal officer is held "in pursuance of a law of the United States" for habeas purposes even when no statute authorizes what he did, because the duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed reaches obligations growing out of the Constitution itself. Protection of the judges and the mails falls within that duty, an officer who performs it is beyond state criminal punishment, and the habeas court may decide the necessity of his act.

- "[A]ny obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument, or any duty of the marshal to be derived from the general scope of his duties under the laws of the United States, is a 'law,' within the meaning of this phrase."
- The duty is not confined to enacted text but takes in "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself, our international relations, and all the protection implied by the nature of the government under the constitution."

## Operative facts

- **In the Sharon litigation a federal circuit court on which Field sat declared Sarah Althea Hill's marriage contract a forgery; she then married David S. Terry, a former Chief Justice of California.** *The origin of the grudge, and why the threatened institution is a federal court.*
- **On September 3, 1888, when Field ruled against her, Hill accused him from the courtroom of having been bought; Terry struck the marshal in the face and reached for a bowie knife, and both Terrys were sentenced for contempt.** *A credible threat, recorded by a court; without it the protective order is unreasonable.*
- **Each Terry afterwards threatened Field's life, Terry saying of the danger, "Well, that's as good a thing as I want to get."** *Fixes the threat on Field in his judicial capacity, bringing the "peace of the United States" into play.*
- **On April 27, 1889, Attorney General W. H. H. Miller directed the marshal to employ special deputies so that the judges would "feel entirely safe and free from anxiety"; Neagle was appointed and travelled with Field.** *The authority is executive and nothing more — a letter, under no statute — which makes "law" the question.*
- **At Lathrop on August 14, 1889, Terry struck Field twice from behind and "had his arm drawn back and his fist doubled up, apparently to strike a third blow."** *Gives Neagle a defense-of-another justification needing no federal authority.*
- **Told "Stop! stop! I am an officer!", Terry thrust his hand toward his bosom, "as Neagle felt sure, with the purpose of drawing a bowie-knife"; Neagle fired twice.** *Necessity rests on Neagle's belief, not on what Terry in fact carried.*
- **Sheriff Cunningham held Neagle on a state murder warrant, and on August 16, 1889 Neagle petitioned the circuit court for habeas corpus, alleging imprisonment "for an act done in pursuance of a law of the United States."** *The federal question arrives as a habeas pleading rather than a trial defense, which is how it escapes the state courts.*
- **The circuit court (Sawyer, Circuit Judge, with Sabin, District Judge) took testimony and discharged him; the sheriff appealed, and the Court decided on April 14, 1890.** *The writ withdrew the case from the state courts instead of reviewing them, and the Court holding Field's protection required was the Court he sat on.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (6–2; Miller, J., for the Court; Field, J., took no part)

- **"Law" is not limited to statutes.** The narrow reading would be "an unwarranted restriction" of a law "designed to extend in a liberal manner the benefit of the writ of habeas corpus."
- **The protective power exists without Congress.** "We cannot doubt the power of the President to take measures for the protection of a judge . . . threatened with a personal attack."
- **The killing was justified, on the habeas record.** It "became necessary for the protection of Judge Field . . . to kill Terry."
- **Discharge affirmed.**

**Miller, J., for the Court**

- **The statutory question comes first, and the answer is a definition rather than a balance.** Any obligation "fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument" is a "law," and everything after that locates such an obligation.
- **It is located in Article II, § 3.** The opinion concedes that no "special act of Congress" authorizes marshals "in express terms to accompany the judges," treating that absence as its premise rather than an obstacle.
- **Necessity from consequences.** If the mails "are liable to be robbed," no one can doubt the President's authority "to make an order for the protection."
- **A federal peace, and a federal peace officer.** "That there is a peace of the United States; that a man assaulting a judge of the United States while in the discharge of his duties violates that peace." Section 788 supplies the officer, so the marshal stands to that peace as the sheriff stands to the State's.

**Lamar, J., dissenting (joined by Fuller, C.J.)**

- **Not about the killing, but about who decides.** "Our dissent is not based on any conviction as to the guilt or innocence of the appellee." The question is whether Neagle "shall in this ex parte proceeding be discharged and delivered from any trial or further inquiry in any court, state or federal."
- **The word "law."** "[A] wholly inadmissible construction is placed on the word 'law,' as used in that statute." Habeas is itself a creature of enacted law, so a statute freeing a prisoner held under "a law" cannot be satisfied by an inference from structure.
- **A defense, not an exemption.** Neagle "is answerable to the courts of the state of California, and to them alone," and "[n]o officer or employe of the United States is placed by his position . . . above responsibility to the legal tribunals of the country."

## Arguments rejected

- *"Law of the United States" means an enacted law; Congress has legislated in detail about marshals and never authorized bodyguards for judges.* Miller: the phrase covers "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible" from the Constitution, and the duty to protect the courts is itself the law being executed.
- *A State's power to punish a homicide on its own soil is the core of its reserved authority.* Miller: the officer was doing what federal law required, and the Supremacy Clause leaves no room for a State to punish performance of a federal duty.
- *Whether Neagle was justified is a question for a California jury, and he may raise his federal defense at trial and on writ of error.* Miller: the habeas evidence showed the killing was necessary, and an officer held for doing his duty need not stand trial to establish it.
- *Even accepting a protective power, it must come from Article II's enumeration, and guarding a Justice on a train executes nothing Congress enacted.* Miller: on that view the government would be "without the means of self-protection."

## Where it sits

**Implied executive power.** Neagle is the executive half of a pair with *In re Debs* (1895), where the Attorney General won an injunction against the Pullman strike on an implied authority to protect federal instrumentalities. Together they mark the high-water line of implied executive power before Youngstown pulled it back.

**Supremacy over state process.** The jurisdictional half belongs with *Tennessee v. Davis* (1880), which let a federal revenue officer remove a state murder prosecution, and with the line from [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]: a State may not obstruct or punish federal operations. [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] is the mirror image.

**Against Youngstown.** [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] is where the class turns. Clark's concurrence cites Neagle only in a footnote listing decisions that "have upheld the exercise of presidential power"; Black's opinion for the Court treats the absence of a statute as fatal.

**The oath, and Article VI.** The oath runs to the Constitution rather than to Congress, the seed of departmentalism in [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] and the target of [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]], while "in Pursuance thereof" — [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]]'s textual hook — suggests enacted law, which is Lamar's reading.

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

- **What was the statute, and what was the law?** The only statutes in the chain are Rev. Stat. § 753, the habeas remedy, and § 788, which gives marshals sheriffs' powers; neither authorizes protecting a Justice. The "law" Neagle executed is the President's Article II duty, mediated by a letter and a marshal's appointment, neither of which is law.
- **Is Neagle a Youngstown category two case?** Jackson's second category, the "zone of twilight" where Congress has neither granted nor denied authority, is the natural home for Neagle. Category one is arguable because § 788 is a congressional grant; category three because Congress occupied the field and the omission was deliberate. Miller never reaches for congressional authorization, which is why the opinion reads as independent Article II power.
- **What would Black have done with Neagle?** Black says presidential power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself," which is Neagle's answer in substance. The difference is that Black treats lawmaking as what the President may not do, and Miller does not think an order to protect a judge is lawmaking.
- **Vary the officer and the act.** A deputy marshal who shoots a man threatening a federal courthouse is squarely within Neagle, and a Secret Service agent who shoots an assassin is the same logic. An off-duty officer who kills a man in a bar fight has no federal duty.
- **The tension with the Take Care cases.** [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]] holds that the decision to prosecute is executive and the courts cannot compel it; [[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]] holds that a general abdication of enforcement is reviewable. Both read "take Care" against enacted statutes, while Neagle reads the clause as authority where Congress has passed nothing, so the duty turns partly on the executive's own view of its implied obligations.
- **Lamar's strongest point, and the answer.** The question was never whether Neagle would be convicted but whether he must be tried. The answer is institutional: an officer who must stand trial in a hostile state court for doing his duty is deterred from doing it, so the protection runs to the process, not merely the judgment.
- **Doctrine against original meaning.** The Take Care Clause imposes a duty and sits among the President's obligations rather than his powers, so reading it as a grant of power is structural rather than textual. The originalist defense is that executive power at common law included keeping the peace.
- **Memorable lines.** "[A]ny obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law'." "[T]hat there is a peace of the United States."
