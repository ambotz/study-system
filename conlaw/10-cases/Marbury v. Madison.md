## Rule

A court deciding a case must treat the Constitution as "a superior, paramount law," so it may not give effect to a statute "repugnant to the constitution"; and Article III's list of the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction is exclusive, so Congress cannot enlarge it. Along the way, the Court holds that an officer with a vested, fixed-term right may obtain a judicial remedy against a department head who withholds a ministerial act, while discretionary "political" acts are "only politically examinable."

## Facts

- **Adams signed Marbury's commission and the Secretary of State sealed it, but it was never delivered.** *Pivotal to the right: the appointment is complete on signature, and delivery is "a practice directed by convenience, but not by law."*
- **The statute gave justices of the peace five-year terms.** *An officer removable at will would have no vested right, and the question when the appointment was complete would be "of no concern."*
- **On Jefferson's instruction, Madison withheld the undelivered commissions.** *Delivering a completed commission is a ministerial duty on which individual rights depend, not an act of executive discretion.*
- **Marbury sought mandamus in the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction.** *Fatal: he wins on right and remedy and loses on forum.*
- **Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 authorized the Court to issue mandamus to "persons holding office, under the authority of the United States."** *Marshall reads § 13 as conferring original jurisdiction in such cases, which creates the conflict with Article III; a narrower reading of § 13 would have avoided it.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (unanimous; Marshall, C.J., for the Court)

- **Right.** Marbury was appointed when Adams signed the commission; the seal is "conclusive testimony" of the signature and of "the completion of the appointment." Because the office ran five years independent of the executive, the appointment "conferred on him a legal right to the office," and withholding the commission is "violative of a vested legal right."
- **Remedy.** The laws afford a remedy. "The very essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws, whenever he receives an injury." Where "a specific duty is assigned by law, and individual rights depend upon the performance of that duty," the courts may examine it; where officers act as the President's political agents in matters of discretion, "their acts are only politically examinable."
- **Mandamus is the proper remedy.** The writ lies to an officer to perform a duty "not depending on executive discretion," and a head of department's "exalted station" does not bar it. "This, then, is a plain case for a mandamus."
- **But not from this Court.** Section 13 purports to authorize the writ to officers; Article III gives the Court original jurisdiction only in cases affecting ambassadors, ministers and consuls and cases in which a State is a party. A mandamus to an officer "for the delivery of a paper" is original, not appellate. The statute is therefore "not . . . warranted by the constitution."
- **An unconstitutional statute cannot be applied.** "[A] law repugnant to the constitution is void; and . . . courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument." "The rule must be discharged."

**Rejected**

- *The appointment was incomplete until the commission was delivered and accepted; an undelivered commission confers nothing.* Transmission "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law." If possession of the paper were the office, "fire or theft" could destroy an office; the law makes a copy from the record equal to the original.
- *Delivering or withholding a commission is a political act committed to the executive alone, and courts cannot examine it.* Whether a department head's act is examinable "must always depend on the nature of that act." When the legislature assigns a specific duty on which individual rights depend, the officer "cannot at his discretion sport away the vested rights of others."
- *A court that orders a cabinet officer to act intrudes "into the cabinet" and intermeddles "with the prerogatives of the executive."* The Court disclaims any such jurisdiction as "[a]n extravagance, so absurd and excessive," but this case concerns "a paper, which, according to law, is upon record," and an officer's office cannot exempt him from a remedy that would lie against anyone else.
- *Article III's original-jurisdiction clause contains no negative words, so Congress may add cases to the Court's original jurisdiction so long as they are within the judicial power.* (argued "at the bar"). On that reading the distribution is "mere surplusage" and "form without substance"; "[a]ffirmative words are often, in their operation, negative of other objects than those affirmed," and no clause is presumed "to be without effect."
- *Mandamus to an officer can be an exercise of appellate jurisdiction.* "It is the essential criterion of appellate jurisdiction, that it revises and corrects the proceedings in a cause already instituted, and does not create that cause."
- *Even a statute that exceeds Congress's power binds the courts, which must "close their eyes on the constitution, and see only the law."* That would give the legislature "a practical and real omnipotence, with the same breath which professes to restrict their powers within narrow limits," and reduce to nothing "the greatest improvement on political institutions—a written constitution."

## Context

The casebook calls Marbury "the first time that the entire Court wrote an opinion holding an act of Congress unconstitutional," but the idea was not new: [[Brutus No. 11]] and [[The Federalist No. 78]] both assumed it, the Justices had already refused to give advisory opinions ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]) and objected to the statute in [[Hayburn's Case]], and [[Hylton v. United States]] upheld a federal tax on the merits without anyone questioning review. Marshall's argument is Hamilton's, with text added.

The case is inseparable from its politics: the election of 1800, the lame-duck Judiciary Act of 1801, the Jeffersonian Repeal Act, the cancellation of the Court's 1802 Term, and impeachment proceedings against Federalist judges ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]], Class 15). The confrontation everyone expected came one week later in [[Stuart v. Laird]], where the Court upheld the Repeal Act. McConnell's verdict: "Marbury must be understood as the product of a defeated and demoralized Court."

Marbury holds that courts may refuse to apply an unconstitutional statute in a case before them. Whether that makes the Court's interpretation binding on the other branches is Class 13: the departmentalism of Jefferson, Jackson and [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates|Lincoln]] against the judicial supremacy of [[Cooper v. Aaron]] (1958), which read Marbury as establishing that the federal judiciary is supreme in expounding the Constitution. The Court did not again hold an act of Congress unconstitutional until [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] (1857, Class 12).

The first half of the opinion is a separation-of-powers holding about the executive. Its line between discretionary "political" acts and ministerial duties owed to individuals is the root of the political question doctrine ([[A Map of Article III]]) and of the later mandamus cases. Its premise that an officer with a fixed term is not "removable at the will of the executive" connects to [[The Decision of 1789]], [[Myers v. United States]] and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]]. Its insistence that high office does not exempt an officer from legal process returns in [[United States v. Nixon]] and [[Trump v. United States]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Original action in the Supreme Court. Marbury's counsel, former Attorney General Charles Lee, moved on December 16, 1801, for a rule requiring Secretary of State Madison to show why a writ of mandamus should not issue compelling delivery of Marbury's commission.
- Because Congress abolished the Court's 1802 session, the case was not decided until 1803. The Court discharged the rule for want of jurisdiction.

### Facts

Jefferson defeated Adams in 1800. Before leaving office the lame-duck Federalists passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating new circuit judgeships that Adams filled, and the Organic Act for the District of Columbia, creating more than forty justices of the peace with five-year terms. Adams also appointed his Secretary of State, John Marshall, Chief Justice; Marshall kept both offices for almost a month. On March 3, 1801, the Senate confirmed the justices of the peace, Adams signed their commissions, and Marshall sealed them, but some, including William Marbury's, were not delivered; Marshall thought delivery "legally inconsequential." Jefferson directed Madison not to deliver them. Marbury sued. Meanwhile the Jeffersonian Congress repealed the 1801 Act, abolished the new circuit courts, restored circuit riding and cancelled the Court's 1802 Term, while the more important challenge, [[Stuart v. Laird]], was pending.

### Issue

- Does Marbury have a right to the commission he demands?
- If the right has been violated, do the laws afford him a remedy?
- Is mandamus the proper remedy, and may this Court issue it in its original jurisdiction under § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789?
- If § 13 is inconsistent with Article III, must the Court nonetheless give it effect?

### Holding

- Yes. The appointment was complete when Adams signed the commission and the seal was affixed; the office's five-year term, independent of the executive, gave Marbury a vested legal right.
- Yes. Delivering the commission is a ministerial duty on which Marbury's rights depend, not a discretionary political act, so the withholding is judicially examinable and the laws afford a remedy.
- Mandamus is the proper remedy in kind, but this Court cannot issue it: the writ here would be an exercise of original jurisdiction, and Article III limits the Court's original jurisdiction to cases affecting ambassadors, ministers and consuls and cases in which a State is a party. Section 13, read to confer that jurisdiction, is unconstitutional.
- No. A statute repugnant to the Constitution is void, and a court deciding a case must apply the Constitution rather than the statute. The rule is discharged.

### Rule

- A presidential appointment to an office not removable at will is complete when the President signs the commission; delivery is not required, and the appointment cannot then be revoked.
- Where a specific duty is assigned by law and individual rights depend on it, the officer is amenable to the courts; where the officer exercises the President's constitutional or legal discretion, the act is "only politically examinable."
- Congress cannot add to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond the cases listed in Article III, § 2, cl. 2.
- "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." When a statute and the Constitution both apply to a case and conflict, the court must follow the Constitution.

### Reasoning

**(Marshall, C.J., for the Court)**

- **Order of questions.** Right, then remedy, then whether the remedy can issue "from this court." The order lets the Court declare the executive's conduct unlawful before holding that it has no power to correct it.
- **Right.** Appointing (Art. II, § 2) and commissioning (Art. II, § 3) are distinct acts in "two separate and distinct sections of the constitution." The President's last act is the signature. "Some point of time must be taken when the power of the executive over an officer, not removable at his will, must cease." After signature the Secretary's duties are fixed "by law, and not to be guided by the will of the President"; sealing and recording are "a ministerial act."
- **Remedy.** "The government of the United States has been emphatically termed a government of laws, and not of men." A loss to an office "of trust, of honor or of profit" is not damnum absque injuria. The test is "the nature of that act": political acts that "respect the nation, not individual rights" are for the President and his agents, and "the decision of the executive is conclusive"; but "[t]he question whether a right has vested or not, is, on its nature, judicial."
- **Mandamus.** Blackstone's definition fits; the writ must be directed to someone to whom it may issue and the applicant must lack another remedy. "The province of the court is, solely, to decide on the rights of individuals." "Questions, in their nature political, or which are, by the constitution and laws, submitted to the executive, can never be made in this court."
- **Section 13.** The Secretary of State "is precisely within the letter of the description" of § 13; if the Court cannot issue the writ, "it must be because the law is unconstitutional."
- **Article III.** If Congress could redistribute original and appellate jurisdiction at will, the clause would be "mere surplusage" and "form without substance." "[T]he plain import of the words seems to be, that in one class of cases its jurisdiction is original, and not appellate; in the other it is appellate, and not original."
- **Judicial review: the structural argument.** The people's "original right" is exercised rarely and establishes permanent, fundamental principles. "The powers of the legislature are defined, and limited; and that those limits may not be mistaken, or forgotten, the constitution is written." "The constitution is either a superior, paramount law, unchangeable by ordinary means, or it is on a level with ordinary legislative acts." "Between these alternatives there is no middle ground." Otherwise written constitutions are "absurd attempts, on the part of the people, to limit a power, in its own nature illimitable."
- **Judicial review: the judicial-duty argument.** "Those who apply the rule to particular cases, must of necessity expound and interpret that rule. If two laws conflict with each other, the courts must decide on the operation of each." Choosing between the Constitution and a conflicting statute in a case "is of the very essence of judicial duty."
- **Judicial review: the textual arguments.** (1) The judicial power extends to cases "arising under the constitution"; a case arising under the Constitution cannot be decided "without examining the instrument under which it arises." (2) The export-tax, bill-of-attainder and treason clauses; the two-witness rule is "addressed especially to the courts." (3) The judicial oath: "How immoral to impose it on them, if they were to be used as the instruments, and the knowing instruments, for violating what they swear to support!" The statutory oath to act "agreeably to the constitution" would be "worse than solemn mockery." (4) The Supremacy Clause lists the Constitution first and gives supreme rank only to laws "made in pursuance of the constitution."

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. Unanimous opinion by Marshall, C.J.

### Cold-call notes

- **What are the three questions, and why that order?** Right, remedy, jurisdiction. Ordinarily a court asks about jurisdiction first. Note 2's charge: Marshall provoked Jefferson in the first half ("violative of a vested legal right") and then declined to issue any order Jefferson could defy. The best defense of the order: § 13 authorizes mandamus only "in cases warranted by the principles and usages of law," so whether the writ was otherwise proper (right, remedy, nature of the writ) arguably had to be settled before asking whether § 13 conferred the power. The best criticism: if the Court lacked jurisdiction, everything before the jurisdictional holding is dicta.
- **What is the holding and what is dicta?** Note 2. Narrowly: the Court lacks original jurisdiction to issue mandamus to the Secretary of State, § 13 is unconstitutional to the extent it confers it, and courts may not apply unconstitutional statutes. The right and remedy holdings are necessary only if Marshall's order of analysis was necessary.
- **Was the constitutional question necessary? (Note 3).** Section 13's mandamus language sits in a sentence about appellate jurisdiction and follows a grant of "writs of prohibition to the district courts, when proceeding as courts of admiralty and maritime jurisdiction." Read that way, § 13 makes mandamus a remedy the Court may use where it already has jurisdiction; it confers no original jurisdiction. On that reading Marbury loses on the statute, there is no conflict with Article III, and there is no occasion for judicial review. Marshall's reading was chosen, not compelled.
- **Is Marshall right about Article III? (Note 4).** The alternative reading: the original-jurisdiction list is a floor, guaranteeing those cases a Supreme Court forum Congress cannot remove, not a ceiling. That reading answers Marshall's surplusage argument (the clause still does work), and the Exceptions Clause suggests Congress may adjust the allocation. Marshall's strongest point is the pairing: "in one class of cases its jurisdiction is original, and not appellate; in the other it is appellate, and not original." His holding that Congress cannot enlarge original jurisdiction remains the law.
- **Vary the forum.** Had Marbury sued in a lower federal court and come up on appeal, the Court would have had appellate jurisdiction, no constitutional problem, and a direct order to Madison to issue, which Jefferson would likely have ignored. The choice of original jurisdiction is what let Marshall avoid the confrontation.
- **Vary the office.** Had the justice of the peace served at the President's pleasure, Jefferson could simply have removed Marbury; "the circumstance which completes his appointment is of no concern." The five-year term is what makes the right vested. Compare the removal cases: [[Myers v. United States]], [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]], [[Trump v. Slaughter]].
- **Vary the act.** Had Adams not yet signed, or had the dispute concerned nomination itself, the act would be political and discretionary, "only politically examinable." The line between ministerial and discretionary duties is the source of the political question doctrine and returns in [[Adams v. Richardson]] and [[United States v. Cox]] on judicial control of executive discretion.
- **The textual bases, ranked.** The arising-under clause (courts must read the Constitution to decide such cases); specific clauses addressed to courts (the treason rule); the judicial oath (Art. VI, cl. 3, and the statutory oath); and the Supremacy Clause's "in pursuance" language. Weaknesses to have ready: the oath binds legislators and executive officers too, so it proves every branch must follow its own reading (departmentalism); and the Supremacy Clause expressly binds state judges, so it is a stronger text for review of state law than of federal statutes.
- **Brutus or Hamilton? (Note 1).** Marshall's structure is Hamilton's: a limited, written Constitution; the people's will above the legislature's; interpretation as the province of courts; conflict of laws. Marshall's claim is tied to deciding "particular cases," which is Hamilton's modest review, not the finality Brutus feared. Marshall adds text Hamilton did not cite.
- **Constitutional supremacy or judicial supremacy? (Note 5).** Major premise: the Constitution prevails over contrary government action. Minor premise: an independent branch cannot be bound by another branch's constitutional views. The logic applies to the President (Marbury's first half is itself review of executive action), to a President's refusal to enforce a statute he thinks unconstitutional ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]), and, harder, to a President who thinks a judicial decision unconstitutional. Marshall's own conclusion says "courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument": the Constitution binds all, which is the departmentalist reading. That is Class 13: [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]], [[Ex parte Merryman]], [[Cooper v. Aaron]].
- **What about the states? (Note 5e).** The same logic would let state officials resist federal acts they think unconstitutional, as Virginia and Kentucky did against the Alien and Sedition Acts. The Supremacy Clause answers state courts (they are bound by federal law) but the premise of independent interpretation makes the Resolutions harder to dismiss.
- **The conflict of interest.** Marshall, as Secretary of State, sealed the very commissions and failed to deliver them, then decided the case as Chief Justice. Be ready to say whether he should have sat.
- **Defeat dressed as victory.** Marshall asserts the power of judicial review in a case where exercising it meant declining power Congress had given the Court. The exercise was self-denying, so no other branch had anything to resist. One week later, in [[Stuart v. Laird]], the Court declined to confront the Repeal Act. McConnell: "Marbury must be understood as the product of a defeated and demoralized Court."
- **Memorable lines.** "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." "The government of the United States has been emphatically termed a government of laws, and not of men." "The very essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws, whenever he receives an injury." "Between these alternatives there is no middle ground." "The rule must be discharged."
