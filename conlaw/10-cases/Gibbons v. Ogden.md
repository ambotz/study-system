## Rule

Congress's commerce power reaches navigation and all commercial intercourse that "concerns more States than one," the power is "complete in itself" and checked chiefly by politics, and a state law that collides with a valid federal commerce statute must yield under the Supremacy Clause even if the state enacted it under its own police power.

## Facts

- **New York granted Robert Fulton and Robert Livingston an exclusive right to run steamboats in New York waters, and Ogden ran a ferry under their license.** *The grant's stated purpose, encouraging investment in steamboat technology, did not save it: once the New York law collided with an act of Congress, "it will be immaterial" whether New York acted under a commerce power or under its power over "domestic trade and police."*
- **Gibbons ran a competing ferry between New York City and Elizabethtown, New Jersey, so his boats necessarily entered New York waters.** *A route wholly inside New York would have been the "completely internal commerce of a State," which Marshall reserves to the state.*
- **Gibbons's vessels were enrolled and licensed for the coasting trade under the federal act of February 18, 1793.** *The federal license created the conflict; without the license, the case would have turned on whether the commerce power by itself excludes state regulation, a question Marshall touches only in dictum.*
- **Gibbons's business was navigation, not the buying and selling of goods.** *Ogden's counsel argued that commerce means only traffic; Marshall held that Americans have always understood "commerce" "to comprehend navigation."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Marshall, C.J., for the Court)

- **Construction.** The enumerated powers are not construed strictly; the framers "must be understood to have employed words in their natural sense, and to have intended what they have said."
- **"Commerce."** "Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic, but it is something more: it is intercourse." Commerce includes navigation.
- **"Among the several States."** "Among" means "intermingled with," so the power does not stop at state lines and "may be introduced into the interior."
- **The internal limit.** The power is restricted to "that commerce which concerns more States than one"; "[t]he enumeration presupposes something not enumerated," namely "the exclusively internal commerce of a State."
- **"Regulate."** The power is "to prescribe the rule by which commerce is to be governed," and the power "is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations, other than are prescribed in the constitution."
- **The check on abuse.** "The wisdom and the discretion of Congress, their identity with the people, and the influence which their constituents possess at elections" are "the sole restraints" the people relied on.
- **Supremacy.** The New York monopoly conflicts with the federal coasting licenses and is invalid under the Supremacy Clause; a state law, "though enacted in the exercise of powers not controverted, must yield to it." The injunction against Gibbons was dissolved.

**Rejected**

- *The enumerated powers were surrendered by sovereign states and should be construed strictly, to protect what the states kept.* No sentence in the Constitution "prescribes this rule," and Marshall will not adopt a "narrow construction, which would cripple the government."
- *"Commerce" means traffic — buying, selling and exchanging commodities — and so does not include navigation.* Congress has regulated navigation "from the commencement of the government," "with the consent of all," and "[a]ll America understands" commerce to include navigation.
- *The states keep a concurrent power over interstate commerce, just as they keep a concurrent power to tax, and the Tenth Amendment secures that power.* Two governments can tax at once for different purposes, but a state regulating interstate commerce "is doing the very thing which Congress is authorized to do." Marshall does not rest the decision on this answer; the casebook's Note 4 calls the answer dictum.
- *State inspection laws prove that the states regulate commerce.* Inspection, quarantine and health laws come from the "immense mass of legislation" the states never surrendered, not from a commerce power.
- *A valid state law and a valid federal law in conflict meet "like equal opposing powers."* The framers "foresaw this state of things" and made federal law supreme.

## Context

Gibbons follows [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] by five years and copies its structure: first the scope of the federal power, then whether a conflicting state law survives. Gibbons opens the commerce unit, and every later case in the unit reads it. [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] read "prescribe the rule" as a power to govern commerce, not to forbid it; [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] quoted "complete in itself" in overruling Hammer; [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]] said Marshall described the power "with a breadth never yet exceeded" and left restraints to come "from political rather than from judicial processes"; [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] turned "[t]he enumeration presupposes something not enumerated" into a judicially enforced limit. Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]] cites Gibbons for the point that the channels and instrumentalities categories are "the ingredients of interstate commerce itself." See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]] and, on the conflict holding, [[Supremacy and the oath - Art. VI]].

Marshall's dictum that a state regulating interstate commerce does "the very thing which Congress is authorized to do" is the seed of the dormant Commerce Clause ([[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]]). The Marshall and Taney Courts did not press the dictum: *Willson v. Black-Bird Creek Marsh Co.* (1829) and *Cooley v. Board of Wardens* (1851) upheld state regulation where Congress had not acted. Marshall's list of reserved "[i]nspection laws, quarantine laws, health laws of every description" is the baseline [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] starts from, and [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]] cited Gibbons to reject a commerce-power objection to a state law.
