## What it is

The eleventh essay of "Brutus" (probably Melancton Smith), a leading New York Anti-Federalist, published January 31, 1788, during the ratification fight. It is the first sustained critique of the proposed federal judiciary, and the casebook pairs it with [[The Federalist No. 78]], Hamilton's reply, as the founding-era debate over judicial review. Brutus saw in Article III "the seeds of despotism and judicial oligarchy" (the casebook's description).

## What to notice

- **The judges' position is "altogether unprecedented in a free country."** They are "to be rendered totally independent, both of the people and the legislature, both with respect to their offices and salaries." "No errors they may commit can be corrected by any power above them, if any such power there be, nor can they be removed from office for making ever so many erroneous adjudications." The complaint is about accountability, not about judicial review as such.
- **The real effect of the government will be felt through the courts.** The system is "a complete system, not only for making, but for executing laws," and the people will feel it "through the medium of the judicial power." The courts, with their own officers, execute their own decisions.
- **An anti-surplusage reading of "arising under this constitution."** "The cases arising under the constitution must be different from those arising under the laws, or else the two clauses mean exactly the same thing." So the clause "vests the judicial with a power to resolve all questions that may arise on any case on the construction of the constitution." Marshall makes the same kind of argument twice in [[Marbury v. Madison]]: the arising-under clause means judges must look into the Constitution, and the original-jurisdiction clause cannot be "mere surplusage."
- **Brutus describes ordinary legal interpretation, then predicts its abandonment.** The courts are to give the words "the common, and generally received acceptation," read dubious words by context, and attend to "[t]he end of the clause." But in practice "they will not confine themselves to any fixed or established rules, but will determine, according to what appears to them, the reason and spirit of the constitution." The fear is not that courts will interpret the Constitution but that they will interpret it loosely.
- **Brutus assumes judicial finality.** "The opinions of the supreme court, whatever they may be, will have the force of law; because there is no power provided in the constitution, that can correct their errors, or controul their adjudications. From this court there is no appeal." The legislature cannot set aside a judgment, because "[t]he legislature must be controuled by the constitution, and not the constitution by them." That last sentence is Marbury's major premise, stated by the critic. Brutus predicted what Class 13 calls judicial supremacy; Hamilton defended something narrower.
- **The federalism fear.** The judicial power will effect, "in the most certain, but yet silent and imperceptible manner," "an entire subversion of the legislative, executive and judicial powers of the individual states." Every decision on the extent of federal power fixes "the limits of the state jurisdiction." This is the fear [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] would test.
- **The self-interest argument.** "Every body of men invested with office are tenacious of power." Judges' "dignity and importance" grow with the extent of federal power, and so will their "emolument"; they will therefore "construe the constitution as much as possible, in such a way as to favour it." Brutus applies Madison's Federalist 51 logic of ambition to the one branch Federalist 78 says has no ambition to feed.
- **How strong is "any power above them"?** Weigh it against the checks the casebook lists in [[A Map of Article III]]: appointment, Congress's control of the lower courts and of the Supreme Court's size and appellate jurisdiction, the budget, Article V, and impeachment (Hamilton's "complete security" in The Federalist No. 81). Brutus is right that Congress cannot reverse a judgment in a decided case. He is wrong if he means Congress has no lever at all: [[Stuart v. Laird]] shows the first Jeffersonian Congress abolishing judgeships and reshaping the courts within fifteen years.
- **Was Brutus right?** Marbury and Stuart look like vindications of Hamilton: a weak Court declining to confront the political branches. [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] (Class 12) is the case for Brutus: a Court deciding a national political question on its view of the "reason and spirit" of the Constitution. Be ready to take a side.
