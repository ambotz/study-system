## Rule

An independent counsel is an inferior officer whom a court may appoint, and Congress may protect her against removal except for good cause, because the test is not whether an officer is "purely executive" but whether the removal restriction impedes "the President's ability to perform his constitutional duty."

## Facts

- **The Ethics in Government Act let a special court, the Special Division, appoint an independent counsel on the Attorney General's application to investigate and prosecute certain high-ranking officials.** *Appointment by a court is valid only for an inferior officer, so the Appointments Clause question came first.*
- **The counsel could be removed "only by the personal action of the Attorney General and only for good cause."** *Congress kept no role in removal, which made the case "more analogous to Humphrey's Executor . . . and Wiener . . . than to Myers or Bowsher."*
- **The counsel held "full power and independent authority to exercise all investigative and prosecutorial functions and powers of the Department of Justice."** *The Court conceded the functions are "executive"; for Scalia that concession ended the case.*
- **The counsel's jurisdiction was whatever the Special Division defined on the Attorney General's request, her office ended when her single task was done, and she had to follow Justice Department policy "except where not possible."** *These limits made her inferior. [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] later confined Morrison to "inferior officers with limited duties and no policymaking or administrative authority."*
- **The Attorney General's decision not to seek a counsel was committed to his unreviewable discretion.** *One of the controls the Court counted: the Executive keeps "a degree of control over the power to initiate an investigation."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (7–1; Rehnquist, C.J., for the Court; Scalia, J., dissenting; Kennedy, J., not participating)

- The counsel "clearly falls on the 'inferior officer' side" of the line: a higher Executive Branch official can remove her, she performs "only certain, limited duties," and her office is limited in jurisdiction and in tenure.
- Congress may vest her appointment in a court, because the power to appoint an inferior officer "is not in itself an 'executive' function in the constitutional sense," at least when Congress vests it in the "courts of Law."
- Whether Congress may limit removal "cannot be made to turn on whether or not that official is classified as 'purely executive'"; "the real question is whether the removal restrictions are of such a nature that they impede the President's ability to perform his constitutional duty."
- The good-cause limit passes that test: the President's "need to control the exercise of that discretion" is not "so central to the functioning of the Executive Branch" as to require at-will removal, and good cause leaves the Attorney General "ample authority to assure that the counsel is competently performing his or her statutory responsibilities."
- Taken as a whole, the Act does not violate the separation of powers: Congress kept no control beyond impeachment and oversight, the courts usurp no executive function, and the Executive retains "sufficient control over the independent counsel to ensure that the President is able to perform his constitutionally assigned duties."

**Rejected**

- *[[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] rests on the line between "purely executive" officers and "quasi-legislative" or "quasi-judicial" ones; a prosecutor is purely executive, so [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] governs and the President must be able to remove her at will (appellees).* Rehnquist: the "quasi" labels "in large part reflected our judgment that it was not essential to the President's proper execution of his Article II powers that these agencies be headed up by individuals who were removable at will."
- *Article II vests "all of the executive power" in the President, prosecution is "a quintessentially executive function," and the Act takes exclusive control of it from him, so "[t]he case is over" (Scalia).* Rehnquist (n.29): requiring every holder of executive power to serve at the President's pleasure "depends upon an extrapolation from general constitutional language which we think is more than the text will bear."
- *"Inferior" means subordinate, and a counsel removable only for good cause is subordinate to no one, "not even to the President" (Scalia).* Rehnquist: the line between principal and inferior officers "is one that is far from clear," and the four factors place her on the inferior side without deciding exactly where the line falls.
- *Calling good-cause removal a means of control "is somewhat like referring to shackles as an effective means of locomotion" (Scalia).* Rehnquist: good cause permits removal for "misconduct" and gives the Executive "substantial ability to ensure that the laws are 'faithfully executed.'"

## Context

Morrison moved removal doctrine from categories to a functional balancing test. It follows [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] in the casebook's sequence, and its footnote 28 conceded that the 1935 FTC's powers "would at the present time be considered 'executive'"; Thomas in [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] and the Court in [[Trump v. Slaughter]] later turned that concession against Humphrey's. Scalia's dissent is the formalist answer that eventually carried the Court on Humphrey's, which he said the majority had "swept into the dustbin of repudiated constitutional principles." The statutory counsel succeeded the Special Prosecutor of [[United States v. Nixon]], who was protected only by regulation, and the prosecution-is-executive premise of Scalia's dissent is also the premise of [[United States v. Cox]]. Congress let the Act expire after independent counsel Kenneth Starr's investigation of the Clinton administration.

Later doctrine narrowed Morrison without overruling it. *Edmond v. United States* (1997; Scalia, J.) defined inferior officers as those "whose work is directed and supervised at some level by others who were appointed by Presidential nomination with the advice and consent of the Senate," which leaves two tests: Morrison's test of importance and Edmond's test of supervision and control. Seila Law read Morrison as an exception for inferior officers with limited duties, and [[Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) left that exception untouched. See [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]] and [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].
