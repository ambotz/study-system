## What it is

The casebook's twenty numbered questions on Article V, pp. 804–812 — the substance of Class 26. There is no case and almost no doctrine: the questions run from the Twenty-seventh Amendment outward to time limits, who judges a ratification's validity, rescission, unratified proposals, entrenchment, the Fourteenth Amendment's adoption, amendment outside Article V, and the convention route.

## What to notice

- **Time limits (Questions 1, 2, 3, 7, 11).** Article V says nothing about time. Is a limit implicit? [[Dillon v. Gloss]] (1921) suggests ratification must reflect a "contemporaneous consensus." May Congress put a deadline in the amendment's text (the Eighteenth, Twentieth, Twenty-first and Twenty-second Amendments do), or only in the proposing resolution? May it impose a deadline after proposal, or "recall" a proposal outright? If there is no deadline, does a proposal "live forever, floating above us, waiting to be ratified"?
- **Who decides validity (Questions 3, 4, 5, 6, 15).** Congress voted to accept the Twenty-seventh Amendment — "What if Congress had voted not to accept it?" May Congress judge whether a state legislature followed its own rules? *Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433 (1939), suggests "either that Congress possesses this power or that its acceptance of a state ratification is a 'political question'," without a controlling majority. And the Archivist certified before Congress voted, on OLC advice.
- **The Reconstruction hypothetical, Question 6.** Could Congress declare a state's refusal illegitimate, "install a new state legislature that would be more sympathetic," and withhold representation until it ratified? The casebook's point is blunt: that "is remarkably close to the story of the ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment." Question 15 returns to it with Amar's answer — Congress could conclude those governments were not "republican" under Art. IV § 4, so the question is really about Reconstruction powers, not Article V.
- **Unratified proposals still pending (Question 8).** The 1789 apportionment amendment on the size of the House; the 1810 titles-of-nobility amendment stripping citizenship for accepting a foreign title or "emolument"; the **Corwin Amendment** of 1861, which would have barred any amendment giving Congress power over the states' "domestic institutions . . . including that of persons held to labor or service" — i.e. slavery — and "[h]ad it been ratified at the time, it would have become the Thirteenth Amendment"; and the 1920s Child Labor Amendment, aimed at [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] and [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co|Bailey]], which later interpretation made unnecessary.
- **Entrenchment and "unconstitutional" amendments (Questions 9, 10).** May an amendment forbid future amendments, as Corwin purported to? If not, what of Article V's own two provisos — the 1808 slave-trade clause and equal Senate suffrage? Could a state be deprived of equal suffrage by first amending the proviso, or by doing both in one amendment? Claims that some amendments cannot be adopted at all were "raised in, and rejected by, *Leser v. Garnett*, 258 U.S. 130 (1922)."
- **Rescission (Questions 12, 13, 14).** After the three-fourths threshold, no: "the scale-tipping ratification locks the amendment in place," or a state could "secede" from part of the Constitution by withdrawal. Before the threshold is harder: Idaho's rescission of the ERA was upheld in *Idaho v. Freeman*, 529 F. Supp. 1107 (D. Idaho 1981), vacated as moot; Ohio and New Jersey rescinded as to the Fourteenth Amendment and appear not to have been counted by the Secretary of State, though Congress wanted them counted. And the mirror question: may a state ratify after rejecting? Several Southern states did, on the Fourteenth.
- **The "forgotten ratification" problem (Question 13).** A claimed 1792 Connecticut ratification of the House-size amendment would have carried it 12 of 15 states — meaning it has been part of the Constitution for over two centuries unnoticed. *LaVergne v. Bryson*, 497 F. App'x 219 (3d Cir. 2012), disposed of a suit on standing and political-question grounds without reaching the merits.
- **Amendment outside Article V (Questions 16, 17).** Ackerman: "constitutional moments" — the Founding, Reconstruction and the New Deal — amend the Constitution without Article V, and the Fourteenth was not properly ratified but is valid anyway. Amar: Article V is not the exclusive route to a new *text*, because "We the People of the United States" could ratify by national referendum, just as the 1787 Convention departed from the Articles' rules. The casebook's rejoinder is the obvious one: "Why go to the bother of listing the prescribed methods of amendment if they are not the prescribed methods of amendment?"
- **The convention route (Question 18).** Never used, and designed "to circumvent Congress if its members were too resistant to constitutional change." Can a convention be limited to named topics? Charles Black read "a Convention for proposing Amendments" as one for "proposing such amendments as that convention decides to propose"; Rappaport argues text and original meaning allow a limited convention. The casebook's follow-up is the harder one: "who would enforce it?"
- **Difficulty and amendment culture (Question 19).** A blocking coalition in one house in thirteen of the smallest states defeats any amendment — Madison's Rhode Island complaint in The Federalist No. 43 in modern form. Ginsburg & Melton argue "amendment culture" matters more than the formal rules.
- **The interpretive payoff (Question 20).** Does a hard-to-amend Constitution justify courts updating it by interpretation, or does Article V's existence show that the document is *not* to be "amended" by interpretation? That is the question to have an answer to, because it connects this class to [[Types of Constitutional Argument]] and to everything in the course.
