## Snapshot

Congress answered [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] by taxing 10 percent of the net profits of any mine or factory employing children below the prescribed ages or hours. A furniture company that employed one under-age boy paid $6,312.79 under protest and won a refund in the district court. The Court held the exaction a penalty rather than a tax, invalid because the power to regulate child labor rests with the states. Article I, § 8, cl. 1 and the Tenth Amendment are in play.

## Issue

- Is the Child Labor Tax Law a valid exercise of Congress's power to lay and collect taxes, or is the 10 percent exaction a penalty enforcing a regulation of child labor?
- If it is a penalty, is there any enumerated power to support it, after [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] denied Congress power to reach child labor through commerce?

## Rule

An exaction labeled a tax is a penalty, and so must be supported by some other enumerated power, when the statute on its face sets out a detailed code of conduct and enforces it with a heavy exaction. The line is drawn from the statute's own terms, not from legislative history or from what Congress called it. A tax does not lose its character merely because Congress also meant to discourage the activity; it loses it when the penalizing features take over.

- An act that "provides a heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business" is a penalty.
- Scienter is "associated with penalties, not with taxes."
- The presumption of validity fails where "the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions."

## Operative facts

- **[[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (1918) held 5–4 that Congress could not exclude child-made goods from interstate commerce, the goods being harmless and production local.** *The absence of a regulatory power is what makes the tax label load-bearing; this case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart."*
- **Within months Congress wrote the same policy into the revenue laws as Title XII of the Revenue Act of 1918, the Child Labor Tax Law.** *An end-run enacted while the ink was dry; the Court treats the two statutes as one.*
- **The statute itself prescribed minimum ages, capped daily and weekly hours for those aged fourteen to sixteen, and fixed the earliest and latest hours a child could work.** *That is "a detailed and specified course of conduct in business," the feature that converts the exaction into a penalty.*
- **The exaction was 10 percent of the employer's entire net profits, owed in the same amount whether he used five hundred children for a year or one child for a day.** *"The amount is not to be proportioned in any degree to the extent or frequency of the departures" — heavy, and untethered from the violation.*
- **The tax was owed only for a knowing departure, with a defense for reasonable mistake about a child's age or reliance on an official age certificate.** *Scienter is "associated with penalties, not with taxes."*
- **Inspection belonged to the Secretary of Labor as well as the Commissioner of Internal Revenue.** *An enforcing agency "whose normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers" is another tell.*
- **Drexel Furniture employed one boy under fourteen in its North Carolina factory during 1919 and was assessed $6,312.79 on the whole year's profits of the business.** *One child, a year's profits: the disproportion in miniature.*
- **It paid under protest, was refused a refund, and sued Bailey, the collector of internal revenue; the district court held the law unconstitutional (276 Fed. 452) and the Government appealed.** *A refund suit puts the question up with no standing or remedy problem in the way.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (8–1; Taft, C.J., for the Court; Clarke, J., dissenting without opinion)

- **A penalty, not a tax.** The act's own provisions prove its character, and the judgment for Drexel Furniture is affirmed.
- **No power supports it.** Because the exaction is a penalty it must rest on a power to regulate child labor, and [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] holds there is none.

**Taft, C.J., for the Court**

- The statute reads as a regulatory code: "It provides a heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business."
- Disproportion, scienter and the enforcing agency are the tells; inspection by officers "whose normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers" is not revenue work.
- Purpose is read from the text, not from legislative history: "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children within the age limits prescribed."
- The presumption cannot save it: the Court has "gone far to sustain taxing acts as such," but here "the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions."
- The line is one of degree. Taxes laid with "the incidental motive of discouraging" an activity "do not lose their character as taxes," but "there comes a time in the extension of the penalizing features" when the exaction "becomes a mere penalty."
- Why classification matters: "Where the sovereign enacting the law has power to impose both tax and penalty, the difference between revenue production and mere regulation may be immaterial, but not so when one sovereign can impose a tax only, and the power of regulation rests in another."
- The slippery slope is the heart of the opinion. Congress could take over any reserved subject by enacting "a detailed measure of complete regulation" and enforcing it "by a so called tax upon departures from it"; such "magic to the word 'tax'" would "wipe out the sovereignty of the states."

**Clarke, J., dissenting without opinion**

- He had dissented in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] too, the only member to take Congress's side in both. His position, which Taft never answers: McCray held that courts may not police the purposes behind a lawful power, and this statute does nothing a tax cannot do.

## Arguments rejected

- *This is an excise on the profits of manufacture, a classic indirect tax laid uniformly, and under McCray the Court has no business asking why Congress laid it.* Taft relocates the inquiry from motive to the statute's text: where the regulatory design "is found on the very face of its provisions," the presumption of validity has nothing to work on.
- *Veazie Bank left the weight of a tax to Congress, and the bank-note tax was heavy enough to extinguish its subject; 10 percent is milder than that.* Taft: in neither Veazie nor McCray did the statute "show on its face . . . the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern." Weight alone is not the test; weight attached to a written code of conduct is.
- *Congress has an independent taxing power, so it needs no regulatory power to support an exaction collected by the Treasury.* Taft: the difference between revenue and regulation is immaterial only where one sovereign holds both powers. Child labor belongs to the states, so calling the exaction a tax is the question, not the answer.

## Where it sits

**The two-case story.** [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (1918) held 5–4 that Congress could not exclude child-made goods from interstate commerce; Congress answered within months and the Court struck the tax 8–1. Congress proposed the Child Labor Amendment in 1924, never ratified; Hammer was overruled in [[United States v. Darby]] (1941).

**The outlier in the taxing survey.** In [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]] the pattern runs the other way — the *License Tax Cases* (1866), *Veazie Bank v. Fenno* (1869), *McCray v. United States* (1904) — and after Bailey the Court tacked back in *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis* (1937) and *United States v. Kahriger* (1953), never reconciled with it.

**NFIB runs the factors backwards.** [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] (2012) asks Bailey's questions — how heavy, is there scienter, who collects — and gets opposite answers. Bailey holds a thing called a tax is a penalty; NFIB holds a thing called a penalty is a tax, and keeps Bailey as the outer limit.

**Pretext.** Bailey acts on Marshall's caution in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] — the survey's only case holding an enumerated power unusable because of its object. [[United States v. Darby]] buried that inquiry for commerce: motive is "for the legislative judgment."

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

- **What exactly are Bailey's factors?** Four: (1) a heavy exaction out of proportion to the violation — a year's net profits for one child for one day; (2) a detailed code of conduct written into the statute; (3) a scienter requirement; (4) enforcement by an agency whose job is not revenue. The Court never says how many are needed, or how heavy is too heavy.
- **Why isn't this just McCray?** McCray refused to ask *why* Congress taxed oleomargarine; Taft says he is reading what Congress wrote. That holds only if a statute's structure is evidence of a different kind from its legislative history — real, since the text is enacted, but thin, because Congress can drop the code and leave it to regulations.
- **Hypo: 10 percent of net profits on every manufacturer, with a credit for any manufacturer certified by a state labor department as complying with state law.** That is Steward Machine's architecture, and it survived: the conduct is no longer prescribed federally and the credit does the work. If that is valid and Bailey is not, the rule is about drafting technique.
- **Hypo: the same tax without the scienter requirement, collected only by the IRS; or a flat $500 licence fee on any factory employing anyone under sixteen.** The first leaves only weight and detail, and under [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] weight alone can still be prohibitory. The second looks like the *License Tax Cases* and is probably valid — so Bailey is about how hard Congress may press, not whether it may press.
- **Why did the vote jump from 5–4 in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart|Hammer]] to 8–1 here?** Institutionally, a tax enacted a year after the Court said no is open defiance. Doctrinally, the Hammer ban operated on goods once in commerce, while the tax operated inside the factory, dictating ages and shifts and putting Labor inspectors on the premises — the more intrusive statute.
- **Federalism, not liberty — and the unit's punch line.** Taft's objection is that child labor is "reserved to [the states] by the Tenth Amendment," the instinct behind [[Printz v. United States]]. The argument fails against a state legislature, which holds both powers. Meanwhile the taxing power reaches what [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] and Hammer put beyond commerce; Bailey forbids only writing the regulation into the revenue statute.
- **Memorable lines.** "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children." Such "magic to the word 'tax'" would "wipe out the sovereignty of the states." "The good sought in unconstitutional legislation is an insidious feature." The numbers: 10 percent of net profits, $6,312.79, 8–1.
