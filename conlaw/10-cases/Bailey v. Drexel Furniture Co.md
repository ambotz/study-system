## Rule

An exaction labeled a tax is a penalty, and so must be supported by some other enumerated power, when the statute on its face sets out a detailed code of conduct and enforces it with a heavy exaction: the tax/penalty line is drawn from the statute's own terms, not from legislative history or from what Congress called it.

## Facts

- **The exaction was 10 percent of the employer's entire net profits, owed in the same amount whether he used five hundred children for a year or one child for a day.** *"The amount is not to be proportioned in any degree to the extent or frequency of the departures" — heavy, and untethered from the violation.*
- **The tax was owed only for a knowing departure, with a defense for reasonable mistake or reliance on a government age certificate.** *Scienter is "associated with penalties, not with taxes."*
- **The statute itself prescribed minimum ages, a cap on daily and weekly hours, and the earliest and latest hours a child could work.** *That is "a detailed and specified course of conduct in business," which is what converts the exaction into a penalty.*
- **The Secretary of Labor could inspect, alongside the Commissioner of Internal Revenue.** *An enforcing agency "whose normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers" is another tell.*
- **Congress enacted the tax after [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] denied it power to bar child-made goods from interstate commerce.** *Decisive: the case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (8–1; Taft, C.J., for the Court; Clarke, J., dissenting without opinion)

- The Child Labor Tax Law is unconstitutional, and the judgment of the District Court for Drexel Furniture, which had paid $6,312.79 under protest and sued the collector for a refund, is affirmed.
- The act's own provisions prove its character: "It provides a heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business."
- Motive may be read from the text: "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children within the age limits prescribed. Its prohibitory and regulatory effect and purpose are palpable. All others can see and understand this. How can we properly shut our minds to it?"
- The usual presumption cannot save the act: "Out of a proper respect for the acts of a co-ordinate branch of the government, this court has gone far to sustain taxing acts as such, even though there has been ground for suspecting, from the weight of the tax, it was intended to destroy its subject. But in the act before us the presumption of validity cannot prevail, because the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions."
- The federalism stake: "Grant the validity of this law, and all that Congress would need to do, hereafter, in seeking to take over to its control any one of the great number of subjects of public interest, jurisdiction of which the states have never parted with, and which are reserved to them by the Tenth Amendment, would be to enact a detailed measure of complete regulation of the subject and enforce it by a so called tax upon departures from it. To give such magic to the word 'tax' would be to break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states."
- The line is one of degree, not of motive alone: "Taxes are occasionally imposed in the discretion of the Legislature on proper subjects with the primary motive of obtaining revenue from them and with the incidental motive of discouraging them by making their continuance onerous. They do not lose their character as taxes because of the incidental motive. But there comes a time in the extension of the penalizing features of the so-called tax when it loses its character as such and becomes a mere penalty, with the characteristics of regulation and punishment. Such is the case in the law before us."
- Why the classification matters: "Where the sovereign enacting the law has power to impose both tax and penalty, the difference between revenue production and mere regulation may be immaterial, but not so when one sovereign can impose a tax only, and the power of regulation rests in another."
- Because the exaction is a penalty, it must rest on a power to regulate child labor, and [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] holds there is none.

**Rejected**

- *This is an excise on the profits of manufacture, a classic indirect tax laid uniformly across the United States, and under [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] the Court has no business asking why Congress laid it; a lawful power does not become unlawful because of the object the legislature hoped to reach.* Taft does not abandon McCray's rule against hunting for motive; he relocates the inquiry to the statute's text. Where the regulatory design "is found on the very face of its provisions," no inquiry into motive is needed, so the presumption of validity has nothing to work on.
- *[[Veazie Bank v. Fenno|Veazie Bank]] settled that the weight of a tax is for Congress, and the bank-note tax was heavy enough to extinguish its subject; a 10 percent levy is milder than that.* Taft distinguishes both Veazie and McCray on the ground that in neither did the statute "show on its face . . . the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern and business with a heavy exaction to promote the efficacy of such regulation." Weight alone is not the test; weight attached to a written code of conduct is.
- *Even if the act regulates, Congress has an independent taxing power, so it does not need a regulatory power to support an exaction collected by the Treasury.* Taft: the independence of the taxing power matters only where the same sovereign holds both powers. Here the regulation of child labor belongs to the states, so calling the exaction a tax is precisely the question, not the answer.

## Context

Bailey is the second half of a two-case story that begins in Class 18. [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] (1918) held 5–4 that Congress could not exclude child-made goods from interstate commerce, because the goods were harmless and production is local. Congress responded within months with the Child Labor Tax Law, Title XII of the Revenue Act of 1919, and the Court struck it down 8–1. Three Justices who had dissented in Hammer, and so thought Congress could reach child labor through commerce, nonetheless thought it could not reach it through taxation; only Justice Clarke dissented in both. The Court's answer to the end-run was to say that the two statutes were the same statute in different clothes. Congress then tried Article V, proposing the Child Labor Amendment in 1924; it was never ratified. The problem was finally solved by overruling Hammer in [[United States v. Darby]] (1941), which also upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act's child-labor provisions.

Placed in the survey of [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]], Bailey is the outlier. The [[License Tax Cases]] (1866) allowed Congress to tax intrastate businesses it admittedly could not regulate; [[Veazie Bank v. Fenno]] (1869) left the weight of a tax to Congress; [[McCray v. United States]] (1904) refused to examine motive. After Bailey the Court tacked back: [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis]] (1937) upheld the unemployment tax-and-credit scheme designed to push states into legislating, distinguishing the Child Labor Tax Case, and [[United States v. Kahriger]] (1953) upheld the bookmakers' tax because "regardless of its regulatory effect, the wagering tax produces revenue." Bailey has never been overruled, and Kahriger has never been reconciled with it in any way that supplies a usable line.

The modern law runs through [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] (2012), which takes Bailey's three factors and turns each one over. Roberts asks the same questions — how heavy is the exaction, is there a scienter requirement, who collects it — and gets the opposite answers: the shared responsibility payment can never exceed the price of insurance, requires no state of mind, and is "collected solely by the IRS through the normal means of taxation." So Bailey holds that a thing called a tax is really a penalty; NFIB holds that a thing called a penalty is really a tax. Roberts also keeps Bailey as the outer limit, quoting through [[Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch|Kurth Ranch]] that a tax may become "a mere penalty with the characteristics of regulation and punishment."

The structural point runs deeper than the tax clause. Bailey is the only place in the federal-powers unit where the Court holds that an enumerated power cannot be used because of what it is being used *for*. That is Marshall's pretext caution in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] — a law whose real object is an end not entrusted to the government is not "necessary and proper" — applied to a power Marshall never discussed in those terms. [[United States v. Darby]] rejected the pretext inquiry for the commerce power in so many words: motive is "for the legislative judgment." Bailey is what survives of it, and it survives mainly as a limit nobody has enforced since.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Drexel Furniture Company employed a boy under fourteen in its North Carolina factory during 1919 and was assessed $6,312.79 under the Child Labor Tax Law. It paid under protest, was refused a refund, and sued Bailey, the United States collector of internal revenue, to recover the money.
- The District Court for the Western District of North Carolina held the law unconstitutional and gave judgment for the company (276 Fed. 452). The United States took the case to the Supreme Court, which heard argument in March 1922 and affirmed on May 15, 1922.

### Facts

After [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] struck down the 1916 child-labor act, Congress wrote the same policy into the revenue laws. The Child Labor Tax Law imposed, on top of all other taxes, an excise of 10 percent of the entire net profits of any mine or quarry employing children under sixteen, and of any mill, cannery, workshop, factory or manufacturing establishment employing children under fourteen, or children between fourteen and sixteen for more than eight hours a day, more than six days a week, after seven in the evening or before six in the morning. Bona fide boys' and girls' canning clubs were exempt, and an employer who made a reasonable mistake about a child's age, or relied on an official age certificate, owed nothing. Enforcement was shared between the Commissioner of Internal Revenue and the Secretary of Labor. Drexel Furniture employed one under-age boy and was assessed a tax on the whole year's profits of the business.

### Issue

- Is the Child Labor Tax Law a valid exercise of Congress's power to lay and collect taxes, or is the exaction a penalty enforcing a regulation of child labor, which lies outside Congress's enumerated powers under [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]]?

### Holding

- A penalty, not a tax, and therefore invalid. The statute's detailed specification of ages, hours and times of work, the disproportion between the 10 percent exaction and the violation, the scienter requirement and enforcement by the Department of Labor show on the face of the act that its object is to stop child labor; because the power to regulate child labor rests with the states, the exaction cannot be sustained as a tax.

### Rule

- An exaction that on its face couples "a heavy exaction" with "a detailed and specified course of conduct in business" is a penalty, whatever Congress calls it.
- A tax does not lose its character because Congress also meant to discourage the activity; it loses its character when "the extension of the penalizing features" passes the point at which it "becomes a mere penalty, with the characteristics of regulation and punishment."
- Scienter is "associated with penalties, not with taxes."
- The tax/penalty distinction matters only where the taxing and regulating powers belong to different sovereigns; in that case calling a regulation a tax cannot enlarge the taxing sovereign's authority.
- The presumption of constitutionality does not apply where "the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of [the statute's] provisions."

### Reasoning

**(Taft, C.J., for the Court)**

- Reads the statute as a regulatory code. The act prescribes three sets of conditions — a minimum age in mines and quarries, a lower minimum age in mills and factories, and hours limits for those aged fourteen to sixteen — and attaches a fixed 10 percent exaction to any departure from them.
- Disproportion. "The amount is not to be proportioned in any degree to the extent or frequency of the departures": the same 10 percent falls on an employer who uses five hundred children for a year and on one who uses one child for a day. No revenue measure behaves this way.
- Scienter. Liability attaches only to a knowing departure, and a good-faith mistake or an official age certificate is a defense. Mental state is the vocabulary of punishment.
- Enforcing agency. Inspection belongs not only to the taxing officers of the Treasury but also to "the Secretary of Labor and his subordinates, whose normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers."
- From these features the purpose is visible without any inquiry into legislative history: "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children within the age limits prescribed."
- Distinguishes [[Veazie Bank v. Fenno|Veazie]] and [[McCray v. United States|McCray]]: in neither did the challenged law "show on its face . . . the detailed specifications of a regulation of a state concern."
- The slippery slope is the heart of the opinion. If this works, Congress can take over any reserved subject by writing a complete regulatory code and enforcing it with "a so called tax upon departures from it," which would "break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states."
- Concedes the general rule before departing from it: taxes imposed with "the incidental motive of discouraging" an activity "do not lose their character as taxes because of the incidental motive." The holding is that this one passed the point where the penalizing features take over.
- Warns against being moved by the object: "The good sought in unconstitutional legislation is an insidious feature," because it leads people of good purpose to overlook "the serious breach it will make in the ark of our covenant."
- Ties the result to the commerce holding: the case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart," so the absence of a regulatory power is settled and the tax label is all that stands between the statute and invalidity.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Justice Clarke dissented without opinion. He had also dissented in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]], and he was the only member of the Court to take Congress's side in both cases.
- The strongest form of his position, which the opinion never answers: [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] held that the judiciary may not police the purposes behind a lawful power, and nothing in the Child Labor Tax Law does anything a tax cannot do — it takes money from people who make profits in a specified way. Reading purpose off the statute's structure instead of its legislative history is the same inquiry conducted with better manners, and it leaves Congress free to enact the identical policy by writing the exaction more crudely.

### Cold-call notes

- **What exactly are Bailey's factors?** Four, and be ready to list them: (1) a heavy exaction out of proportion to the violation — 10 percent of a year's entire net profits for employing one child for one day; (2) a detailed code of prescribed conduct written into the statute; (3) a scienter requirement; (4) enforcement by an agency whose job is not collecting revenue. The Court never says how many are needed or how heavy is too heavy.
- **Why isn't this just [[McCray v. United States|McCray]]?** McCray refused to ask *why* Congress taxed oleomargarine. Taft says he is not asking why; he is reading what Congress wrote. The distinction holds only if a statute's structure is evidence of a different kind from its legislative history. It is a real distinction — the text is enacted and the motives are not — but it is thin, because Congress can get the same result by dropping the code from the statute and leaving it to regulations.
- **Hypo: Congress taxes 10 percent of the net profits of any manufacturer, with a credit for any manufacturer certified by a state labor department as complying with that state's child-labor law.** That is [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]'s architecture, and it survived. The prescribed conduct is no longer in the federal statute, the tax is owed by everyone and the credit is the operative device. If that is valid and Bailey is not, the rule is about drafting technique.
- **Hypo: the same 10 percent tax, but with no scienter requirement and collected only by the IRS.** Two factors drop out and only weight and detail remain. Under [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] weight alone can still be "prohibitory," so the answer probably does not change — but say what the exaction would have to look like to change it: a payment capped at something like the cost of complying, owed regardless of knowledge, collected with the annual return.
- **Hypo: a flat $500 annual license fee on any factory employing anyone under sixteen.** Now it looks like the [[License Tax Cases]]: a modest, revenue-raising levy on an activity Congress cannot regulate, with no code attached. Probably valid — which shows that Bailey is not about whether Congress may discourage child labor through the tax code, only about how hard it may press.
- **How does [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] use this case?** As the test, applied to the opposite conclusion. The shared responsibility payment is never more than the price of insurance, has no scienter requirement, and is collected by the IRS without criminal prosecution; so the "fairly possible" reading is that it is a tax. Be able to run both cases through the same three questions and get opposite answers, and to say that the label went the other way each time: Bailey's statute said "tax" and the Court said penalty; NFIB's statute said "penalty" and the Court said tax. The consistent principle is that the label never controls the constitutional question.
- **Why did the vote jump from 5–4 in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart|Hammer]] to 8–1 here?** Two accounts. The institutional one: a tax enacted four years after the Court had said no is an open defiance, and the Court closed the door on its own authority. The doctrinal one: the shipping ban in Hammer operated on goods once they entered commerce, while the tax operated inside the factory, dictating ages, hours and shifts and putting Labor Department inspectors on the premises. On that view the tax was the more intrusive statute, and Justices who would let Congress close the channels of commerce still would not let it run the mill.
- **Was the Court policing pretext?** Yes, and that is the deeper point. [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] warns that if Congress passes a law "for the accomplishment of objects not entrusted to the government" under the pretext of executing an enumerated power, it becomes "the painful duty of this tribunal" to say so. Bailey is the rare case that acts on the warning. [[United States v. Darby]] then buries the inquiry for the commerce power — "the motive and purpose of a regulation of interstate commerce are matters for the legislative judgment." Ask why pretext should be reviewable for taxes but not for commerce.
- **Is the tax/penalty line administrable? (Casebook Note 4)** The honest answer is that Bailey states factors, not a threshold, and that [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] upheld a tax whose "intent to curtail and hinder" was "manifest" and whose revenue was "negligible." If the surviving criterion is simply whether the exaction produces revenue, Bailey's law would have failed it too — the whole point was that nobody would pay. The distinction that can be defended is procedural: Kahriger's tax had no code of conduct in it, and no Labor Department.
- **Does the taxing power let Congress reach what [[Hammer v. Dagenhart|Hammer]] and [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]] put out of reach?** Mostly yes, and that is the unit's punch line. The [[License Tax Cases]] and [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] say the taxing power reaches activity the commerce power cannot touch. Bailey is the only limit, and the only thing it actually forbids is writing the regulation into the revenue statute. The comfort Roberts offers in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] — that a tax can only make you pay money, never command you — is the real constraint, and it is a modest one.
- **Federalism, not liberty.** Taft's objection is not that the tax burdens the employer; it is that child labor is a subject "reserved to [the states] by the Tenth Amendment." The same instinct animates [[Printz v. United States]] and Roberts's Medicaid holding in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]. Notice that the argument would fail entirely against a state legislature, which holds both the tax power and the police power — which is exactly what Taft says: the distinction matters "not . . . when one sovereign can impose a tax only, and the power of regulation rests in another."
- **Memorable lines.** "[A] court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children." "To give such magic to the word 'tax' would be to break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states." Scienter is "associated with penalties, not with taxes." "The good sought in unconstitutional legislation is an insidious feature." And the numbers: 10 percent of entire net profits, $6,312.79, 8–1, one dissent by Clarke.
