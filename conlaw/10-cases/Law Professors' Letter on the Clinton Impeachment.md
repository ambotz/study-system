## What it is

A letter of November 6, 1998, signed by some 430 law professors and addressed to the House leadership and the Judiciary Committee's chair (Hyde) and ranking member (Conyers), arguing that the misconduct alleged in Independent Counsel Starr's referral did not amount to "high Crimes and Misdemeanors." The charges were that President Clinton lied under oath about a sexual relationship, in his deposition in the Paula Jones suit and before the grand jury, and obstructed justice to conceal it. The House impeached him on perjury and obstruction anyway, and the Senate acquitted.

The casebook prints it after [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson|the Johnson impeachment]] as the modern statement of the narrow view. Johnson asked whether policy disagreement is impeachable; the letter asks whether a crime is, when it involves no use of presidential power. Henry Hyde's floor statement answering it begins at the foot of p. 358 and runs on in the packet past the assigned pages.

## What to notice

- **The thesis: crime is neither necessary nor sufficient.** The letter rejects both camps in the 1867 Johnson committee. Perjury and obstruction are serious crimes and still not impeachable here; "[n]on-indictable conduct might rise to this level," for example a President who "as a result of drunkenness . . . recklessly and repeatedly misused executive authority." The test is the kind of misconduct, not whether it is criminal.
- **The method: treason and bribery as the paradigms.** "[T]he proper interpretation of the Impeachment Clause must begin by recognizing treason and bribery as core or paradigmatic instances, from which the meaning of 'other high Crimes and Misdemeanors' is to be extrapolated." That is ejusdem generis, argued by contrast:
    - The clause does not read "Treason, Felony, or other Crime," the phrase of Article IV's extradition clause, so not every crime counts.
    - Nor "Arson, Larceny, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors," so not every serious crime counts.
    - Nor "Adultery, Fornication, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors," so not every moral lapse counts.
- **The common feature: "grossly derelict exercise of official power."** Treason uses executive powers "deliberately to aid an enemy"; bribery exercises them "in exchange for corrupt gain." Both convert "those awful powers into an instrument either of enemy interests or of purely personal gain." Bribery to obtain or keep office is the one variant that turns on "gross criminality in the pursuit of official power" rather than its exercise.
- **Applied to Clinton.**
    - Perjury about his sexual conduct and concealing evidence "involved no exercise of presidential power as such."
    - Seeking a Pentagon job for someone, or lying to subordinates hoping they would repeat it, "could have involved a wrongful use of presidential influence," but not "grossly derelict" use.
    - Some charges "strain good sense": declining to testify voluntarily, or pressing "a debatable privilege claim that was later judicially rejected." Such "litigation 'offenses' are not remotely impeachable" — a direct line to [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|Nixon]], where losing a privilege claim was the ordinary course of law.
- **Where perjury and obstruction would count.** "A President who corruptly used the Federal Bureau of Investigation to obstruct an investigation would have criminally exercised his presidential powers" — Watergate, in all but name. So would perjury "to cover up his subordinates' criminal exercise of executive authority," because covering up a crime aids it.
- **The private-crime exception.** A "private" crime could be "so heinous as to warrant impeachment": "an individual who by the law of the land cannot be permitted to remain at large, need not be permitted to remain President." Murder is the example. "Short of heinous criminality, impeachment demands convincing evidence of grossly derelict exercise of official authority."
- **The structural argument: a low threshold weakens the President.** "The lower the threshold for impeachment, the weaker the President. If the President could be removed for any conduct of which Congress disapproved, this fundamental element of our democracy—the President's independence from Congress—would be destroyed." Independence matters most for the veto and under divided government. This is Madison's objection to "maladministration" (tenure "during pleasure of the Senate") and Trumbull's warning in 1868, restated.
- **The prudential argument: discretion.** Even if the House had power to impeach for any perjury, "a responsible House would not exercise this awesome power on the facts alleged." "The House's power to impeach, like a prosecutor's power to indict, is discretionary," to be used "not for partisan advantage" but only when circumstances justify "the enormous price the nation will pay." The letter cites public opposition to impeachment as "political wisdom."
- **Who interprets.** "No existing judicial precedents bind Congress's determination of the meaning of 'high Crimes and Misdemeanors.'" The letter is addressed to Congress as the Constitution's interpreter on this question, and says members "would violate their constitutional responsibilities" by impeaching below the standard. Impeachment is the clearest case of non-judicial constitutional interpretation in the course.
- **The answer in the packet (beyond the assignment).** Hyde: perjury "is a public act, not a private act," and "[t]he matter before the House is a question of the willful, premeditated, deliberate corruption of the nation's system of justice." His frame is the rule of law — "[n]o man or woman, no matter how highly placed . . . can be above the law" — and the President's Take Care duty. The Senate acquitted 55–45 on perjury and 50–50 on obstruction.

## Cold-call notes

- **Is lying under oath impeachable?** The letter says not when it concerns private conduct and involves no use of office. Hyde says yes because the chief law-enforcement officer who perjures himself corrupts the system he must "take Care" to execute. The strongest reply to Hyde: on his reasoning every crime by a President is a Take Care violation, which collapses the letter's distinction and its ejusdem generis argument with it.
- **Does the letter's test fit the text?** Its paradigm is official misuse. But the Impeachment Judgment Clause leaves the convicted party liable to "Indictment, Trial, Judgment and Punishment," which assumes impeachable conduct is often criminal, and the 1867 committee dissent read "[c]rimes" and "[m]isdemeanors" as "terms of art." The letter's answer is that crime may accompany an impeachable act without defining it.
- **What is the precedent of the Clinton acquittal?** At most that private perjury and obstruction do not get two thirds. Compare Johnson: Currie found the 1868 precedent "equivocal" on the words and clear on the result. Each acquittal settles a result, not a test.
- **Pair it with [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]].** Read together, the two make a rough division:
    - The letter's core of impeachable conduct is grossly derelict *official* acts, and Trump makes official acts presumptively, and core ones absolutely, immune from prosecution. So impeachment becomes the only remedy for exactly the category the letter thinks impeachable.
    - The letter's non-impeachable category is private crime, which Trump leaves fully prosecutable.
    - The overlap and the hard case is bribery: a corrupt use of an official act, an enumerated impeachable offense, and the subject of the Trump majority's footnote 3 and Barrett's "quid and the quo."
- **Clinton v. Jones as the backstory.** The perjury charge came from Clinton's deposition in the Paula Jones suit, which proceeded only because *Clinton v. Jones* (1997) denied a sitting President temporary immunity from civil suits over unofficial conduct. The case that refused immunity produced the impeachment.
- **Who should decide?** The professors argue as constitutional interpreters to Congress, not to a court. Is a letter of 430 law professors a legitimate input into that interpretation, or an interest group's? Trumbull's and Fessenden's votes are the Senate's own version of the same argument.
