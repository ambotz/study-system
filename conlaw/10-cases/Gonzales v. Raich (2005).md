## Snapshot

Federal agents destroyed six cannabis plants a patient grew for her own medical use under California law. She and another patient sued to stop the Controlled Substances Act from reaching them, won in the Ninth Circuit, and lost here. The Court held that Congress may ban local cultivation and possession of marijuana, because the conduct belongs to an economic class of activities whose aggregate market effect Congress could rationally fear. The commerce power and the Necessary and Proper Clause are in play.

## Issue

- Does the commerce power, with the Necessary and Proper Clause, authorize Congress to prohibit local cultivation and possession of marijuana for personal medical use under state law?
- May the class be drawn as narrowly as respondents draw it — intrastate, noncommercial, state-authorized medical use?
- **Argued, not decided.** Do substantive due process or medical necessity bar enforcement?

## Rule

Congress may regulate purely local, noncommercial activity that is part of an economic "class of activities" when it rationally concludes that leaving the class unregulated would undercut a comprehensive scheme regulating an interstate market in a fungible commodity. The narrower subclass a litigant carves out does not escape if Congress rationally declined to exempt it.

- "Congress can regulate purely intrastate activity that is not itself 'commercial' . . . if it concludes that failure to regulate that class of activity would undercut the regulation of the interstate market in that commodity" ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]]).
- The question is only whether "a 'rational basis' exists," not whether effects are shown in fact.
- State law cannot shrink the commerce power, which "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or non-exercise of state power" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]]).
- Noneconomic local activity may be reached where the regulation "is a necessary part of a more general regulation of interstate commerce" (Scalia).

## Operative facts

- **In 1970 Congress consolidated federal drug law into the Controlled Substances Act, a "closed regulatory system" barring manufacture, distribution and possession of any controlled substance outside its terms, with marijuana in Schedule I.** *The comprehensive scheme separates this case from Lopez's freestanding § 922(q): there is a larger regulation to protect.*
- **Marijuana is a fungible commodity with an established, albeit illegal, interstate market, and Congress was concerned above all to prevent diversion into illicit channels.** *Homegrown supply is divertible and indistinguishable from trafficked supply, so removing the market defeats the Wickard analogy.*
- **In 1996 California voters passed Proposition 215, the Compassionate Use Act, exempting from state prosecution patients and caregivers who cultivate or possess marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation.** *Held irrelevant under the Supremacy Clause: state action "cannot circumscribe Congress' plenary commerce power," though for the dissenters it marks the medical–recreational line.*
- **Angel Raich and Diane Monson, Californians with serious medical conditions, grew or obtained marijuana solely for their own use; they never bought or sold it, and it never crossed a state line.** *Thomas's dissent rests on these facts, which the majority treats as beside the point once the class is broad.*
- **On August 15, 2002, county deputies and DEA agents came to Monson's home; the county officials found her use lawful under state law, but after a three-hour standoff the federal agents destroyed all six of her plants.** *Two sovereigns reached opposite conclusions about the same six plants, and the seizure gives Monson a concrete injury.*
- **Respondents sued the Attorney General and the head of the DEA for declaratory and injunctive relief barring enforcement of the CSA against their personal cultivation and use.** *They sued to stop enforcement rather than defend a prosecution, so no criminal judgment stands in the way.*
- **Respondents conceded that enacting the CSA was within the commerce power and that no provision is invalid on its face, challenging only its application to them.** *The case therefore turns on how narrowly the "class of activities" may be drawn.*
- **The Ninth Circuit held the CSA unconstitutional as applied and ordered a preliminary injunction.** *A lower court had accepted the narrow class, which is why the Court must decide who draws it.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (6–3; Stevens for the Court; Scalia concurring in the judgment; O'Connor dissenting, joined by Rehnquist, C.J., and Thomas as to all but Part III; Thomas dissenting separately)

- **Yes.** Congress may "regulate purely local activities that are part of an economic 'class of activities' that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce."
- **The narrow class fails.** Congress rationally concluded that exempting homegrown marijuana "would leave a gaping hole in the CSA"; the subclass "was an essential part of the larger regulatory scheme."
- **Due process and medical necessity left open.** The judgment below, which ordered a preliminary injunction, is vacated.

**Stevens, for the Court**

- "[T]he similarities between this case and Wickard are striking": home cultivation of a fungible commodity, a statute controlling a national market, and the risk that rising prices draw home supply into that market.
- Lopez and *Morrison* involved noneconomic activity, whereas the CSA "regulates the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities for which there is an established, and lucrative, interstate market."
- The task is "a modest one," and relief lies in reclassification and in "the democratic process."

**Scalia, concurring in the judgment**

- The third Lopez category is "misleading and incomplete": activities that substantially affect commerce "are not themselves part of interstate commerce," so the power to reach them is a Necessary and Proper power.
- That derivative power is bounded: it "can only be exercised in conjunction with congressional regulation of an interstate market," and reaches only what is "necessary to make the interstate regulation effective."
- [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s limits are "not merely hortatory," and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] shows a law violating state sovereignty is not "proper." Still, marijuana grown at home "is never more than an instant from the interstate market."

**O'Connor, dissenting (joined by Rehnquist, C.J., and Thomas as to all but Part III)**

- Immunizing local regulation by "packaging regulation of local activity in broader schemes" leaves Lopez "nothing more than a drafting guide."
- The Court's definition of economic activity is "breathtaking," the class should be fixed by "objective markers," and the Government never showed that homegrown patients affect the national market.

**Thomas, dissenting**

- This marijuana "has never been bought or sold, that has never crossed state lines, and that has had no demonstrable effect on the national market"; if Congress can reach it, "it can regulate virtually anything."
- Commerce is "selling, buying, and bartering, as well as transporting for these purposes," and state-authorized patients are "a distinct and separable subclass" lacking any "obvious, simple, and direct relation" to trafficking.

## Arguments rejected

- *Wickard is distinguishable: the Act exempted small farms, Filburn ran a commercial farm, and the record there proved the market effect.* Stevens: the exemption did not limit the Secretary's power; Filburn's home-consumption wheat "was not treated . . . as part of his commercial farming operation"; and congressional findings do the work the Wickard stipulation did.
- *Lopez and Morrison control, because respondents' conduct is noneconomic and local.* Stevens: "the activities regulated by the CSA are quintessentially economic," since economics is "the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities."
- *The relevant class is intrastate, noncommercial medical use on a physician's advice in compliance with state law, "different in kind from drug trafficking."* The narrower class might justify a policy exemption, but Congress rationally chose to include it; if medical use lay outside, recreational home use would too.
- *State authorization takes the conduct out of federal reach.* The Supremacy Clause answers it; under Darby the commerce power "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or non-exercise of state power."

## Where it sits

**The limit of [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]].** Lopez left open that noneconomic conduct can be reached as "an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity," and Raich walks through that door. The Lopez majority splits, with Kennedy joining Stevens and only Rehnquist, O'Connor and Thomas in dissent. *United States v. Morrison* (2000, not assigned) extended Lopez to gender-motivated violence despite congressional findings, but once the activity is production of a traded commodity inside a comprehensive scheme, Lopez offers little.

**The Necessary and Proper route.** Scalia relocates the substantial-effects category to the Necessary and Proper Clause and reads it through [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]], under which means must be "plainly adapted" and consistent "with the letter and spirit of the constitution"; a law violating state sovereignty, as in [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] (Class 20), is not "proper." Thomas uses the same text for the opposite result. The route returns in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] (Class 23), where a mandate to buy insurance is defended as a means to the ACA's reforms.

**Thomas, after the casebook.** Writing in 2021 on the denial of certiorari in *Standing Akimbo, LLC v. United States*, he observed that federal tolerance of state marijuana markets had undermined the premise of the "gaping hole" reasoning.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Is Raich a Commerce Clause case or a Necessary and Proper Clause case? Does it matter?"**

- Both: Stevens cites the two clauses together, while Scalia insists the third Lopez category always was a Necessary and Proper power.
- It matters, because the derivative route has its own limits: it must attach to a real interstate regulation, reach only what makes it effective, and satisfy "proper."

**Note 2. "Is Raich easy or hard? Is it an easy case because the Necessary and Proper Clause allows Congress to make regulations that are appropriate for its regulations of interstate commerce? Or . . . if this can happen, there is no limit? Or is it a harder case, because the Court's precedents . . . give one answer, while the framers' attempt to strike a balance . . . points to another?"**

- Easy on precedent. Wickard upheld a ban on home-consumed wheat, and an illegal market is harder to police than a licensed one.
- Hard if the framers' balance is the measure, because nothing in the enumeration suggests a federal police power over six plants.

**Note 3. "If the majority is right, can Congress legislate on anything, no matter how local, as long as its scheme of regulation is very broad? Are some federal statutes too big to fail? But if the dissenters are right, does every federal statute become a patchwork . . . ? Is it possible to have . . . federal regulation that is limited and uniform?"**

- Scalia answers the first half: the power exists only alongside regulation of an interstate market, and only for measures necessary to make it effective.
- The patchwork worry cuts against the dissents: the in-state farmer and the hobbyist pilot in the notes both have the same as-applied claim.

**Note 4. "Should it be of any relevance that medical marijuana was allowed—and indeed, regulated and controlled—by state law? . . . How, exactly, would the dissents make state law relevant?"**

- Stevens says no, on the Supremacy Clause and Darby. But that clause answers a narrower question: state law cannot defeat a valid statute, which differs from being irrelevant to its validity as applied.
- O'Connor makes state law evidentiary, an objective line between medical and recreational use; Thomas makes it functional, since identification cards and affirmative defenses keep the interstate ban enforceable.

### Further drilling

- **Why Raich and not Lopez?** Growing and consuming a commodity is economic, carrying a gun is not, and the CSA regulates a whole market that § 922(q) did not.
- **Remove the scheme, or remove the market.** A freestanding ban on homegrown medical marijuana closes the Scalia route; a homemade good with no interstate market defeats the Wickard analogy.
- **Draw the class.** Stevens takes all intrastate marijuana, O'Connor takes personal medical use by objective markers, and Thomas takes state-authorized patients as a separable subclass.
- **Memorable lines.** Six plants seized after a three-hour standoff. Stevens: "a gaping hole in the CSA." Scalia: "never more than an instant from the interstate market."
