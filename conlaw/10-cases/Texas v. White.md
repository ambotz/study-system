The casebook cites the case as 1868 (the December 1868 Term); it was decided April 12, 1869, and is usually cited as 1869. On p. 776 the 4th-ed. text reads "a discretion in the choice of means is necessary allowed," which looks like a typo for "necessarily allowed"; check the 5th ed.

## Rule

A State cannot leave the Union by its own act: its ordinance of secession and every act giving the ordinance effect are "absolutely null," so the State and its citizens keep their obligations and the State keeps its title to its property. But rebellion suspends the State's rights as a member, and restoring its relations to the Union is an exercise of the Guarantee Clause power, which "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress."

## Facts

- **The United States issued bonds in 1851, payable after 1864, to the State of Texas or bearer.** *Bearer form is why the case turns on the State's title rather than on the mechanics of transfer.*
- **A Texas convention adopted an ordinance of secession, a majority of citizens ratified it, and the legislature passed acts to give it effect.** *If those acts had legal force, Texas was not a State of the Union and could not sue in the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction.*
- **The insurgent state government contracted the bonds away to White and others during the rebellion.** *Whether title passed turns entirely on whether the insurgent legislature's acts were valid.*
- **Emancipation had changed who "the people" of Texas were.** *Chase uses this to explain why the pre-war government could not simply resume, which is what forces the Guarantee Clause analysis.*
- **After surrender a government created under presidential direction, and later treated by Congress as provisional, took office in Texas.** *It is this government that sued in the State's name, which forces Chase to decide whose power Reconstruction is.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (5–3; Chase, C.J.)

- **The Union is perpetual, and it was perpetual before the Constitution.** It "began among the Colonies," received "definite form, and character, and sanction from the Articles of Confederation," by which "the Union was solemnly declared to 'be perpetual.'" and the Constitution was then ordained "to form a more perfect Union." "What can be indissoluble if a perpetual Union, made more perfect, is not?"
- **Perpetuity does not swallow the States.** Powers not delegated are reserved; "the people of each State compose a State, having its own government, and endowed with all the functions essential to separate and independent existence"; and "without the States in union, there could be no such political body as the United States." Hence the line the case is known for: "The Constitution, in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States."
- **Admission is not a revocable compact.** "The act which consummated her admission into the Union was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final." The only exits are "through revolution, or through consent of the States."
- **The secession ordinance and the acts implementing it "were absolutely null. They were utterly without operation in law."** Texas therefore "continued to be a State, and a State of the Union," and her citizens remained citizens of the United States.
- **Nullity is not the same as no change.** "Obligations often remained unimpaired, while relations are greatly changed." During the rebellion "the rights of the State as a member, and of her people as citizens of the Union, were suspended," and the State's government and citizens "assumed the character of enemies, and incurred the consequences of rebellion."
- **Two duties followed, from two different powers.** Suppressing the rebellion rested on "the power to suppress insurrection and carry on war"; re-establishing the State's relations rested on "the obligation of the United States to guarantee to every State in the Union a republican form of government," which "seems to be a necessary complement to the former."
- **Restoration is primarily Congress's job.** While the war continued the President "might institute temporary government within insurgent districts, occupied by the National forces," under his commander-in-chief functions; but "the power to carry into effect the clause of guaranty is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," so "[t]he action of the President must, therefore, be considered as provisional," and Congress so regarded it.
- **The means are tested as in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].** "[A] discretion in the choice of means is necessary allowed. It is essential only that the means must be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the power conferred."
- **Disposition.** "[T]he title of the State [to the bonds] was not divested by the act of the insurgent government in entering into this contract."

**Rejected**

- *Texas's acts accomplished what they set out to accomplish: the relations of Texas to the Union were in fact broken up and new relations to a new government established, so Texas had ceased to be a State of the Union and cannot invoke the original jurisdiction.* Chase accepts the factual premise in full — "In all respects, so far as the object [of secession] could be accomplished by ordinances of the convention, by acts of the legislature, and by votes of the citizens, the relations of Texas to the Union were broken up" — and answers that the acts had no legal operation whatever. The consequence of the contrary view is his real argument: "the State must have become foreign, and her citizens foreigners. The war must have ceased to be a war for the suppression of rebellion, and must have become a war for conquest and subjugation."
- *An indissoluble Union is incompatible with State sovereignty and self-government.* "[T]he perpetuity and indissolubility of the Union, by no means implies the loss of distinct and individual existence, or of the right of self-government by the States." The preservation of the States is "as much within the design and care of the Constitution as the preservation of the Union."
- *If Texas never left, then nothing about her membership changed, and the acts of her government during the war bind her like any other state legislation.* "No one has been bold enough to contend that... senators chosen by her legislature, or representatives elected by her citizens, were entitled to seats in Congress."
- *Congress's own Reconstruction Acts treat the Southern governments as outside the Union, which concedes that the States had left.* The Acts "themselves show that the governments, which had been established and had been in actual operation under executive direction, were recognized by Congress as provisional, as existing, and as capable of continuance."

## Context

Texas v. White is the Supreme Court's ratification of [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln's]] theory of the Union, eight years late and after the question had been settled by arms. The three steps of Lincoln's historical argument — the Articles' "perpetual" Union, the Preamble's "more perfect Union," and the conclusion that a more perfect union cannot have lost perpetuity — reappear in Chase's opinion almost unchanged, and both are steps [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] had already taken in December 1860 while drawing the opposite conclusion about remedy. Against [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]], the opinion's decisive sentence is that admission "was something more than a compact": if the Union is not a compact, there is nothing for a sovereign State to revoke.

On [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell's]] map the opinion is nationalist, but of a careful kind. "[I]ndestructible States" is a concession to the States that the same Court used elsewhere, and the sentence about "the people of each State" borrows [[McCulloch v. Maryland|Marshall's]] move of locating the constituent power in the people rather than in the State governments. The modern anti-commandeering cases quote the indestructible-States half: [[Printz v. United States]] and the dissenters in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] both rely on the idea that the States' separate existence is constitutionally protected.

The Guarantee Clause holding is the case's other legacy. [[Luther v. Borden]] (1849) had held that enforcement of the clause belongs to the political branches; Chase turns that into an affirmative grant to Congress and uses it to supply a constitutional basis for Reconstruction. The consequence was the collision over who directs Reconstruction, which produced the Reconstruction Acts and [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]], and it is the same Court's answer in [[Ex parte McCardle]] that shows how little appetite it had to test Congress on the subject.

The bond holding started a long line on which acts of a rebel state survive. [[Horn v. Lockhart]] (1873) drew the line the opinion implies but does not state: acts of a Confederate state government are valid except so far as they were in furtherance of the rebellion or impaired the rights of loyal citizens. That is not the rule Chase announced here, and the gap between "absolutely null" and the workable rule is the case's weakest seam.

The Notes add three Article IV puzzles. Texas's 1845 admission permitted its division into as many as five States, so the "indestructible" State may be destructible by its own terms (Note 1, citing Kesavan & Paulsen, *Let's Mess with Texas*). Article IV, § 3 provides for admission but not withdrawal, which invites an *expressio unius* argument (Note 2). And [[Reference re Secession of Quebec]] (1998) reached a different answer for Canada: a clear referendum majority would oblige the federation to negotiate terms of separation (Note 3).

## Class layer

### Posture

- Original action in the Supreme Court brought in the name of the State of Texas by its Reconstruction government, to recover United States bonds sold by the insurgent state government during the rebellion and to restrain their payment to the purchasers.
- Jurisdiction was itself the contested question: the Court has original jurisdiction over suits by a State, so the defendants' answer that Texas was not a State of the Union went to the Court's power to hear the case, and the merits of the secession question had to be decided to decide jurisdiction.

### Facts

The United States issued bonds in 1851, payable after 1864, to the State of Texas or bearer, as part of the settlement of Texas's boundary claims. In 1861 a Texas convention adopted an ordinance of secession, a majority of the State's citizens ratified it, and the legislature passed acts to carry it into effect, breaking off Texas's relations with the Union and establishing new relations with the Confederacy. During the war the insurgent government contracted the bonds away to White and others. After the surrender the President appointed a provisional governor and called a convention; the state constitution was amended, elections were held, and a government acknowledging its obligations to the Union took office, which Congress afterwards treated as provisional under the Reconstruction Acts. That government sued in the name of the State to get the bonds back.

### Issue

- Did Texas cease to be a State of the Union, for purposes of the Court's original jurisdiction, by reason of the ordinance of secession and the acts giving it effect?
- Did the acts of the insurgent state government divest the State's title to the bonds?
- From what constitutional power does the authority to restore a rebel State's relations to the Union derive, and in which branch does that power reside?

### Holding

- No. The ordinance and the implementing acts were absolutely null and without operation in law, so Texas remained a State of the Union and could sue; her rights as a member were suspended during the rebellion, but her existence and her citizens' citizenship were not.
- No. Because the insurgent legislature's acts were null, the contract transferring the bonds did not divest the State's title, and the State recovers.
- From the Guarantee Clause. The President could institute provisional governments in occupied districts under his commander-in-chief functions while the war lasted, but the power to carry the guarantee into effect is primarily legislative and resides in Congress, so the executive-created government was provisional only.

### Rule

- The Union is perpetual and indissoluble, and the Constitution "looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States."
- Admission to the Union "was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body," and is final except "through revolution, or through consent of the States."
- Acts of a State purporting to sever its relation to the Union, and acts giving them effect, are "absolutely null" and "utterly without operation in law."
- Rebellion suspends a State's rights as a member of the Union and its citizens' rights as citizens, without destroying the State or the citizenship.
- Suppressing rebellion rests on the war and insurrection powers; restoring a State's constitutional relations rests on the Guarantee Clause, and that power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress."

### Reasoning

**(Chase, C.J., for the Court)**

- He declines to argue the secession question at length — "It is needless to discuss, at length, the question whether the right of a State to withdraw from the Union for any cause, regarded by herself as sufficient, is consistent with the Constitution" — and reasons instead from the history and nature of the Union.
- History: the Union began among the Colonies and grew out of "common origin, mutual sympathies, kindred principles, similar interests, and geographical relations," was declared perpetual by the Articles, and was made "more perfect" by the Constitution.
- Structure, in both directions: the same Constitution that makes the Union indissoluble reserves the States' powers and depends on their existence, so indestructibility runs both ways.
- Consequence: treating secession as effective would convert a war for the suppression of rebellion into "a war for conquest and subjugation" and make Texans foreigners, which nobody was prepared to accept.
- The distinction that does the work at the second step is between obligations and relations. Obligations survived intact; relations changed, so the State's rights of membership were suspended and its government's acts were those of an enemy.
- Reconstruction: emancipation made "[t]he new freemen... part of the people," and "the people still constituted the State; for States, like individuals, retain their identity, though changed to some extent in their constituent elements." Because the old government could not resume and no election could be held under the old constitution, some outside authority had to act, and the Guarantee Clause is the only clause addressed to a State's form of government.
- Allocation: executive action is justified only while the war lasts and only as a war measure, so it is provisional; the guarantee itself is legislative, and Congress's Reconstruction Acts confirm that reading by treating the executive governments as provisional but existing.
- Merits: since the insurgent legislature's acts were null, the contract could not pass title, and the State recovers the bonds.

### Dissent / concurrence

- The casebook prints no separate opinion. It should be read knowing that the decision was divided: Justice Grier dissented from the whole of it, arguing that whether Texas was a State of the Union was a political question already answered by Congress, which had refused her representation, and that the Court should not hold Texas to be a State when Congress had decided she was not. Justices Swayne and Miller dissented separately on the same jurisdictional ground while agreeing that the bonds had been unlawfully sold.
- The dissenting position is the mirror image of Chase's: it takes the suspension of Texas's rights as a member seriously enough to deny her the rights of a State in the Court as well as in Congress, and it treats Reconstruction as a political settlement the judiciary should not second-guess.

### Cold-call notes

- **What was actually at stake?** A suit over bonds. The secession holding is a jurisdictional predicate, which is worth saying out loud: the most famous statement of the nature of the Union is a ruling on whether a party could sue.
- **Why did the Court have to decide the secession question at all?** Because the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction extends to controversies in which a State is a party. If Texas had left the Union she was not a State, and the bill had to be dismissed without reaching the bonds.
- **Where does the "indestructible" formula come from?** Not from the text. Chase builds it from the Articles' word "perpetual," the Preamble's "more perfect," and the inference that perfecting a perpetual union cannot destroy perpetuity — the same three steps as [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln's]] inaugural and [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan's]] message.
- **The remedy problem: which acts of the rebel state survive?** "[A]bsolutely null" cannot mean what it says. If every act of the Texas legislature from 1861 to 1865 were void, no marriage was valid, no deed recorded, no judgment enforceable, no tax lawfully collected. Chase's own rule is narrower than his language: what is null is the ordinance of secession "and all the acts of her legislature intended to give effect to that ordinance." [[Horn v. Lockhart]] (1873) drew the line explicitly — acts of a rebel state government stand except so far as they furthered the rebellion or impaired the rights of loyal citizens.
- **Which side of that line is the bond sale on?** Chase treats it as an act in furtherance of the rebellion, because the insurgent government sold the State's bonds to finance the war. The hard version of the hypothetical is a sale of the same bonds to pay a state judge's salary or to buy schoolbooks; on Chase's stated rule the sale is still void, on *Horn v. Lockhart*'s rule it stands. Be ready to say which rule the opinion actually supports.
- **Vary a pivotal fact: the bonds are bought by an innocent purchaser for value who knew nothing of the rebellion.** The bonds were payable to bearer, so ordinary commercial law would protect him. Chase's nullity theory has no room for a bona fide purchaser, which is the strongest practical objection to it and the reason the later cases softened the rule.
- **Vary a pivotal fact: Texas seceded with the consent of Congress.** Chase reserves exactly this — revocation "through... consent of the States" — so the holding does not decide it. Note 2 gives the two arguments: *expressio unius* (Article IV provides a way in and none out), against the power to undo being incident to the power to do.
- **If secession was legally void, why did Texas need re-admission?** Chase's answer is that she did not need re-admission, only restoration of a lawful government; her statehood never lapsed, only her rights of membership. The awkward fact is the Thirteenth Amendment, which was ratified by "the requisite three-fourths of the States" while the Southern States were unrepresented — Chase says emancipation "was confirmed, rather than ordained" by it, which quietly avoids asking whether those States were counted in the denominator.
- **The same tension in Reconstruction.** If the States never left, Congress had no Territories Clause power over them and the Reconstruction Acts must be Guarantee Clause legislation; if they left, the Territories Clause supplies plenary power. Note 7 of the [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln]] material frames this as the central question of the era. Chase chooses the first and thereby makes the Guarantee Clause the constitutional engine of Reconstruction.
- **Is the Guarantee Clause holding consistent with [[Luther v. Borden]]?** Luther held that which government is the lawful government of a State is a political question for Congress and the President. Chase relies on Luther for Congress's primacy, yet the Court here decides for itself that the Reconstruction government is Texas's lawful government for purposes of the suit. The dissenters' position is that Luther should have controlled all the way down.
- **What does the case do for federalism doctrine?** "[I]ndestructible States" is quoted far more often for the States than against them. Pair it with [[Printz v. United States]] and the dissents in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]: the States' separate existence, not merely their reserved powers, is a constitutional premise.
- **Does the opinion answer [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]]?** Only by assertion. Davis's premise is that the States are sovereign and may reclaim grants made to an agent; Chase's reply is that admission was "something more than a compact." Neither gives a textual argument, and the honest account is that the question was answered by the war and the opinion registers the answer.
- **Memorable lines.** "The Constitution, in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States." "[S]omething more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final." "They were utterly without operation in law." "What can be indissoluble if a perpetual Union, made more perfect, is not?"
