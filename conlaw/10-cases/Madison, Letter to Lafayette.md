## What it is

A November 1826 letter from former President Madison to Lafayette. Madison explains why, after opposing the First Bank's constitutionality in the House in 1791, he signed the 1816 charter of the Second Bank. The casebook sets it just after [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] and its aftermath ("After McCulloch," p. 502), and just before [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson's veto]]. It is paired with Jackson as the opposite answer to one question: must a President accept a constitutional question that practice has settled against his own view?

The "After McCulloch" section records the backlash. Pennsylvania proposed an amendment confining the Bank to the District of Columbia. Ohio's auditor raided a branch and carried off $100,000, which was later returned only "after much legal wrangling and another decision of the U.S. Supreme Court." Bills to strip the Court's jurisdiction over state-court appeals failed. In Virginia, "Amphictyon" and "Hampden" (Spencer Roane) attacked McCulloch, and Marshall answered pseudonymously as "A Friend of the Constitution" and "A Friend of the Union."

## What to notice

- **Madison did not change his mind on the merits.** "My construction of the Constitution on this point is not changed." He signed the bill in spite of his reading, not because he had come around to Hamilton's.
- **What settled the question for him.** He points to "the reiterated sanctions given to the power by the exercise of it, thro' a long period of time, in every variety of form, and in some form or other, under every administration preceding mine, with the general concurrence of the State authorities, and acquiescence of the people at large, and without a glimpse of change in the public opinion, but evidently with a growing confirmation of it." Each clause is a separate condition: repetition, duration, variety, all three branches (the 1815 veto message says "legislative, executive, and judicial"), the States, and the people.
- **The theory is liquidation.** The practice amounted to "a construction put on the Constitution by the Nation, which having made it had the supreme right to declare its meaning." An indeterminate provision is fixed by a deliberate course of practice that the public ratifies. That is Baude's account of "constitutional liquidation," drawn largely from Madison, and it is the professor's own scholarship.
- **The 1815 veto said the same thing six years earlier.** Madison vetoed the 1815 bill on policy grounds, "[w]aiving the question of the constitutional authority of the Legislature to establish an incorporated bank as being precluded in my judgment by repeated recognition under varied circumstances of the validity of such an institution in acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial branches of the Government, accompanied by indications, in different modes, of a concurrence of the general will of the nation" (quoted in the casebook at p. 484).
- **Practice was not the only reason.** He also regarded a bank "under the existing circumstances as the only expedient for substituting a sound currency in place of the viciated one." The letter combines constitutional settlement with necessity, as Marshall's account of the 1816 recharter also does.
- **The role-morality point.** "I did not feel myself, as a public man, at liberty, to sacrifice all these public considerations to my private opinion." Madison separates what he thinks the Constitution means from what he may act on as an officer. Jackson rejects that separation: each officer "swears that he will support it as he understands it."
- **Madison versus Jackson on the same record.** Madison reads 1791–1816 as unbroken acceptance. Jackson counts the votes (1791 for, 1811 against, 1815 against, 1816 for) and finds the precedents "equal," and he adds state opinion running "as 4 to 1" against the Bank. The disagreement is about what counts as settled practice: Madison looks to the long course of institutional acceptance, Jackson to a tally of individual decisions. Jackson's own test, precedent binds only "where the acquiescence of the people and the States can be considered as well settled," is close to Madison's.
- **Is liquidation compatible with Madison's 1791 position?** Yes, if the 1791 argument showed only that the text was doubtful, not that it clearly forbade the Bank. Liquidation fixes only what the text leaves open. Marshall draws the same line in McCulloch: "a bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long the acquiescence.
- **Hypothetical: a President believes a long-standing federal program is unconstitutional, and the program meets every one of Madison's conditions.** On Madison's view the President should sign its reauthorization. On Jackson's view he must veto it. On Lincoln's view in [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]], a constitutional question that is "fully settled" binds the political branches too. Lincoln sides with Madison on settled questions, and with Jackson on unsettled ones.
- **Compare Marshall on practice.** McCulloch gives practice "a considerable impression" but claims not to depend on it. Madison makes practice decisive. Madison's view gives the political branches and the public the last word on meaning, while Marshall's leaves it to the Court ("by this tribunal alone can the decision be made").
