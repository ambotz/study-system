## Rule

Congress may not protect from at-will removal the head of an agency led by a single Director who wields significant executive power, because the exceptions of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] reach only multimember expert bodies without substantial executive power and inferior officers with limited duties.

## Facts

- **The CFPB is led by a single Director, not a board or commission.** *The decisive fact: a single Director "cannot be described as a 'body of experts'" and is not "non-partisan" the way a bipartisan commission is. [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] later made the number of heads irrelevant.*
- **The Director serves a five-year term and may be removed by the President only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office."** *These are the words the Court upheld for the FTC in [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]]; the term, longer than the President's, means some Presidents never appoint a Director.*
- **The Director makes binding rules under 19 statutes, issues final decisions in agency adjudications, and sues in federal court for civil penalties.** *Distinguishes the 1935 FTC, which reported to Congress and recommended dispositions to courts; seeking penalties is "a quintessentially executive power not considered in Humphrey's Executor."*
- **Everyone agreed the Director is a principal officer, not an inferior one.** *So [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] cannot apply: the independent counsel's power was "trained inward to high-ranking Governmental actors," while the Director's reaches "millions of private citizens and businesses."*
- **The CFPB is funded by the Federal Reserve outside the annual appropriations process.** *Aggravating, not necessary: the Director's insulation from removal alone "is enough to render the agency's structure unconstitutional."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (5–4 on the merits; Roberts, C.J., for the Court; Kagan, J., joined by Ginsburg, Breyer and Sotomayor, JJ., dissenting on the merits; Thomas, J., joined by Gorsuch, J., dissenting on severability)

- "[T]he CFPB's leadership by a single individual removable only for inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance violates the separation of powers."
- "The entire 'executive Power' belongs to the President alone," and the President's power over his subordinates "generally includes the ability to remove executive officials."
- [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] are two exceptions: "one for multimember expert agencies that do not wield substantial executive power, and one for inferior officers with limited duties and no policymaking or administrative authority."
- The Court declined to extend either exception to "an independent agency led by a single Director and vested with significant executive power," because "[s]uch an agency has no basis in history and no place in our constitutional structure."
- The constitutional strategy is to "divide power everywhere except for the Presidency, and render the President directly accountable to the people through regular elections."
- The removal restriction is severable (Roberts, C.J., for seven Justices), so the rest of the Dodd-Frank provisions governing the CFPB remain operative without it.

**Rejected**

- *The Constitution says nothing about removal, the Necessary and Proper Clause lets Congress structure the Executive Branch, the Take Care Clause "speaks of duty, not power," and a for-cause standard leaves the President "ample authority" to ensure faithful execution (Kagan).* Roberts: the executive power belongs to the President alone, and "[i]f any power whatsoever is in its nature Executive, it is the power of appointing, overseeing, and controlling those who execute the laws" (Madison).
- *The First Congress was divided, and the only view the [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]] "definitively rejected" was Hamilton's Senate-consent theory (Kagan).* Roberts: the First Congress's recognition of the removal power "provides contemporaneous and weighty evidence of the Constitution's meaning."
- *Humphrey's Executor should be read through the functional gloss that [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] later gave it, and the 1935 FTC may have had broader powers than the Humphrey's Court described (Kagan).* Roberts (n.4): "we take the decision on its own terms, not through gloss added by a later Court in dicta," and "what matters is the set of powers the Court considered as the basis for its decision, not any latent powers that the agency may have had."
- *History supplies precedents for single-headed agencies with for-cause protection (the CFPB's defenders).* Roberts: there are four examples, and apart from a "one-year blip for the Comptroller of the Currency," they are "modern and contested."
- *The FSOC's supermajority power to set aside CFPB rules reduces the Director's insulation.* Roberts (n.9): the veto is a "narrow escape hatch" that "has never been used."

## Context

Seila Law is the casebook's last word on removal. It reads [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]] through the features of the 1935 FTC (five members, partisan balance, staggered terms, no executive power) and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] through the independent counsel's limited duties, and it follows [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] in refusing to extend either exception to a new situation. Thomas, joined by Gorsuch, would "reconsider Humphrey's Executor in toto." Kagan's dissent is the fullest defense of congressional power to create "zones of administrative independence." The case also raises a question the Court did not reach: whether the CFPB's funding from the Federal Reserve squares with the Appropriations Clause (Note 3; see [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]]).

*Collins v. Yellen* (2021) applied Seila Law to another single-director agency. [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] (2026) then dropped the single-head line: once an agency wields executive power the number of heads is irrelevant, and whatever remained of Humphrey's was overruled. Seila Law's footnote 8 began the Federal Reserve reservation that [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] later resolved. Seila Law's challenge came as the target of a civil investigative demand, the pattern [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] contrasts: structural objections are enforced when raised by a party the officer is regulating.
