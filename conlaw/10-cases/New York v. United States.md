## Rule

Congress may not compel the States to enact or administer a federal regulatory program, and offering a State a choice between two things Congress cannot command is still a command.

## Facts

- **The Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Amendments Act of 1985 gave the States three types of incentives to dispose of radioactive waste generated within their borders.** *The first two incentives were upheld as authorized by affirmative grants of power to Congress and "not inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment."*
- **The third incentive, the "take title" provision, required a State either to legislate as Congress directed or to take title to, and possession of, the waste.** *The choice did not save the provision, because Congress could compel neither alternative.*
- **The take-title provision was addressed to the States as governments, not to private waste producers.** *A law regulating private parties, or regulating States alongside private parties, would have been judged under [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] instead.*
- **No other federal statute had offered "a state government no option other than that of implementing legislation enacted by Congress."** *The Court called the provision "unique."*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- "The Federal Government may not compel the States to enact or administer a federal regulatory program."
- "[T]he Constitution simply does not give Congress the authority to require the States to regulate."
- The Framers "explicitly chose a Constitution that confers upon Congress the power to regulate individuals, not States"; federal law acts "directly upon individuals, without employing the States as intermediaries."
- The Tenth Amendment is "essentially a tautology" as a limit on Congress's powers, but the anti-commandeering rule rests on "the province of state sovereignty reserved by the Tenth Amendment" and on the structure of federalism.
- "[F]ederalism secures to citizens the liberties that derive from the diffusion of sovereign power."
- The take-title provision is "inconsistent with the federal structure of our Government established by the Constitution"; the first two incentives stand.

**Rejected**

- *The take-title provision commands nothing, because a State that dislikes regulating as Congress directs is free to take title instead.* Congress had no power to force the States to take title, so the "option" "really amounted to a simple command to the States to enact and enforce a federal regulatory program."
- *The federal interest in waste disposal is weighty and the burden on the States is modest, so a balancing test should sustain the provision.* [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] reports that the Court "expressly rejected such an approach in New York": where the object of a law is to direct how a State governs, no weighing of interests can cure the defect.

## Context

New York is the first half of the anti-commandeering pair. [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] (1997) extended the rule from state legislatures to state executive officers and rested "most conclusively" on New York. Stevens's Printz dissent answered that New York's "administer" language was dictum, "wholly unnecessary to the decision," because taking title was "in principle . . . no different than a congressionally compelled subsidy," a legislative act. Printz Note 2 presses the gap the rule leaves open: under *Testa v. Katt* (1947) Congress may require state courts to hear federal claims. Neither case overruled [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]]; both distinguish laws that regulate States alongside private parties from laws that command States to govern. See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].

The rule's reach runs through the spending and necessary-and-proper cases. [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|South Dakota v. Dole]] lets Congress buy with conditional grants what New York forbids it to command. In [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]], Roberts grounded the new coercion limit on spending conditions in New York ("the Constitution simply does not give Congress the authority to require the States to regulate"), while Ginsburg read New York and Printz as confined to laws compelling state officials. In [[United States v. Comstock]], the respondents invoked New York's "province of state sovereignty" language, and Breyer answered that powers delegated to Congress are "[v]irtually by definition" not reserved. The casebook's Tenth Amendment note (p. 1274) asks which mode of argument does the heavy lifting in New York: the Amendment's text, or the structure of the document as a whole.
