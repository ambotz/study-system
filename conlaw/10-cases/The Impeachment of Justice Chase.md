## What it is

David Currie's account of the 1804–05 impeachment and acquittal of Associate Justice Samuel Chase — the only impeachment of a Supreme Court Justice. Currie is the casebook authors' recurring narrator of early constitutional history, and this is the companion to his treatment of [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] assigned for Class 10. It opens Class 15's three congressional checks on the Court, read with [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] (jurisdiction) and the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] (size). The one doctrinal payoff is large: Chase's acquittal fixed, by practice rather than any court ruling, that a judge is not removed for the content of his rulings.

## What to notice

- **The setting is the Jeffersonian attack on the Federalist bench.** Chase was a Maryland Federalist appointed by Washington in 1796 and "not popular with Republicans." There had long been complaints about his aggressive conduct of sedition and treason trials, his political charges to grand juries, and his absence from the bench to campaign for Adams.
- **The trigger was a political grand jury charge.** In May 1803 Chase told a Baltimore grand jury that the repeal of the Judiciary Act and Maryland's move to "universal" suffrage would "take away all security for property, and personal liberty," and that "[t]he independence of the National Judiciary [was] already shaken to its foundation." Jefferson wrote Representative Nicholson asking whether this "seditious [and] official attack on the principles of our Constitution" should "go unpunished"; Randolph moved for a committee; the House voted 73–32 to impeach, "without much debate."
- **Eight articles, almost all about rulings and trial management, not politics.** They rested on the Baltimore charge plus three episodes in Chase's circuit duties:
  - **Fries** (the treason trial of the leader of an armed anti-tax uprising): Chase delivered a written opinion on the law of treason before argument and kept Fries's lawyers from arguing the question to the jury, said to deny the rights to counsel and jury trial.
  - **Callender** (a Sedition Act libel on President Adams): refusing to excuse a juror who had prejudged guilt, excluding defense evidence, "manifest injustice, partiality, and intemperance," ordering arrest rather than a summons, and trying the case in the same term it was indicted, both against Virginia law.
  - **The New Castle, Delaware grand jury**: refusing to discharge jurors who found no one to indict, pressing them toward a "seditious printer," thus "stoop[ing] to the level of an informer."
  - **The Baltimore charge** (final article): "endeavor[ing] to excite the odium" against the Government, "tending to prostitute the high judicial character... to the low purpose of an electioneering partizan."
- **The central legal question: must an impeachable offense be an indictable crime?** The articles alleged "high crimes and misdemeanors" but accused Chase of no specific criminal offense. Chase's position, from the plain language of Article II: "officers could be impeached only for crimes."
- **The House managers gave two answers, and the weaker one is the one to remember.**
  - *Rodney's theory (reject it):* Caesar Rodney argued Article II "did not limit the occasions for impeachment at all; it merely made removal from office mandatory upon conviction." This is the Blount–Bayard line that everyone is impeachable "as in England," and Senator Giles's gloss that impeachment is "nothing more than an enquiry... whether the office of any public man might not be better filled by another." Currie's refutation is the structural core of the whole unit: removal "at the whim of the Senate" is "wholly incompatible with the separation of legislative and executive powers," and with Article III's good-behavior tenure; the Convention chose "high crimes and misdemeanors" over "maladministration" at Madison's urging precisely because so vague a term "will be equivalent to a tenure during the pleasure of the Senate."
  - *The better argument:* "high crimes and misdemeanors" is a term of art "not restricted to indictable offenses" — never so limited in England, from whose practice it was taken, and paraphrased by Hamilton as "the abuse or violation of some public trust." This view does not require a crime, but it does require a serious abuse of office.
- **Why the acquittal, even on the better theory.** The real question was whether Chase's acts were high crimes "even in this expanded sense," and the two-thirds requirement produced "a resounding 'no.'" Most charges amounted to claims that Chase had *misapplied* the law; but the issue "was not whether he should be reversed... it was whether he was guilty of seriously abusing his position." Currie's line is the keeper: "To elevate a legitimate difference of opinion into a high crime or misdemeanor would obliterate the independence plainly contemplated by the impeachment provisions." Even removal for errors rising to maladministration "would contradict the explicit intentions of the Framers." The only plausible ground for conviction was that Chase had *deliberately* violated the law — and on each count his conduct was shown to be "at least arguably proper."
- **Currie works the counts the way the Senate did.** Fries: the treason definition was already "the law of the case," and Chase told the lawyers the next day they could argue as they pleased. The Callender juror had formed no opinion on the facts and it was "not obvious that he should have been excused." The excluded testimony was of marginal relevance. Whether Virginia law even barred Callender's arrest or same-term trial was "highly debatable." The grand jury charges "look more serious today," but the judge–grand jury relation "was different two centuries ago": Blackstone said it was the judge's task to "instruct" grand jurors, and Harper cited numerous instances of judges flagging particular offenses and commenting on political matters.
- **The best case for the other side.** Rehnquist later said the case against Chase "was not devoid of substance"; Henry Adams, "no fan of the Jeffersonians," thought the charges "serious" and that in Callender "Chase's temper had led him to strain, if not to violate the law"; Raoul Berger argues Chase should have been convicted on Callender, where consistent rulings against the accused, "illuminated by conduct that exhibits the judge's unflagging hostility," show a bent design. Currie's answer: the maxim that it is better to free a hundred guilty than convict one innocent "seems especially compelling in the case of impeachment of a federal judge," and Kurland's warning to "tread warily lest the ultimate cost far outweigh the immediate gains."
- **The aftermath confirms the stakes.** On acquittal, Randolph went back to the House and proposed a constitutional amendment to permit removal of judges on joint address of both Houses — simple majority, for any reason; Nicholson added a proposal to let states recall their senators. Both were "enthusiastically postponed... and disappeared." Had either passed, judicial independence would mean something far weaker.
- **What the acquittal established.** "Not until the attack on Andrew Johnson did the House again attempt to remove a federal officer on essentially political grounds"; Chase's acquittal "has stood ever since for the salutary principle that judges may not be removed simply because the Senate disagrees with their decisions."
- **Constitutional norm or merely political?** The text gives Congress the "sole" power to impeach and try, and *Nixon v. United States* (1993) makes the Senate's procedures nonjusticiable, so no court will enforce the Chase norm. The case that it is constitutional rests on text (Article III "good Behaviour" tenure; "high Crimes and Misdemeanors" read with Article II) and on structure (a judge removable for his rulings is not independent; [[The Federalist No. 78|Federalist No. 78]]). The case that it is political: the standard is enforced only by the Senate's own vote, and the acquittal is a precedent of restraint rather than a construction. The strongest reading treats the acquittal as a settled practice that liquidates the clause — real interpretive weight, enforced only by Congress.
- **Hypos varying the offense.**
  - A Justice delivers a partisan speech from the bench (the Baltimore charge today): the closest call in 1805 and still the strongest article, because it is conduct, not a ruling.
  - A Justice writes an opinion Congress thinks lawless (e.g. [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]): the Chase norm says no — the remedy is amendment, jurisdiction, or appointments.
  - A Justice takes bribes or lies to Congress: impeachable on any view; the indictability question never arises.
  - A Justice sits in a case involving a spouse or benefactor: misconduct in office but not an indictable crime — excluded on Chase's own theory, reached on the managers' better one.
- **Departmentalism from Congress's side.** [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] and [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] claim final interpretive authority for the Court. Impeachment is the one check that would let Congress punish a Justice for disagreeing, and Chase is where Congress declined to use it that way. The remaining congressional levers — jurisdiction ([[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]]) and size ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]) — work on the Court as an institution, not on the individual judge.
