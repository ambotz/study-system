## Rule

An agency's non-enforcement is reviewable when the agency has "consciously and expressly adopted
a general policy which is in effect an abdication of its statutory duty," at least where the
statute directs enforcement and prescribes the means. The prosecutorial-discretion cases do not
cover a wholesale refusal to carry out a specific congressional enforcement mandate.

## Facts

- **Title VI directs enforcement and specifies how.** Each funding agency "is authorized and
  directed to effectuate" § 2000d, by fund termination after a hearing and an express finding "or
  (2) by any other means authorized by law," after first seeking voluntary compliance. *The court
  finds "law to apply" here, which defeats the committed-to-discretion exception. A statute that
  merely authorized enforcement, with no procedures, would look like
  [[Powell v. Katzenbach]], where discretion was upheld.*
- **The challenge was to a general policy, not to individual decisions.** Plaintiffs alleged HEW
  "consciously and expressly adopted a general policy which is in effect an abdication of its
  statutory duty," not mistaken choices about "a few school districts in the course of a generally
  effective enforcement program." *The court introduces this distinction with "More
  significantly." Had HEW been enforcing generally and passing over some districts, the Cox line
  of cases would control.*
- **HEW kept funding segregated institutions.** HEW was "actively supplying segregated
  institutions with federal funds, contrary to the expressed purposes of Congress." *The final
  distinguishing factor. Non-enforcement here was not just inaction but continued payment, which
  Note 3 reads as a power-of-the-purse case.*
- **HEW relied on voluntary compliance with no end point.** HEW argued that while enforcement was
  required, the means were in its "absolute agency discretion," and it had chosen voluntary
  compliance "in most cases." *The proviso requires a voluntary effort first, but a request not
  followed by compliance "within a reasonable time" leaves the duty to use the statutory means.
  A time-limited voluntary program would have been lawful.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (per curiam, D.C. Circuit en banc; injunction affirmed as modified)

- The APA's committed-to-agency-discretion exception is "a narrow one," applicable only where
  there is "no law to apply" ([[Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe|Overton Park]]). Title
  VI supplies law: a body of case law gives criteria for noncompliance and the statute indicates
  "with precision" the enforcement measures.
- Voluntary compliance is a precondition, not a substitute. If it fails within a reasonable time,
  the agency must use one of the two statutory means. "A consistent failure to do so is a
  dereliction of duty reviewable in the courts."
- The District Court's order is affirmed, with a modification to the higher-education provisions.

**Rejected**

- *Enforcement of Title VI, like the prosecution of crimes, is committed to agency discretion, and
  the prosecutorial-discretion cases make that discretion absolute.* Those cases "do not support a
  claim to absolute discretion," and are distinguishable on three grounds: Title VI mandates
  enforcement and sets its procedures; the challenge is to a general policy of abdication rather
  than to particular decisions; and HEW is affirmatively funding the violators.
- *Limited resources make it impossible to act against every violator.* The court accepts the
  premise for a prosecutor ("It is one thing to say the Justice Department lacks the resources
  necessary to locate and prosecute every civil rights violator") and refuses to extend it to
  funding: "it is quite another to say HEW may affirmatively continue to channel federal funds to
  defaulting schools."
- *Even if enforcement is mandatory, the choice of means is absolutely discretionary, and HEW has
  chosen voluntary compliance.* "This position is untenable in light of the plain language of the
  statute," which makes voluntary compliance a preliminary step to one of the two formal means.

## Context

Adams is the second episode and the counterweight to [[United States v. Cox]]. Cox is category two
in Jackson's [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] scheme; Adams is category three:
Congress "authorized and directed" enforcement and prescribed the means, and the agency adopted a
policy of not using them. Cox's Note 5 asks whether Cox's logic survives an express instruction
to enforce. Adams answers no, at least for an agency funding program.

The case was litigated under the APA, not the Constitution. Note 1 draws the constitutional
implication: the D.C. Circuit "at least implicitly" concluded that Article II does not let the
executive ignore a specific congressional enforcement mandate. That is the Take Care Clause read
as a duty, and the English ban on the suspending power is its historical root: a general policy of
not enforcing a statute is what a suspension looks like in practice.

The purse connection runs back to Class 5. Congress controls spending under the Appropriations
Clause; an executive that keeps paying recipients Congress has disqualified is spending contrary
to the conditions of the grant. Compare [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] (spending
without an appropriation) and [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] (a President announcing how he
will treat funding conditions he considers unconstitutional).

[[Heckler v. Chaney]] (1985) set the general rule the other way: an agency's decision not to
enforce is "generally committed to an agency's absolute discretion." But its footnote 4 reserved
Adams by name: a general policy "so extreme as to amount to an abdication of its statutory
responsibilities" might not be "committed to agency discretion." That footnote is how Adams
survives. [[The Thompson Memo]] adopts it as its third limiting principle.

After the casebook, [[United States v. Texas (2023)]] repeated the Heckler footnote. Kavanaugh's
opinion for the Court said "the standing calculus might change if the Executive Branch wholly
abandoned its statutory responsibilities to make arrests or bring prosecutions," quoting the
abdication language, but noted that the states had not made a Heckler-style abdication argument.
The Adams exception is still reserved, not adopted, at the Supreme Court.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Black students, citizens and taxpayers sued the Secretary of HEW (Elliot Richardson) and the
  Director of HEW's Office of Civil Rights for declaratory and injunctive relief. The District
  Court found HEW's Title VI enforcement deficient and ordered seven specific enforcement measures
  against primary and secondary schools and state systems of higher education.
- HEW appealed; the D.C. Circuit, sitting en banc, decided per curiam.

### Facts

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 bars discrimination in programs receiving federal
financial assistance. Section 602 directs each funding agency to effectuate that prohibition by
rules of general applicability, and allows compliance to be secured by terminating or refusing
assistance to a recipient found, on the record after a hearing, to be out of compliance, or by
"any other means authorized by law." The proviso bars either step until the agency has notified
the recipient and "determined that compliance cannot be secured by voluntary means."

The plaintiffs alleged that HEW had not taken appropriate action to end segregation in public
educational institutions receiving federal funds, and had instead adopted a general policy of
relying on voluntary compliance while continuing to fund those institutions. HEW argued that
Title VI enforcement was committed to its discretion and relied almost entirely on cases refusing
to review prosecutorial discretion by the Attorney General and U.S. Attorneys. The editors omit
the court's review of the substance of the injunction, which it affirmed "in all but one minor
respect." The panel list marks Judge MacKinnon with an asterisk whose footnote is not reproduced.

### Issue

- Is HEW's enforcement of Title VI committed to agency discretion, so that the courts
  cannot review an alleged failure to enforce?
- Does Title VI leave HEW free to rely indefinitely on voluntary compliance while continuing to
  fund noncompliant institutions?

### Holding

- No. The exception is narrow; Title VI provides law to apply, and a general policy amounting to
  abdication, together with affirmative funding of violators, distinguishes the prosecutorial
  discretion cases.
- No. Voluntary compliance is a required first step; if it fails within a reasonable time, HEW
  must terminate funds or use other lawful means, and a consistent failure to do so is a
  reviewable dereliction of duty.

### Rule

- Agency action is presumptively reviewable under the APA; the committed-to-discretion exception
  applies only where "there is no law to apply."
- A statute that "authorized and directed" enforcement and prescribes its means supplies law to
  apply.
- A general policy that "is in effect an abdication of its statutory duty" is reviewable, unlike
  individual enforcement choices within a functioning program.

### Reasoning

**(Per curiam)**

- Text: the statute says "authorized and directed," and lays out the enforcement procedures, so
  it is not "drawn in such broad terms that in a given case there is no law to apply."
- Precedent: the prosecutorial-discretion cases turned in part on the absence of a specific
  statute requiring the Attorney General to act ([[Powell v. Katzenbach]]).
- Scope of challenge: plaintiffs ask the court "to interpret the statute and determine whether HEW
  has correctly construed its enforcement obligations," which is a legal question.
- Purse: funding segregated schools is "contrary to the expressed purposes of Congress," and the
  "anomaly" of the government's position supports taking Congress's "clear statement of an
  affirmative enforcement duty" at face value.
- Structure of the statute: the voluntary-compliance proviso exists "to avoid unnecessary
  invocation of formal enforcement procedures," not to replace them.

### Cold-call notes

- **How does Adams distinguish Cox?** Note 2. Three grounds, in the court's order of weight:
  (1) Title VI directs enforcement and sets procedures; (2) the challenge is to a general policy of
  abdication, not to a few decisions in a working program; (3) HEW is paying the violators. The
  court introduces the second with "More significantly." Ask which ground is doing the work by
  removing each in turn.
- **Hypothetical: HEW terminates funds for some districts and negotiates with others.** Now HEW is
  making individual decisions in a generally effective program. Under ground (2) the case moves
  toward Cox and [[Heckler v. Chaney]], even with the mandatory statute. The line is general policy
  versus case-by-case judgment, the same line [[The Thompson Memo]] draws in its fourth principle.
- **Hypothetical: Title VI said only that agencies "may" terminate funds.** Ground (1) disappears;
  the case looks like [[Powell v. Katzenbach]]. But grounds (2) and (3) remain, and the funding
  point would still press: Congress's substantive prohibition in § 2000d does not become optional
  because the remedy is permissive.
- **Is Adams really a purse case?** Note 3. On the funding ground, yes: the executive is spending
  in a way Congress forbade, which is the Appropriations Clause problem in
  [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] turned around. If the reasoning is limited to
  funding, Adams says little about criminal non-enforcement, and the Cox line is untouched. If it
  is not limited, Adams is a general duty to enforce mandatory statutes.
- **Where is Adams in Youngstown?** Category three. Congress "directed" enforcement, so a policy of
  not enforcing is "incompatible with the expressed or implied will of Congress." The executive
  wins in category three only if the power is "conclusive and preclusive," and nobody claims that
  Article II gives the President an exclusive power to fund segregated schools.
- **What is the best argument for an executive power to ignore the mandate?** Note 1. Resource
  constraints and the Take Care Clause read as a grant of judgment, the Heckler reasons. The best
  argument against: the Convention rejected a suspending power, and a policy of not enforcing a
  mandatory statute is a suspension in substance. Say which you find stronger and why.
- **What did the Supreme Court do with Adams?** Heckler's footnote 4 reserved it; the Thompson memo
  treats it as a limit the executive "ordinarily" cannot cross; [[United States v. Texas (2023)]]
  (Kavanaugh) again reserved a "Heckler-style 'abdication' argument." Know that Adams is a court of
  appeals decision the Supreme Court has repeatedly declined to reject or adopt.
- **The Price test.** Note 7 in the Thompson section: non-enforcement authority extends "neither
  to prospective licensing of prohibited conduct nor to policy-based nonenforcement of federal laws
  for entire categories of offenders." Adams fits: HEW's policy was category-wide non-enforcement,
  and the court treated it as unlawful.
