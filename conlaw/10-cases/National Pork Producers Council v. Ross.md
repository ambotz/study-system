## Snapshot

California's Proposition 12 forbids the in-state sale of pork from breeding pigs confined as its voters condemned, almost all of that pork raised elsewhere. Two trade associations sued on their members' behalf and lost on the pleadings below. The Court affirmed 5–4, holding that the dormant Commerce Clause carries no "almost per se" rule against laws with extraterritorial practical effects and that this Pike claim fails. Article I, § 8, cl. 3 is the provision in play.

## Issue

- Is there an "almost per se" rule against state laws whose practical effect is to control out-of-state commerce?
- Does a Pike claim lie where the plaintiff concedes no discrimination, facial or in operation?
- May a court weigh economic costs to out-of-state producers against noneconomic local benefits?
- Did the complaint plausibly allege a substantial burden on interstate commerce?
- Are noneconomic out-of-state harms cognizable burdens?

## Rule

The dormant Commerce Clause contains no "almost per se" rule against laws whose practical effect is to control out-of-state commerce: absent purposeful discrimination, a state may condition the in-state sale of an ordinary consumer good on how it was produced elsewhere. A Pike claim requires a pleaded substantial burden on the interstate market.

- The antidiscrimination principle "lies at the 'very core'" of the doctrine, barring "regulatory measures designed to benefit in-state economic interests by burdening out-of-state competitors."
- Pike requires, as a threshold, facts plausibly showing a substantial burden on interstate commerce; a "speculative" possibility of harm is not enough.
- The clause protects "the interstate market . . . from prohibitive or burdensome regulations," not "particular . . . firms" (*Exxon*).
- Companies "that choose to sell products in various States must normally comply with the laws of those various States."

## Operative facts

- **The pork industry is largely vertically integrated: farmers sell to large processors that distribute "different parts . . . all over to completely different end users."** *Segregating compliant pork would take new capital investment — the source of the pleaded compliance costs.*
- **In November 2018, with about 63% of voters in support, California adopted Proposition 12, revising its sale standards for eggs and setting new ones for pork and veal.** *A voter initiative, not a legislature's trade measure — the democratic pedigree Gorsuch invokes in Part V.*
- **Proponents pointed to pregnant pigs "[e]ncased" for sixteen weeks at a time in "fit-to-size" metal crates; opponents answered that existing practices better protected animals and consumers.** *The claimed benefit is moral and partly health-based — the noneconomic good Part IV-B will not set against dollars.*
- **The measure forbids the in-state sale of whole pork from breeding pigs "confined in a cruel manner," defined as confinement preventing a pig from "lying down, standing up, fully extending [its] limbs, or turning around freely."** *Regulating in-state sales is what lets the Court treat the law as territorial.*
- **California imports almost all the pork it consumes, so "the majority" of compliance costs "will be initially borne by out-of-state firms."** *Accepted as pleaded and held insufficient: a burden falling mostly out of state is not one on the market.*
- **Congress "has yet to adopt any statute that might displace Proposition 12."** *No preemption, so the only route is the negative implication of the Commerce Clause.*
- **The National Pork Producers Council and the American Farm Bureau Federation sued California officials and disavowed any discrimination claim, conceding "that Proposition 12 imposes the same burdens on in-state pork producers that it imposes on out-of-state ones."** *The concession removes the core of the doctrine, leaving only the two theories the Court rejects.*
- **The complaint also conceded that 28% of the industry "has already converted to some form of group housing" and that complying producers can "pas[s] along" at least "some" costs to consumers.** *These concessions sink the substantial-burden allegation in Part IV-C.*
- **The district court dismissed for failure to state a claim, the Ninth Circuit affirmed, and the Court "agreed to consider the complaint's legal sufficiency for ourselves."** *Everything turns on the pleadings, which is why the fight in Part IV-C is over plausibility rather than proof.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (affirmed 5–4; Gorsuch, J., for the Court in Parts I, II, III, IV-A and V, joined by Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan and Barrett, JJ.; Roberts, C.J., Alito, Kavanaugh and Jackson, JJ., concurring in part and dissenting in part)

- **No "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule** (Part III, for the Court).
- **Outside Pike's heartland** (Part IV-A, for the Court). No discriminatory purpose is even alleged, and "no clear line" separates Pike from the antidiscrimination cases.
- **Courts cannot weigh incommensurables** (Part IV-B, three votes). "No neutral legal rule guides the way."
- **No substantial burden pleaded** (Part IV-C, four votes). The facts "merely allege harm to some producers' favored 'methods of operation.'"
- **Noneconomic derivative harms are not cognizable** (Part IV-D, three votes), and "extreme caution" governs throughout (Part V). Affirmed.

**Gorsuch, J., for the Court**

- Frames the case as two invitations to "fashion two new and more aggressive constitutional restrictions on the ability of States to regulate goods sold within their borders. We decline that invitation."
- Sets the baseline at *Gibbons v. Ogden*: reserved state powers let states regulate in ways "sure to have 'a remote and considerable influence on commerce' in other States" — "[i]nspection laws, quarantine laws, [and] health laws of every description."
- Argues from consequences rather than text, since "many (maybe most) state laws have the 'practical effect of controlling' extraterritorial behavior," and relocates the concern to "sovereignty and comity," Due Process and Full Faith and Credit.

**Gorsuch, J., plurality portions**

- Part IV-B: the goods cannot be compared, the task being "like being asked to decide 'whether a particular line is longer than a particular rock is heavy.'"
- Part IV-C: under *Exxon* the choice to comply, segregate or exit falls "primarily—but not exclusively—for out-of-state businesses"; costs "ultimately borne by in-state consumers thanks to a law they adopted" are not cognizable.

**Sotomayor and Kagan, JJ., concurring in part**

- Affirm "because petitioners fail to allege a substantial burden . . . as required by Pike, not because of any fundamental reworking of that doctrine."

**Barrett, J., concurring in part**

- Agrees the goods are incommensurable, but would hold the costs plausibly alleged as "pervasive, burdensome, and . . . felt primarily (but not exclusively) outside California."

**Roberts, C.J., with Alito, Kavanaugh and Jackson, JJ., dissenting in part**

- Would hold a substantial burden plausibly alleged, distinguishing compliance costs "from other economic harms to the interstate market," and would vacate and remand.

**Kavanaugh, J., separately**

- The opinion's only trace is Gorsuch's Part IV-D, saying he "says the quiet part aloud" about California's market-size leverage.

## Arguments rejected

- *The extraterritoriality trilogy states an "almost per se" rule, and Proposition 12 makes out-of-state farmers rebuild their barns.* Each of those cases condemned a law that "deliberately 'prevent[ed out-of-state firms] from undertaking competitive pricing'"; petitioners' reading would shadow much reserved state power.
- *A 9.2% production-cost increase borne mostly out of state is "clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits."* For the Court, no discriminatory purpose is alleged; for three Justices the comparison cannot be made at all.
- *California's market is so large that conditioning access to it lets one state set national policy.* If size made the difference, voters in smaller-market States would be "constitutionally entitled to greater authority to regulate in-state sales."
- *Pike reaches "excessive" harm whether or not discrimination is shown (the lead dissent).* Many states ban horsemeat, so "all it would take is one complaint from an unhappy out-of-state producer and—presto—the Constitution would protect the sale of horsemeat."
- *The remedy cannot be left to a Congress that has refused to act for years.* Winning "a majority of a handful of judges may seem easier than marshaling a majority of elected representatives across the street."

## Where it sits

**The Corfield pairing.** Pork Producers is the dormant Commerce Clause counterpart to [[Corfield v. Coryell]]. Both police state laws that fall harder on outsiders, with different instruments: Article IV, § 2 protects **citizens**, asking whether a visitor was denied a fundamental privilege the state grants its own; the dormant Commerce Clause protects the **interstate market**, reaches corporations, and asks about protectionist purpose and effect.

**Three narrowings.** Extraterritoriality is dead as an independent theory, the trilogy reclassified as price-control cases inside the antidiscrimination principle. Pike is demoted from independent balancing to an evidentiary route to discriminatory purpose. And *Exxon*'s rule is generalized: no protection for "particular structure[s] or methods of operation."

**The fracture is the lesson.** Five Justices affirm, three say courts cannot balance incommensurables, four say the complaint failed to plead a substantial burden, and four would vacate and remand.

**Federalism, not commerce.** Part V opens on the state tariffs that preceded the Constitution, Rhode Island's tax on imported "New-England Rum" among them — the Confederation's failure, which runs through [[Before the Constitution]] and [[A Map of the Federalism Provisions]]. The "competing claims of sovereign authority" sit beside [[Texas v. White]]'s "indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States."

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

**What is the holding?** On the pleadings, a nondiscriminatory state law banning the in-state sale of a good produced in a way the state's voters condemn does not violate the dormant Commerce Clause. Start with the concession; everything follows from it.

**Why is there no majority on Pike itself?** Barrett and the Sotomayor pair each accept half of Part IV: Barrett says the balance cannot be struck but the burden was pleaded; Sotomayor and Kagan say the balance can be struck but the burden was not. Pike survives, its substantial-burden threshold doing most of the work, with no majority behind its balance.

**Is extraterritoriality dead?** As a freestanding rule under this clause, yes. The interests it protected are redistributed to other doctrines, and "sovereignty and comity" is the most transferable idea.

**Vary the law: California bans the in-state sale of pork raised in Iowa.** That is facial discrimination by origin and fails at the core of the doctrine. Proposition 12 regulates a **practice**, not an **origin**. Flip it — California produces all its own pork and bans imported crate-raised pork — and the practical effect insulates in-state producers, which is how Pike exposes a discriminatory purpose.

**Vary the law: every sale anywhere by a firm doing business in California must comply.** Now the state regulates wholly out-of-state transactions, and Gorsuch's own examples suggest the law fails — under Due Process and sovereignty principles rather than an extraterritoriality rule.

**Why does *Exxon* do so much work?** It supplies the pleading rule: the clause protects a market, not the firms currently serving it. If compliant pork gets produced by someone, the market is intact even if today's producers lose. The allegation that would have satisfied the Part IV-C plurality is that no producer could profitably comply.

**The originalist thread.** Some Justices think the antidiscrimination principle "may be more appropriately housed elsewhere in the Constitution" — the Import-Export Clause, the Privileges and Immunities Clause, or the preemption structure — and Gorsuch declines to decide. The negative implication of Article I, § 8, cl. 3 has thin textual support, which is why the opinion retreats to caution and to Congress.

**Formalism and functionalism.** Gorsuch's is a competence argument, not a text argument: judges lack a "neutral legal rule" for weighing pork prices against moral objections to gestation crates. Compare Jackson's institutional realism in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] and the political-safeguards reasoning of [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]].

**Memorable lines.** "While the Constitution addresses many weighty issues, the type of pork chops California merchants may sell is not on that list." "Your guess is as good as ours." The "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause.
