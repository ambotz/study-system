## Rule

The dormant Commerce Clause contains no "almost per se" rule against state laws that have the practical effect of controlling out-of-state commerce: absent purposeful discrimination, a state may condition the in-state sale of an ordinary consumer good on how it was produced elsewhere. A Pike claim requires pleading a substantial burden on the interstate market, and harm to particular producers' preferred "methods of operation" is not one.

## Facts

- California's Proposition 12 forbids the in-state **sale** of whole pork from breeding pigs "confined in a cruel manner," defined as confinement preventing a pig from "lying down, standing up, fully extending [its] limbs, or turning around freely." *A restriction on sales inside California is what lets the Court treat the law as territorial regulation.*
- Petitioners **disavowed any discrimination claim**, conceding "that Proposition 12 imposes the same burdens on in-state pork producers that it imposes on out-of-state ones." *The concession removes the core of the doctrine and forces them onto the two theories the Court rejects.*
- California imports almost all the pork it consumes, so "the majority" of compliance costs "will be initially borne by out-of-state firms." *Accepted as pleaded and held insufficient: a burden falling mostly out of state is not a burden on the interstate market.*
- The complaint conceded that 28% of the industry "has already converted to some form of group housing" and that complying producers can "pas[s] along" at least "some" of their costs to consumers. *These two concessions sink the substantial-burden allegation in Part IV-C.*
- Congress "has yet to adopt any statute that might displace Proposition 12." *No preemption, so the only route is the negative implication of the Commerce Clause.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (judgment affirmed 5–4; Gorsuch, J., announcing the judgment of the Court and writing for the Court in Parts I, II, III, IV-A and V, joined by Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan and Barrett, JJ.)

- **No "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule** (Part III, for the Court). Baldwin, Brown-Forman and Healy are discrimination cases, not the source of a freestanding rule: "each typifies the familiar concern with preventing purposeful discrimination against out-of-state economic interests." They involved "price control or price affirmation statutes" that tied "the price of . . . in-state products to out-of-state prices."
- **Language in those opinions cannot be read as a statute.** "[T]he language of an opinion is not always to be parsed as though we were dealing with language of a statute." The highlighted phrases "appeared in a particular context and did particular work."
- **Extraterritorial effects are ubiquitous.** "In our interconnected national marketplace, many (maybe most) state laws have the 'practical effect of controlling' extraterritorial behavior" — income tax, environmental, libel, securities, charitable registration, franchise and tort laws, and since the founding an "immense mass" of "[i]nspection laws, quarantine laws, [and] health laws of every description."
- **Territory still matters, but not through this clause.** Disputes about the reach of one state's power are resolved through "original and historical understandings of the Constitution's structure," the "principles of 'sovereignty and comity' it embraces," and express provisions including "the Due Process Clause and the Full Faith and Credit Clause." "[T]he antidiscrimination principle found in our dormant Commerce Clause cases may well represent one more effort to mediate competing claims of sovereign authority under our horizontal separation of powers." The Court "has never before claimed so much 'ground for judicial supremacy under the banner of the dormant Commerce Clause.'"
- **Pike is largely a discrimination detector, and this claim falls outside it** (Part IV-A, for the Court). "[N]o clear line" separates Pike from the core antidiscrimination precedents; "the Pike line serves as an important reminder that a law's practical effects may also disclose the presence of a discriminatory purpose." Pike itself concerned an Arizona order that "requir[ed] business operations to be performed in [state] that could more efficiently be performed elsewhere." Petitioners "nowhere suggest that an examination of Proposition 12's practical effects in operation would disclose purposeful discrimination," so their claim "falls well outside Pike's heartland."
- **Extreme caution** (Part V, for the Court). "'[E]xtreme caution' is warranted before a court deploys this implied authority"; displacing a democratically adopted state law under the dormant Commerce Clause is "a matter of 'extreme delicacy,'" done only "where the infraction is clear." Petitioners' two invitations are "incautious" and both are declined. Affirmed.
- **Courts cannot weigh incommensurables** (Part IV-B, Gorsuch, J., joined by Thomas and Barrett, JJ. — three votes only). "How is a court supposed to compare or weigh economic costs (to some) against noneconomic benefits (to others)? No neutral legal rule guides the way." The task "is like being asked to decide 'whether a particular line is longer than a particular rock is heavy.'" "In a functioning democracy, policy choices like these usually belong to the people and their elected representatives."
- **The complaint pleads no substantial burden** (Part IV-C, Gorsuch, J., joined by Thomas, Sotomayor and Kagan, JJ. — four votes). [[Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland|Exxon]] controls: the dormant Commerce Clause protects the "interstate market . . . from prohibitive or burdensome regulations," not "particular . . . firms" or "particular structure[s] or methods of operation." "That goes for pigs no less than gas stations." The pleaded facts "merely allege harm to some producers' favored 'methods of operation.'"
- **Noneconomic "derivative harms" are not cognizable burdens** (Part IV-D, Gorsuch, J., joined by Thomas and Barrett, JJ.). Petitioners never asked any court "to treat putative harms to out-of-state animal welfare or other noneconomic interests as freestanding harms"; the Court's decisions authorize claims alleging "burdens on commerce," not "a roving license" to reassess state legislation "in light of any conceivable out-of-state interest, economic or otherwise."

**Rejected**

- *[[Baldwin v. G. A. F. Seelig|Baldwin]], [[Brown-Forman Distillers Corp. v. New York State Liquor Authority|Brown-Forman]] and [[Healy v. Beer Institute|Healy]] together establish an "almost per se" rule against state laws with the "practical effect of controlling commerce outside the State," and Proposition 12 does exactly that by forcing farmers in Iowa and North Carolina to rebuild their barns.* Gorsuch: read in context, each case condemned a law that "deliberately 'prevent[ed out-of-state firms] from undertaking competitive pricing'"; Baldwin's minimum-price law "plainly discriminate[d]" and "operated like 'a tariff or customs duty'"; Brown-Forman and Healy "differed from Baldwin only in that they involved price-affirmation, rather than price-fixing, statutes." Petitioners' reading "would cast a shadow over laws long understood to represent valid exercises of the States' constitutionally reserved powers" and "invite endless litigation and inconsistent results." "Can anyone really suppose Baldwin, Brown-Forman, and Healy meant to do so much?"
- *Even a nondiscriminatory law falls if its burdens are "clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits," and a 9.2% production-cost increase borne mostly by out-of-state farmers is clearly excessive next to a moral preference about animals California voters will never see.* For the Court: the claim is outside Pike's heartland because no discriminatory purpose is even alleged. For three Justices: the comparison cannot be made at all, and "[j]udges cannot displace the cost-benefit analyses embodied in democratically adopted legislation guided by nothing more than their own faith in 'Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics.'"
- *California's market is so large that conditioning access to it on production practices lets one state set national policy, which is precisely the evil of a national market carved up by local rules.* Gorsuch (Part IV-D): if market size made the difference, "voters in States with smaller markets are constitutionally entitled to greater authority to regulate in-state sales than voters in States with larger markets. So much for the Constitution's 'fundamental principle of equal sovereignty among the States.'"
- *The lead dissent's position: Pike reaches "excessive" harm to the interstate market for a good, whether or not any discrimination is shown.* Gorsuch: many states ban the sale of horsemeat, which "har[m] the interstate market" for it; under that test "all it would take is one complaint from an unhappy out-of-state producer and—presto—the Constitution would protect the sale of horsemeat," and likewise for fireworks and single-use plastic bags. Such a rule "would require any consumer good available for sale in one State to be made available in every State."
- *The remedy for a massive nationwide disruption cannot be left to a Congress that has refused to act for years.* Gorsuch: the "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause gives Congress the power and the institutional competence, and "it is hard not to wonder whether petitioners have ventured here only because winning a majority of a handful of judges may seem easier than marshaling a majority of elected representatives across the street."

## Context

Pork Producers is the **dormant Commerce Clause** counterpart to [[Corfield v. Coryell]]. Both police state laws that fall harder on outsiders; they do it with different instruments. Article IV § 2 protects **citizens** and asks whether the state has denied a visitor a fundamental privilege it grants its own; the dormant Commerce Clause protects the **interstate market**, reaches corporations, and asks about protectionist purpose and effect. Note 3 of the casebook's Corfield notes says as much: "Many of these cases are decided under the so-called 'dormant Commerce Clause.' . . . But not all." Corfield itself carried a dormant commerce claim, which Justice Washington rejected in a single unreported paragraph. Two centuries later the doctrine is still, on this Court's telling, mostly about discrimination.

The case narrows the doctrine in three ways worth naming. First, **extraterritoriality is dead as an independent theory**; Baldwin, Brown-Forman and Healy are reclassified as price-control cases inside the antidiscrimination principle. Second, **Pike is demoted** from an independent balancing test to an evidentiary route to discriminatory purpose, though the Court preserves the "courtroom door open" language from Department of Revenue of Kentucky v. Davis and concedes that "a small number of our cases have invalidated state laws . . . that appear to have been genuinely nondiscriminatory." Third, **Exxon's rule is generalized**: no protection for "particular structure[s] or methods of operation."

Count the votes carefully, because the fracture is the lesson. Five Justices affirm. Only three (Gorsuch, Thomas, Barrett) say courts cannot balance incommensurables; only four (Gorsuch, Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan) say the complaint failed to plead a substantial burden. Justice Barrett agrees the goods are incommensurable but thinks the burden was adequately pleaded; Justices Sotomayor and Kagan think the burden was inadequately pleaded but would not rework Pike. Four Justices (Roberts, C.J., with Alito, Kavanaugh and Jackson) would vacate and remand. The result: Pike survives, with no majority for either killing it or applying it.

Gorsuch's Part V is a federalism argument, not a commerce argument, and it belongs with the Union material. He opens it with the state tariffs that preceded the Constitution — Rhode Island's tax on imported "New-England Rum," Connecticut's duties on goods "brought into th[e] State, by Land or Water, from any of the United States of America," Virginia's tax on "vessels coming within th[e S]tate from any of the United States" — the same failure of the Confederation that runs through [[Before the Constitution]] and [[A Map of the Federalism Provisions]]. The "equal sovereignty among the States" point in Part IV-D, and the observation that the antidiscrimination principle mediates "competing claims of sovereign authority under our horizontal separation of powers," sit next to [[Texas v. White]]'s "indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States": the horizontal relations among states are structural, not merely commercial.

The Syllabus's own statement of the Framers' design cites both halves of the problem — "See U. S. Const., Art. I, §8, cl. 3; Art. IV, §2" — which is the tidiest possible statement of why this case and Corfield are assigned together.

Post-decision, the invitation to Congress has been taken up and not yet accepted: preemption language aimed at Proposition 12 has moved through House Farm Bill drafts while Senate versions have been marked up without resolving it, and as of September 2026 no federal statute displaces the California law (verified by search, not from the opinion). The doctrinal point survives the legislative news — the Court's answer to a complaint about one state setting national policy is that the remedy is a federal statute.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Two trade associations sued California officials on behalf of member pork farmers and processors; the district court dismissed the complaint for failure to state a claim, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed in a unanimous panel opinion by Judge Ikuta.
- The Supreme Court "agreed to consider the complaint's legal sufficiency for ourselves." Everything is decided on the pleadings, with petitioners' factual allegations taken as true — which is why the fight in Part IV-C is over plausibility rather than proof.

### Facts

In November 2018, with about 63% of participating voters in support, California adopted Proposition 12, revising its standards for in-state sales of eggs and setting new standards for pork and veal. Proponents pointed to pregnant pigs "[e]ncased" for sixteen weeks at a time in "fit-to-size" metal crates, and argued the measure would remove such pork "from the California marketplace" and reduce food-poisoning risk; opponents answered that existing practices better protected animal welfare by preventing pig-on-pig aggression, better protected consumers by avoiding contamination, and would impose new costs. The pork industry is largely vertically integrated, with farmers selling to large processors that distribute "different parts . . . all over to completely different end users," so segregating and tracing compliant pork would require new capital investment. The National Pork Producers Council and the American Farm Bureau Federation sued on their members' behalf.

### Issue

- Does the dormant Commerce Clause impose an "almost per se" rule against state laws that have the practical effect of controlling commerce outside the state, even absent purposeful discrimination?
- Does a Pike claim lie where the plaintiff concedes the law neither discriminates on its face nor operates so as to reveal a discriminatory purpose?
- May a court, under Pike, weigh a law's economic costs to out-of-state producers against its noneconomic benefits to in-state residents?
- Did the complaint plausibly allege a substantial burden on interstate commerce?
- Are noneconomic harms to out-of-state interests — animal welfare, husbandry traditions, industry practice — cognizable burdens under the dormant Commerce Clause?

### Holding

- No, for the Court. Baldwin, Brown-Forman and Healy are price-control and price-affirmation cases resting on purposeful discrimination; a rule against laws with extraterritorial "practical effects" would condemn inspection, quarantine, health, tax, environmental and tort laws alike.
- Such a claim "falls well outside Pike's heartland," for the Court. There is "no clear line" between the Pike line and the core antidiscrimination precedents, and Pike's function is to expose discriminatory purpose through practical effects; petitioners disclaim both facial discrimination and discriminatory operation.
- No, but only three Justices so hold (Gorsuch, Thomas, Barrett). The competing goods "are insusceptible to resolution by reference to any juridical principle," and the choice belongs to voters and legislatures.
- No, and only four Justices so hold (Gorsuch, Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan). Under Exxon the pleaded facts show harm to particular firms' "methods of operation," and a substantial harm to interstate commerce "remains nothing more than a speculative possibility."
- Not on this record, three Justices (Gorsuch, Thomas, Barrett). Petitioners never advanced such a theory, and the Court's cases authorize claims alleging "burdens on commerce," not a "roving license."

### Rule

- The Commerce Clause's negative implication forbids state laws that seek to "build up . . . domestic commerce" through "burdens upon the industry and business of other States"; absent purposeful discrimination, "a State may exclude from its territory, or prohibit the sale therein of any articles which, in its judgment, fairly exercised, are prejudicial to" its citizens' interests.
- The antidiscrimination principle "lies at the 'very core'" of the doctrine: it bars laws "driven by . . . 'economic protectionism—that is, regulatory measures designed to benefit in-state economic interests by burdening out-of-state competitors.'"
- There is no separate "almost per se" rule against laws with extraterritorial effects. Baldwin and Healy state a rule about "price control or price affirmation statutes."
- Pike requires, as a threshold, facts plausibly showing a substantial burden on interstate commerce; a "speculative" possibility of harm is not enough.
- The clause protects "the interstate market . . . from prohibitive or burdensome regulations," not "particular . . . firms" or "particular structure[s] or methods of operation" (Exxon).
- Companies "that choose to sell products in various States must normally comply with the laws of those various States."

### Reasoning

**(Gorsuch, J., for the Court — Parts I, II, III, IV-A, V)**

- Frames the case as a pleading case about two novel theories: "they invite us to fashion two new and more aggressive constitutional restrictions on the ability of States to regulate goods sold within their borders. We decline that invitation."
- Sets the baseline at [[Gibbons v. Ogden|Gibbons]]: Chief Justice Marshall recognized that reserved state powers let states regulate in ways "sure to have 'a remote and considerable influence on commerce' in other States," instancing "[i]nspection laws, quarantine laws, [and] health laws of every description."
- Declines to engage the critiques of the dormant Commerce Clause itself: some Members "have suggested that it may be more appropriately housed elsewhere in the Constitution," but "we have no need to engage with any of them to resolve this case."
- Reclassifies the extraterritoriality trilogy by looking at what each law did, not at the adjectives used to describe it.
- Argues from the consequences of the proposed rule — the list of state laws with out-of-state ripple effects — rather than from text.
- Uses [[Minnesota v. Clover Leaf Creamery Co|Clover Leaf Creamery]], [[Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland|Exxon]] and [[United Haulers Assn v. Oneida-Herkimer Solid Waste Management Authority|United Haulers]] to show that "the presence or absence of discrimination in practice proved decisive" in the Pike line itself.
- Ends on institutional competence and the founding-era tariff problem, pointing petitioners to Congress.

**(Gorsuch, J., plurality — Part IV-B, with Thomas and Barrett, JJ.)**

- Refuses "freewheeling" cost-benefit review: "our cases have expressly cautioned against judges using the dormant Commerce Clause as 'a roving license for federal courts to decide what activities are appropriate for state and local government to undertake.'"
- Draws the Lochner parallel explicitly: "'[t]here was a time when this Court presumed to make such binding judgments for society, under the guise of interpreting the Due Process Clause,'" and the Court has refused "'to reclaim that ground'" under the Commerce Clause.
- Notes petitioners' concessions that states may ban goods they deem immoral (goods made with child labor) and may address "imperfectly understood" health risks — which, he says, is arguably just what Proposition 12 does.
- "Your guess is as good as ours." And: "More accurately, your guess is better than ours."

**(Gorsuch, J., plurality — Part IV-C, with Thomas, Sotomayor and Kagan, JJ.)**

- Runs the Exxon comparison item by item: in Exxon the law posed a choice only for out-of-state firms and merely shifted market share among out-of-state firms; here the choice (comply, segregate, or exit California) falls "primarily—but not exclusively—for out-of-state businesses," and the same market-share shift is possible.
- Turns the complaint's own concessions against it: 28% conversion already, costs partly passed to consumers, and a declaration that some out-of-state consumers "will 'not value these changes and will not pay an increased price.'"
- Costs "ultimately borne by in-state consumers thanks to a law they adopted" are not a cognizable harm.

**(Gorsuch, J., plurality — Part IV-D, with Thomas and Barrett, JJ.)**

- Credits the lead dissent for rejecting the "almost per se" rule and for disapproving "freewheeling judicial weighing of benefits and burdens," then attacks its alternative as a back door to the same result.
- The horsemeat, fireworks and plastic-bag examples.
- The equal-sovereignty answer to the market-size argument.

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Sotomayor, J., concurring in part, joined by Kagan, J.)**

- Joins all but Parts IV-B and IV-D, and writes "to clarify my understanding of why petitioners' Pike claim fails."
- Affirms "because petitioners fail to allege a substantial burden on interstate commerce as required by Pike, not because of any fundamental reworking of that doctrine." Courts can, in principle, do what Pike asks.
- Treats the substantial-burden allegation as "a threshold requirement that plaintiffs must satisfy before courts need even engage in Pike's balancing and tailoring analyses."

**(Barrett, J., concurring in part)**

- Agrees the goods are incommensurable: California's interest in keeping allegedly inhumane products out of its markets "cannot be weighed on a scale opposite dollars and cents."
- Disagrees on the pleading point: the complaint plausibly alleges that Proposition 12's costs "are pervasive, burdensome, and will be felt primarily (but not exclusively) outside California."
- Her two halves are why there is no majority in Part IV: she supplies the third vote for incommensurability and withholds the fifth vote for the pleading holding.

**(Roberts, C.J., concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Alito, Kavanaugh and Jackson, JJ.)**

- Accepts the rejection of the "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule and does not defend "freewheeling" balancing.
- States Pike as it has always been stated: nondiscriminatory regulations are valid "unless the burden imposed on [interstate] commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits."
- Would hold that petitioners plausibly alleged a substantial burden, distinguishing "the costs of complying with a given state regulation from other economic harms to the interstate market," and would vacate and remand for the Ninth Circuit to decide whether that burden is clearly excessive in relation to California's putative local interests.

**(Kavanaugh, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)**

- Filed a solo opinion. It was not retrieved for this file; the only trace of it here is Gorsuch's Part IV-D, which says Kavanaugh "says the quiet part aloud" about the leverage California's market size gives it. Read it in the Canvas PDF before class.

### Cold-call notes

- **What is the holding?** On the pleadings, a nondiscriminatory state law banning in-state sale of a good produced in a way the state's voters condemn does not violate the dormant Commerce Clause, even though almost all of the affected production happens elsewhere. Start with the concession: petitioners gave up discrimination, and everything follows from that.
- **Who joined what?** Parts I, II, III, IV-A and V are the opinion of the Court (Gorsuch with Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan, Barrett). Parts IV-B and IV-D are a plurality of three (Gorsuch, Thomas, Barrett). Part IV-C is a plurality of four (Gorsuch, Thomas, Sotomayor, Kagan). Sotomayor (with Kagan) and Barrett concur in part; Roberts (with Alito, Kavanaugh, Jackson) and Kavanaugh concur in part and dissent in part. Judgment 5–4.
- **Why is there no majority on Pike itself?** Because Barrett and the Sotomayor pair each accept half of Gorsuch's Part IV and reject the other half. Barrett: the balance cannot be struck, but the burden was pleaded. Sotomayor and Kagan: the balance can be struck, but the burden was not pleaded. Four Justices would have let the case proceed. So Pike remains good law with no controlling account of what it requires.
- **Is extraterritoriality dead?** As a freestanding "almost per se" rule under this clause, yes. The interests it protected are redistributed: to the antidiscrimination principle, to Due Process and Full Faith and Credit, and to structural "sovereignty and comity." Be ready to name that redistribution — it is the most transferable idea in the case.
- **Vary the law: California bans the in-state sale of pork raised in Iowa.** That is facial discrimination against an out-of-state source, and it fails at the core of the doctrine without any of this analysis. The point of Proposition 12 is that it regulates a **practice**, not an **origin**.
- **Vary the law: California requires that pork sold anywhere by a firm doing business in California be Proposition 12 compliant.** Now the state regulates wholly out-of-state transactions, and Gorsuch's own examples — a state may not exempt a second state's residents' securities from that state's taxes — suggest the law fails, though under Due Process and sovereignty principles rather than an extraterritoriality rule.
- **Vary the facts: California is the only state producing pork and imports none.** Then the law's burdens fall on in-staters and no one sues. Reverse it: if California produced all its own pork and banned imported pork raised in crates, the practical effect would be to insulate in-state producers, and Pike's "practical effects" inquiry would expose the discriminatory purpose exactly as in [[Pike v. Bruce Church|Pike]] itself.
- **Why does Exxon do so much work?** Because it supplies the pleading rule. The dormant Commerce Clause protects a market, not the firms currently serving it. If the compliant pork gets produced by *someone*, the market is intact even if today's producers lose. Ask yourself what allegation would have satisfied the Part IV-C plurality: probably that no producer could profitably comply, so California's supply would fail and the national market would contract.
- **What happened to Pike balancing?** For the Court it survives but is characterized as a discrimination detector. For three Justices it cannot be performed when the benefits are noneconomic. For Roberts and Sotomayor it is a real test. The safe exam formulation: Pike is intact, its threshold (substantial burden) is now doing most of the work, and its balance has no majority behind it.
- **How does this relate to Corfield?** Same problem — a state favoring its own — two clauses. [[Corfield v. Coryell]] would not have helped these petitioners for three reasons: they are corporations and associations, not citizens; there is no discrimination between citizens to complain of; and Proposition 12 restricts a sale rather than denying anyone a fundamental privilege. Watch for the flip side: Article IV § 2 and the dormant Commerce Clause have different two-step tests, and the Article IV step-two (substantial reason, close relation) is stricter than the "extreme caution" Gorsuch demands here.
- **The originalist thread.** Gorsuch notes that some Justices think the antidiscrimination principle "may be more appropriately housed elsewhere in the Constitution" — the Import-Export Clause, the Privileges and Immunities Clause, or the preemption structure — and declines to decide. Baude's question is what the Constitution actually requires; the honest answer is that the negative implication of Article I § 8 cl. 3 has thin textual support, which is exactly why the opinion keeps retreating to caution and to Congress.
- **Formalism and functionalism.** Gorsuch's argument is a competence argument, not a text argument: judges lack a "neutral legal rule" for weighing pork prices against moral objections to gestation crates. Compare Jackson's institutional realism in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] and the political-safeguards reasoning of [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]]: in each, the Court hands a structural question back to the political branches because it cannot see a judicial standard.
- **Memorable lines.** "While the Constitution addresses many weighty issues, the type of pork chops California merchants may sell is not on that list." "[W]hether a particular line is longer than a particular rock is heavy." "That goes for pigs no less than gas stations." "Your guess is as good as ours." And: "More accurately, your guess is better than ours." The "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause. And the closing jab about "marshaling a majority of elected representatives across the street."
