## Rule

Congress cannot do through the purse what the Bill of Attainder Clause forbids it to do directly. A statute that cuts off the pay of named individuals, if its purpose and effect are to bar them permanently from government service for their supposed disloyalty, is "punishment without a judicial trial," and calling it an appropriation neither saves it nor puts it beyond judicial review.

## Facts

- **Section 304 named three people.** It cut off pay to Lovett, Watson and Dodd unless the President reappointed them with the Senate's consent. *Naming them makes it an attainder; the standard general rider against members of subversive organizations would not have been.*
- **The provision grew out of a legislative trial.** A House subcommittee held secret hearings, excluded lawyers, and found each respondent guilty of "subversive activity" and "unfit" for government employment. *This history supplies the determination of guilt Frankfurter finds missing from the face of § 304.*
- **The agencies kept all three working; only their pay stopped.** *That gave the Court of Claims a contract claim to decide.*
- **The Senate voted 69 to 0 against § 304, then yielded when the House refused to pass any appropriation without it.** *The purse let one House force the other.*
- **Roosevelt signed and called the provision "not only unwise and discriminatory, but unconstitutional."** *The ancestor of [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473|Obama's statement]].*
- **The Solicitor General agreed § 304 was unconstitutional, so Congress had to authorize special counsel to defend it.** *The case is also a removal dispute between the branches.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Black for the Court; Frankfurter, joined by Reed, concurring in the judgment; Jackson
took no part)

- **§ 304 is not a mere appropriation measure.** Its purpose "was not merely to cut off respondents' compensation through regular disbursing channels but permanently to bar them from government service."
- **The challenge is justiciable.** Otherwise "congressional action, aimed at three named individuals, which stigmatized their reputation and seriously impaired their chance to earn a living, could never be challenged in any court."
- **§ 304 is a bill of attainder.** "[L]egislative acts, no matter what their form, that apply either to named individuals or to easily ascertainable members of a group in such a way as to inflict punishment on them without a judicial trial are bills of attainder." Permanent exclusion from government service "is punishment, and of a most severe type."
- **Form does not matter.** Punishment "through the instrumentality of an Act specifically cutting off the pay" is "no less galling or effective than if it had been done by an Act which designated the conduct as criminal."
- **Removal and due process not reached.** The judgments for the respondents are affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *Congress has complete control over appropriations; a statute that only withholds pay is a political question with which courts have nothing to do.* Black: the premise fails on the facts, because § 304 was a dismissal and a permanent bar, not "a mere stoppage of disbursing routine," and a claim for pay for work done is the Court of Claims' ordinary business.
- *Read narrowly, § 304 only stopped disbursement from general funds and left the government's debt intact, so there is no constitutional question to decide.* Frankfurter's reading, and the Court of Claims'. Black: that reading "would completely frustrate the purpose of all who sponsored Section 304," which was to "purge" the government lists.
- *A bill of attainder historically declares guilt of a specified offense and imposes retribution; § 304 declares no guilt and names no crime, and withholding a government job may have non-punitive reasons.* Frankfurter's argument from history. The Court looks past the text to the committee's findings of "guilt" and treats permanent exclusion from a vocation as punishment, following Cummings and Garland.

## Context

**The purse as a weapon against individuals.** Lovett is the first answer in the casebook to the section's first question, how far Congress can use appropriations to limit the executive. The answer here is a limit on Congress: the Appropriations Clause gives Congress no license to evade Article I, § 9, cl. 3, which sits in the same section. The Court never reached the claim that § 304 usurped the President's removal power, but that is the separation-of-powers core of the case and it points straight to Class 6.

**Removal.** Frankfurter's concurrence names the problem the Court avoided: § 304, read as the Court reads it, is Congress removing executive employees, and the Court had wavered on removal from [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] (1926) to [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] (1935). Lovett is what Congress does when it cannot remove directly: it defunds the officer. See [[The Decision of 1789]], [[Myers v. United States]] and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]], and Class 7's [[Trump v. Slaughter]].

**The bill-of-attainder line.** [[Cummings v. Missouri]] and [[Ex parte Garland]] (1867) are the foundation. Later cases outside the casebook excerpt refined Lovett's test: [[United States v. Brown]] (1965) struck a bar on Communist Party members serving as union officers, and [[Nixon v. Administrator of General Services]] (1977) upheld a statute aimed at one former President's papers, asking whether the burden is punitive in history, function and motive. The specificity-plus-punishment frame in Lovett survives; the Nixon case shows that naming one person is not enough by itself.

**Avoidance.** Frankfurter's opinion is a textbook statement of the [[Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority|Ashwander]] canon, and it would have produced the same judgment. Compare the statutory-construction move that decides [[Gundy v. United States]].

**Signing statements.** Roosevelt's statement and the Solicitor General's refusal to defend set the pattern for [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]: the President signs a must-pass appropriations bill and announces that a rider is unconstitutional.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Suits in the Court of Claims by three federal employees for salary earned after November 15,
  1943. The Court of Claims entered judgment for them, the judges splitting on why: some read § 304 as a stoppage of disbursement only, others held it unconstitutional.
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari "because of the manifest importance of the questions involved" and affirmed. The Solicitor General argued for the respondents on removal and attainder; special counsel defended § 304 for Congress.

### Facts

In the late 1930s the House grew alarmed that "subversives" held government posts, and in 1938 it created the Committee on Un-American Activities, the Dies Committee. Congress then barred federal employment of members of organizations advocating overthrow of the government, and such clauses became standard in appropriations acts. In February 1943 Dies attacked 39 named employees on the House floor, including the three respondents, as "irresponsible, unrepresentative, crackpot, radical bureaucrats," and urged Congress to refuse "to appropriate money for their salaries." An amendment barring pay to all 39 drew charges of "legislative lynching," so the House instead authorized a special Appropriations subcommittee to examine the charges. The subcommittee described its work as "in the nature of an inquest of office" and rejected the Secretary of the Interior's "strong appeal" to keep Lovett. The House passed § 304 in the Urgent Deficiency Appropriation Act of 1943, and the three sued for their salary.

### Issue

- Is a challenge to § 304 justiciable, or is it an appropriations measure within Congress's unreviewable control?
- Is § 304 a bill of attainder forbidden by Article I, § 9, cl. 3?
- (Argued, not decided.) Does § 304 usurp the executive's removal power or violate due process?

### Holding

- Justiciable. § 304 was designed to force the respondents' discharge and bar them from all government service, not merely to regulate disbursement, and a claim for compensation for work performed is a controversy the Court of Claims exists to decide.
- Yes, a bill of attainder. § 304 singles out named individuals and imposes permanent exclusion from government employment, a severe punishment, on a legislative finding of disloyalty, with no judicial trial.
- Not reached. The Court found it unnecessary to decide the removal and due process claims.

### Rule

- "A bill of attainder is a legislative act which inflicts punishment without a judicial trial" ([[Cummings v. Missouri|Cummings]]).
- The prohibition reaches acts "no matter what their form" that apply to "named individuals or to easily ascertainable members of a group" and "inflict punishment on them without a judicial trial."
- Permanent exclusion from a vocation, including government service, is punishment.
- Congress's control of appropriations does not make a pay cutoff unreviewable when its purpose is to punish.

### Reasoning

**(Black, J., for the Court)**

- Reads § 304 in light of "the circumstances leading to its passage": the Dies speech, the subcommittee's "inquest," its findings of guilt, the House's insistence.
- Any narrower reading "would completely frustrate the purpose of all who sponsored Section 304."
- Justiciability follows from the reading: a congressional proscription of three named people cannot be immune from every court. "Our Constitution did not contemplate such a result."
- [[Cummings v. Missouri|Cummings]] and [[Ex parte Garland|Garland]] struck loyalty oaths that excluded people from professions; neither has been overruled, and the Court adheres to them.
- § 304 "operates as a legislative decree of perpetual exclusion" from a chosen vocation, a sanction Congress has otherwise used only for crimes like treason and bribery.

**(Frankfurter, J., joined by Reed, J., concurring)**

- Personal condemnation of § 304 would be easy; "the judicial function exacts considerations very different from those which may determine a vote in Congress."
- Quoting Holmes: legislatures "are ultimate guardians of the liberties and welfare of the people in quite as great a degree as the courts."
- Two kinds of constitutional provisions. Broad standards like due process leave room for judgment; specific ones like the Attainder Clause "were defined by history" and must be held to their historic limits.
- A bill of attainder is "the substitution of legislative determination of guilt and legislative imposition of punishment for judicial finding and sentence." Every historical bill specified an offense and declared guilt; § 304 does neither.
- Not every deprivation is punishment: "A man may be forbidden to practice medicine because he has been convicted of a felony, or because he is no longer qualified."
- The Court's reading raises removal and due process questions of great difficulty, as the swing from Myers to Humphrey's Executor shows. [[Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority|Ashwander]] requires the available construction: § 304 only stopped ordinary disbursement and left the government's obligation to pay, so the respondents recover.

### Dissent / concurrence

- No dissent. Frankfurter and Reed concur only in the judgment on the narrow statutory reading above; Jackson took no part.

### Cold-call notes

- **What was the House's best argument, and why did it fail?** Congress has plenary control of appropriations; nobody has a constitutional right to be paid from the Treasury; a refusal to fund is political. It failed because the Court read § 304 as a removal and permanent bar, not a funding choice. Be ready to say what the result would be if § 304 had cut off only one year's salary appropriation and left the employees free to be paid from other funds.
- **Vary the specificity.** A rider barring pay to anyone who belongs to an organization advocating violent overthrow was standard and is not challenged here. A rider naming three people is an attainder. A rider defunding the office of Assistant Secretary for X abolishes a position without naming its occupant: probably no attainder, but a removal question if the office is created only to remove its holder. Compare § 2262 in [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473|Obama's statement]], which defunded named advisory positions.
- **Vary the punishment.** Congress eliminates the three respondents' jobs in a budget cut with no findings of disloyalty. Frankfurter's point is that the harm is the same but the retributive element is missing; the Court's own reliance on the subcommittee's findings suggests the result would change.
- **Is this really a removal case?** The Solicitor General thought so, and removal is the first ground the respondents listed. The Court avoided it by deciding on attainder, which binds Congress whatever the President thinks. Link forward: if Congress cannot remove executive officers directly ([[Myers v. United States|Myers]]), can it do so by defunding them? Lovett suggests courts will look at purpose, not form. That question returns in Class 6 and in [[Trump v. Slaughter]].
- **Formalism or functionalism?** Black is functional about form ("no matter what their form") and formal about the category (legislative punishment is forbidden, full stop). Frankfurter is an originalist of a specific kind: specific clauses mean what their history made them mean, and the historical bill of attainder had features § 304 lacks. That is a strong original-meaning argument, and the Court did not answer it on history.
- **Who wins on original meaning?** Frankfurter's historical account of the formal features of attainder is the stronger narrow reading. Black's answer is Cummings and Garland, which already extended the clause to loyalty-based exclusions from professions in 1867. The debate is about whether the Reconstruction-era cases are part of the "gloss of history."
- **Tie to the purse unit.** The Senate voted 69–0 against § 304 and yielded anyway because the House held the war appropriations hostage. The purse gives each House a veto over all spending, which is also a lever to force unrelated measures. Contrast [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell|Burwell]], where the House sues because the executive spent money without it.
- **Tie to signing statements.** Roosevelt signed, objected, and complied, leaving the question to the courts. Obama in 2011 signed, objected, complied with some riders, and announced he would construe another not to bind him. Be ready to rank the two responses.
- **Memorable lines.** "[L]egislative lynching." "[A] legislative decree of perpetual exclusion." "[N]o less galling or effective." Frankfurter: "the most fundamental principle of constitutional adjudication is not to face constitutional questions but to avoid them, if at all possible."
