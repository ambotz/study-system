## Snapshot

New Jersey's 1820 oyster act reserved the fishery to residents and forfeited the vessel of anyone else who gathered oysters there. Pennsylvanians dredged Maurice River Cove in the Hiram, which was seized, condemned and sold; her Philadelphia owner sued in trespass in the federal circuit court. Washington, sitting as Circuit Justice, gave judgment for the defendant: Article IV § 2 secures only privileges "in their nature, fundamental," and no share in property a state holds in common for its citizens. The commerce and admiralty objections failed too.

## Issue

The introduction asks "what kind of equal treatment did the clause promise?" Corfield answers by defining the class of rights.

- Does the act infringe Congress's commerce power under Article I § 8?
- Does excluding nonresidents from the oyster fishery violate Article IV § 2's "privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states"?
- Does condemnation by two county justices trench on Article III § 2's admiralty jurisdiction?

## Rule

Article IV § 2 entitles a citizen of one state, while in another, only to those privileges and immunities that are "in their nature, fundamental." It is a rule of equal treatment within that class, not a transfer of one state's citizenship rights to visitors, and gives no share in property the state holds in common for its own citizens.

- The clause is confined to privileges "which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments; and which have, at all times, been enjoyed by the citizens of the several states which compose this Union."
- Each is held "subject nevertheless to such restraints as the government may justly prescribe for the general good of the whole."
- In regulating common property the legislature is not "bound to extend to the citizens of all the other states the same advantages as are secured to their own citizens"; the clause is no "grant of a cotenancy in the common property of the state."

## Operative facts

- **New Jersey's act of June 9, 1820 closed the oyster beds to everyone from May to September on a $10 penalty, and its second section forbade dredging at any time by "[n]o person residing in, or out of this state," on a $50 penalty.** *Both fall on residents and outsiders alike, so the case must rest on the one section that draws a line by residence.*
- **The sixth section made it unlawful for anyone "not, at the time, an actual inhabitant and resident of this state" to gather oysters from a vessel not wholly owned by a resident, forfeiting the vessel, oysters and rakes along with $10.** *The only feature of the act that raises Article IV § 2, and the provision the case is about.*
- **The seventh section let any person seize such a vessel and bring it before two county justices, who tried the case and ordered a sale on condemnation.** *State magistrates condemning a vessel in state waters generates the admiralty objection.*
- **The oyster beds were unappropriated: no several fishery existed, and the oysters lay stationary in soil and waters within the State.** *This is what lets Washington treat the fishery as property held in common by the citizens, unlike a trade, a lawsuit or a tax.*
- **Some Pennsylvanians chartered the Hiram out of Philadelphia and were "found engaged in taking oysters in Maurice river cove by means of dredges"; she was seized, condemned and sold.** *Nonresident use of a state resource — what Washington holds the clause does not reach.*
- **Corfield, the Pennsylvania owner, sued Coryell in trespass at Philadelphia, and the defendant justified under the act and the magistrates' sentence.** *A private damages action, so the objections arrive as answers to a justification, not a challenge to the statute.*
- **Plaintiff's counsel conceded at argument that New Jersey's exclusive right in the waters and soil "has never been ceded by that state, in express terms, to the United States."** *The concession settles the sovereignty premise of the holding.*
- **Washington heard the reserved points alone, as Circuit Justice.** *One Justice on circuit: nothing here binds anyone, part of why the case survives for its dicta.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (Washington, Circuit Justice, sitting on circuit; no vote and no separate opinion; judgment for the defendant)

- **No infringement of the commerce power.** The extract carries only the editors' statement of the holding and their cross-reference to *Gibbons v. Ogden*.
- **No violation of Article IV § 2.** The clause secures only privileges "in their nature, fundamental"; a share in the State's common property is not among them.
- **No trespass on admiralty jurisdiction**, again in the editors' summary only.

**Washington, Circuit Justice**

- Narrows the question at the outset: "The inquiry is, what are the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states?" The answer is a category, not whatever a given state happens to confer.
- Says enumeration "would perhaps be more tedious than difficult," then enumerates under general heads: protection by the government; life and liberty, with the right to acquire and possess property and to pursue happiness and safety; passage through or residence in another state "for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits, or otherwise"; habeas corpus; access to "the courts of the state"; taking and disposing of property; and "an exemption from higher taxes or impositions than are paid by the other citizens of the state."
- Adds the elective franchise and immediately drains it: "as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state in which it is to be exercised."
- Grounds the clause in comity, quoting the Articles of Confederation's preamble: the object was "the better to secure and perpetuate mutual friendship and intercourse among the people of the different states of the Union."
- Distinguishes two fisheries. A several fishery is private property, "as much the property of the individual to whom it belongs, as dry land." A common fishery "belongs to all the citizens or subjects of the state. It is the property of all," who "are so exclusively entitled to the use of it, that it cannot be enjoyed by others without the tacit consent, or the express permission of the sovereign."
- Answers the public-law argument with authority rather than principle: Rutherfoth and Grotius have dominion over soil and waters carry the power to exclude foreigners from the fish in them.
- Closes on consequences: such a construction "would, in many instances, be productive of the most serious public inconvenience and injury, particularly, in regard to those kinds of fish, which, by being exposed to too general use, may be exhausted."

## Arguments rejected

- *New Jersey lets its own citizens take oysters, so the clause must mean a Pennsylvanian gets whatever a New Jerseyman gets, and the sixth section denies exactly that.* Washington refuses the premise: the clause secures equality only in rights belonging "to the citizens of all free governments," and use of common property is not one.
- *Oysters in their beds have never been appropriated; they remain common stock to which all are equally entitled, so New Jersey has nothing to reserve.* Rutherfoth and Grotius answer that where the exclusive right in the water and soil "is vested in others, no other persons can claim the liberty of hunting, fishing, or fowling" there, and the sovereign "may prohibit foreigners . . . from taking them."
- *Seizing a Pennsylvania vessel in the coasting trade burdens commerce among the states and invades Article I § 8; and condemnation by two county justices usurps the admiralty jurisdiction Article III § 2 gives the federal courts.* Both rejected, and the extract prints only the editors' summaries, with a cross-reference to *Gibbons v. Ogden*.

## Where it sits

**Two clauses, one phrase.** Article IV § 2 is a comity rule: out-of-staters "do not carry their state's rights with them into another state: A Minnesota fishing license is not good in Wisconsin." The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges *or* Immunities Clause borrowed Washington's phrase for the opposite job — rights against one's own state — and its framers quoted this catalogue as the gloss. *Slaughter-House* (1873) refused the transfer.

**Dicta and holding.** The famous paragraph answers a question the court need not have reached, since the activity before it fell outside the clause; nothing on the list resembles oyster-raking. The operative rule is the narrow common-property exception.

**What modern doctrine kept.** The structure survived and the exception tightened. *Toomer v. Witsell* (1948) put commercial shrimping inside the clause; *Hicklin v. Orbeck* struck "Alaska Hire"; *Piper* (1985) found no substantial reason for a bar-residency rule; *Baldwin* (1978) left recreational elk hunting outside.

**The other policeman.** Most state-discrimination cases now run through the dormant Commerce Clause, and [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] is that branch two centuries on: it protects the market rather than citizens and reaches corporations (*Paul v. Virginia*). Article IV is federalism's horizontal half, following [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]]; the same section's fugitive-slave half is what [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] made unbearable.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "The Privileges and Immunities Clause is sometimes known as the Comity Clause . . . . [It] was understood to bind the states to give out-of-staters equal civil rights, but not equal political rights."**

- The clause creates no substantive rights: New Jersey could close the beds to everyone, as the first two sections did — the proof that it is an antidiscrimination rule.
- Civil rights are the rights of citizens in civil society; political rights — voting, office, jury service — inhere in the polity and may be withheld from outsiders.
- Which makes the elective franchise the odd item on Washington's list. His qualifier, "as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state," drains it — residence is one of those terms.

**Note 2. "Corfield is particularly famous for its list of 'fundamental' privileges and immunities . . . . But where does this list come from? Does it have a common theme?"**

- Not from the text. Washington ties the list to "the citizens of all free governments," so the source is natural law and the law of nations.
- The theme is what a person needs to function as a member of civil society anywhere: security, property, movement, access to courts, equal fiscal burdens.

**Note 3. "Do you agree with Corfield's formulation that the privileges and immunities . . . are limited to 'fundamental' privileges and immunities associated with 'citizenship'? . . . What does this mean for the right of non-residents to practice law?"**

- The civil/political line does more work than "fundamental," which is circular standing alone; state property is a third category, and it is the one Corfield actually applies.
- Law practice is the hard case. *Piper* treats it as a livelihood inside the clause; the casebook's aside that lawyers are officers of the state courts would make it political, like jury service.
- Washington's own arguments are structure and history (the Articles' preamble), natural law and the law of nations, and consequences; text barely, precedent not at all. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].

### Further drilling

- **What did it hold?** That New Jersey could exclude nonresidents from its oyster beds, because a state's common property sits outside the clause. Do not answer with the catalogue.
- **Vary the resource.** A residents-only right to *sell* oysters loses the rationale: selling is trade, which is on Washington's list. A higher nonresident licence fee edges toward the forbidden "higher taxes or impositions."
- **Vary the claimant.** A privately owned several fishery excludes "citizens or strangers" alike, so no discrimination. A Pennsylvania *corporation* owner drops Article IV out entirely (*Paul v. Virginia*).
- **Memorable details.** The Hiram; Maurice River Cove; $10 plus the boat, the oysters and the rakes. Washington quotes the clause as "all the privileges and immunities," a word the Constitution does not contain.
