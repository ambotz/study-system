## What it is

The casebook's opening to Part B, "Departmentalism and Judicial Supremacy" (pp. 397–402): a short introduction defining the two positions, then Lincoln's Springfield speech of June 26, 1857 and excerpts from the third and sixth Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858), all about the authority of [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] (1857). The Notes follow the question into Congress: the 1862 territorial slavery ban, the refused Taney bust, and the seating of Hiram Revels.

The casebook's definitions frame the whole class. "Departmentalism": each department "can and should independently interpret and enforce the Constitution as it performs its own distinctive functions." "Judicial supremacy": all government officials (or perhaps all citizens) "must treat judicial opinions about the Constitution as if they were the Constitution itself."

The reading is assigned because it sets up the three positions of Class 13 with the worst possible precedent as the test case. Douglas states judicial supremacy; Lincoln states a departmentalism that still accepts the judgment between the parties. [[Cooper v. Aaron]] (1958) takes Douglas's side with the moral valence reversed, and [[Ex parte Merryman]] asks whether even the judgment binds the President.

## What to notice

- **Judicial review does not obviously entail judicial supremacy.** The introduction asks whether the practice defended in [[The Federalist No. 78]] and exercised in [[Marbury v. Madison]] "impl[ies] a superiority of the judiciary over the other branches as to any question of interpretation of the Constitution," or would be "the nightmare envisioned by Brutus" ([[Brutus No. 11]]). Marbury's logic is that a court applying the Constitution in a case must prefer it to a statute; nothing in that logic says the President or Congress must adopt the court's reading outside the case.
- **The departmentalist tradition before Lincoln.** Jefferson, as President, concluded the Sedition Act was unconstitutional, halted prosecutions and pardoned those convicted, relying "on his own constitutional judgment, not the courts'." Jackson refused to follow [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s reasoning when he vetoed the Bank's recharter. Both acted through powers the Constitution gives the President alone (pardon, veto), which is the easiest setting for departmentalism.
- **Lincoln's two uses of judicial decisions (Springfield).** "Judicial decisions have two uses—first, to absolutely determine the case decided, and secondly, to indicate to the public how other similar cases will be decided when they arise." The first use binds; the second is a matter of precedential weight. This is the seed of the judgment/opinion distinction that Bates, and later Baude, develop.
- **Lincoln's "we offer no resistance."** Lincoln asks, "who resists it? Who has, in spite of the decision, declared Dred Scott free, and resisted the authority of his master over him?" His plan is to persuade the Court to overrule: "we shall do what we can to have it to over-rule this." Opposing a precedent is not disobeying a judgment.
- **Lincoln concedes more than a pure departmentalist would.** Decisions on constitutional questions, "when fully settled, should control, not only the particular cases decided, but the general policy of the country, subject to be disturbed only by amendments of the Constitution." "More than this would be revolution." Lincoln is a theorist of precedential weight, not a denier of judicial authority: a settled decision binds everyone.
- **Lincoln's test for when a decision is "settled."** A decision commands acquiescence if made "by the unanimous concurrence of the judges, and without any apparent partisan bias, and in accordance with legal public expectation, and with the steady practice of the departments throughout our history, and had been in no part, based on assumed historical facts which are not really true," or else "affirmed and re-affirmed through a course of years." Dred Scott fails every criterion: 7–2, partisan, contrary to the practice of the Missouri Compromise, and (Lincoln says) resting on false history about black citizenship at the founding.
- **Lincoln's sixth-debate formulation is the canonical departmentalist line.** "We do not propose that when Dred Scott has been decided to be a slave by the court, we, as a mob, will decide him to be free." But "we nevertheless do oppose that decision as a political rule, which shall be binding on the voter to vote for nobody who thinks it wrong, which shall be binding on the members of Congress or the President to favor no measure that does not actually concur with the principles of that decision." Bound as to the parties and the property "thus settled"; not bound as a rule for voting and legislating.
- **Douglas's judicial supremacy.** The decision, rendered by "the highest judicial tribunal on earth," "becomes the law of the land, binding on you, on me, and on every other good citizen whether we like it or not." "What are important courts worth unless their decisions are binding on all good citizens? It is the fundamental principles of the judiciary that its decisions are final." The Constitution "created the Supreme Court for the purpose of deciding all disputed questions touching the true construction of that instrument."
- **Douglas's strongest point is consistency.** "I want to know whether he is not bound to a decision which is contrary to his opinions just as much as to one in accordance with his opinions." A rule of obedience that applies only to decisions one likes is no rule. Lincoln's answer is that his precedent criteria are neutral: they would apply to a decision he liked that was 7–2, partisan and historically false.
- **Douglas's weakest point is the equation of criticism with mob rule.** "Will he appeal to a mob? Does he intend to appeal to violence, to Lynch law?" Lincoln has already disclaimed resistance to the judgment. Douglas also asserts that "a Senator has no right to interfere with the decision of judicial tribunals," which assumes the conclusion: the question is whether voting for a territorial ban is interference with a judicial decision.
- **Douglas's "no appeal this side of Heaven."** "I know of but one mode of reversing judicial decisions, and that is by appealing from the inferior to the superior court." Lincoln's reply is the other mode: elect officials who will appoint Justices and present the Court with new cases. That is how Dred Scott's territorial holding was in fact reversed in practice (Note 1), before the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments reversed it formally.
- **Note 1: the 1862 territorial ban.** Congress banned slavery in all the territories and Lincoln signed the bill, which Dred Scott had held Congress could not do. Fessenden: "I shall vote on what I understand to be the law of right, and according to what I understand to be in accordance with the Constitution." Was it constitutional? On departmentalist premises, yes if Dred Scott was wrong on the merits, and it was. On Cooper's premises, the statute was void until the Court overruled Dred Scott. The better view: the political branches may legislate against a precedent they believe wrong, knowing a court may refuse to enforce the statute in a litigated case; that is Lincoln's "political rule" distinction in action.
- **Note 2: the Taney bust.** Sumner opposed honoring Taney: "If a man has done evil during life he must not be complimented in marble." Congress withheld the money "for many years." Can Congress use the purse "purely to reject or condemn a Supreme Court opinion"? A bust is a gratuity, not a salary; the Compensation Clause protects judges' pay, not their portraits. Refusing an honor is a legislative judgment Congress is free to make, and it is the mildest form of departmentalism: expressive, with no effect on any judgment.
- **Note 3: Hiram Revels.** Article I, § 3 requires a Senator to have "been nine years a citizen of the United States." Revels was seated in 1870; the Fourteenth Amendment dated only from 1868, and Dred Scott had said African-Americans could not be citizens. Seating him required the Senate to treat Dred Scott's citizenship holding as never having been the law, an act of departmentalism by the body the Constitution makes judge of its members' qualifications (Art. I, § 5). The alternative route is that the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship clause was declaratory of pre-existing citizenship.
- **Where each speaker stands among the three positions.** Douglas: judicial supremacy (opinions bind everyone). Lincoln: departmentalism as to the "political rule," but acceptance of the judgment as to Dred Scott himself (judgment supremacy). Nobody in 1858 argues that the executive may disregard the judgment itself; that question waits for [[Ex parte Merryman]] and [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]] in 1861, where Lincoln's administration goes further.
- **Cooper Note 1 asks the consistency question back.** "Is Douglas's view the same as the Court's in Cooper? Is Lincoln's view that of Governor Faubus?" Lincoln's principle distinguishes Faubus: Faubus used troops to block the enforcement of a federal decree between parties, which Lincoln disclaimed. A departmentalist who respects judgments can condemn Faubus without adopting Douglas.
