## Rule

A tax is "direct," and so must be apportioned, only if apportionment can sensibly be applied to it; on that test the direct taxes are essentially capitations and taxes on land, and a federal tax on carriages may be laid by the rule of uniformity instead.

## Facts

- **The 1794 act laid an annual duty on carriages kept for the conveyance of persons, at a uniform rate across the United States and without apportionment by population.** *The only question submitted was whether the tax was "direct"; if it was, the absence of apportionment was fatal.*
- **Carriages were distributed very unevenly relative to population.** *This is what makes apportionment produce absurd results, and it is the whole of Chase's and Iredell's reasoning.*
- **A state might have no carriages at all.** *"If any state had no carriages, there could be no apportionment at all" (Iredell) — the point that defeats the rule as applied rather than merely making it awkward.*
- **Congress had deliberately treated the carriage tax as a duty rather than a direct tax.** *Chase would defer to "[t]he deliberate decision of the National Legislature" if the question were doubtful; he decides it is not doubtful.*
- **The case arrived on stipulated facts, with Hylton stipulated to own 125 carriages to reach the jurisdictional minimum.** *A contrived test case, which is why Hylton is thin authority on judicial review.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (judgment for the United States; seriatim opinions by Chase, Paterson and Iredell, each upholding the tax; Wilson concurring in the result without a substantive opinion)

- The carriage tax is not a "direct Tax" within Art. I, § 2 and § 9, so it need not be apportioned, and the judgment of the Circuit Court is affirmed.
- **(Chase)** "The rule of apportionment is only to be adopted in such cases where it can reasonably apply; and the subject taxed, must ever determine the application of the rule." A carriage tax "cannot be laid by the rule of apportionment, without very great inequality and injustice."
- **(Chase)** The taxing power is not exhausted by the four named species: "If there are any other species of taxes that are not direct, and not included within the words duties, imposts, or excises, they may be laid by the rule of uniformity, or not; as Congress shall think proper and reasonable." Otherwise "the general power to lay taxes was unnecessary."
- **(Chase)** The direct taxes "are only two, to wit, a capitation, or poll tax, simply, without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance; and a tax on Land" — offered expressly as a view on which he does "not give a judicial opinion."
- **(Paterson)** "Tax" is the genus and divides into three classes: direct taxes; "Duties, imposts, and excises"; and "All other classes of an indirect kind, and not within any of the classifications enumerated under the preceding heads."
- **(Paterson)** "All taxes on expenses or consumption are indirect taxes. A tax on carriages is of this kind, and of course is not a direct tax." Indirect taxes are "circuitous modes of reaching the revenue of individuals, who generally live according to their income," and he closes by reading Adam Smith.
- **(Paterson)** The apportionment rule was aimed at "a capitation tax and a tax on land," and "[t]he provision was made in favor of the southern States," who otherwise "would have been wholly at the mercy of the other states" as to taxes on slaves and on land.
- **(Iredell)** "As all direct taxes must be apportioned, it is evident that the Constitution contemplated none as direct but such as could be apportioned. If this cannot be apportioned, it is, therefore, not a direct tax in the sense of the Constitution."
- **(Iredell)** The arithmetic: at $10 a carriage on 105 carriages nationwide, Virginia's 19/105 share of $190 spread over 50 carriages is "3 dollars, 80 cents" each, while Connecticut's $70 falls on 2 carriages at "35 dollars" each. "This mode is too manifestly absurd to be supported, and has not even been attempted in debate."
- **No opinion holds that the carriage tax is a duty or an excise.** Chase is "inclined to think" it "may be considered as within the power granted to Congress to lay duties"; Paterson says of "duty" and "excise" that "[t]hey present no clear and precise idea to the mind" and rests instead on indirectness; Iredell says there is "no necessity, or propriety, in determining what is or is not, a direct, or indirect, tax in all cases."

**Rejected**

- *A carriage tax is not a duty, impost or excise, and since those are the only indirect taxes the Constitution names, it must be a direct tax and must be apportioned.* Paterson: "[t]he argument on both sides turns in a circle," and the premise is false — the Constitution leaves room for taxes "of an indirect kind" that fall under none of the three named heads, so failing the duty-impost-excise test does not make a tax direct.
- *The rule of apportionment should be favored over the rule of uniformity, so "direct" should be construed broadly.* Paterson: the Constitution "was the work of compromise," and this particular compromise "is radically wrong; it cannot be supported by any solid reasoning. Why should slaves, who are a species of property, be represented more than any other property? The rule, therefore, ought not to be extended by construction." He adds that "numbers do not afford a just estimate or rule of wealth."
- *A tax that falls on a man's own property, which he cannot avoid except by giving up the property, is direct in substance whatever it is called.* Paterson: taxes "on expenses or consumption" are indirect, and in "many cases of this nature the individual may be said to tax himself." Chase reserves the harder version, doubting "whether a tax, by a general assessment of personal property, within the United States, is included within the term direct tax."
- *A tax that is neither direct nor a duty, impost or excise has no constitutional rule at all, so apportionment should supply the default.* Iredell: where the Constitution provides nothing "one way or another," the tax "ought to be uniform; because the present Constitution was particularly intended to affect individuals, and not states, except in particular cases specified: And this is the leading distinction between the articles of Confederation and the present Constitution."

## Context

Hylton is the first case in which the Supreme Court passed on the constitutionality of an act of Congress, seven years before [[Marbury v. Madison]], and it sustained the act — with Alexander Hamilton arguing for the government. As precedent for judicial review it is weak, because the facts were stipulated, the number of carriages was almost certainly invented to reach the jurisdictional amount, and the United States apparently paid the challenger's lawyers. The mechanics it applies are set out in [[Express Limits on the Power to Tax]].

The holding controlled for ninety-nine years and was then contradicted. In [[Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co|Pollock]] (1895), Fuller, C.J., held that "a tax upon a person's entire income—whether derived from rents or products, or otherwise, of real estate, or from bonds, stocks, or other forms of personal property" was direct, read Hamilton to have believed that "all internal taxes, except duties and excises on articles of consumption, fell into the category of direct taxes," distinguished Hylton and remarked that "[t]he case is badly reported." Pollock's structural argument is the mirror image of Paterson's: apportionment protected the states, who "retained the power of direct taxation, and to that they looked as their chief resource," and the founders expected federal revenue to come "for the most part" from indirect taxes. Harlan, dissenting with three others, invoked decades of practice and [[Springer v. United States]] (1881), and charged the majority with favoring capital over "those having incomes derived from the labor of their hands, or the exercise of their skill, or the use of their brains."

The country answered Pollock by amendment rather than by overruling. The **Sixteenth Amendment** (proposed 1909, ratified 1913) removed income taxes from the apportionment rule, which the casebook counts as "the third time in history that the country had reversed a Supreme Court decision with a constitutional amendment." The first two are [[Chisholm v. Georgia]], answered by the Eleventh Amendment, and [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], answered by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments. Dred Scott is the more instructive comparison: the Fourteenth Amendment's first sentence is written to destroy Taney's citizenship holding, just as the Sixteenth is written to destroy Fuller's classification holding, and in both cases Article V, not the Court, did the correcting.

Hylton's definition of "direct" survives in the modern cases. In [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] (Class 23) Roberts relied on Hylton to confine direct taxes to capitations and taxes on property, and noted that Pollock's extension had been undone by the Sixteenth Amendment; the shared responsibility payment was not a capitation because a capitation is paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance," which is Chase's formulation. Post-casebook, in [[Moore v. United States]] (2024) the Court upheld the Mandatory Repatriation Tax 7–2 (Kavanaugh, J., for five) on the narrow ground that the income of an entity may be attributed to its shareholders, expressly declining to decide whether realization is constitutionally required and saying nothing about taxes on wealth or net worth. The direct-tax question the casebook poses — a federal wealth tax — is therefore still open.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Writ of error to the Circuit Court for the District of Virginia, where judgment had gone for the United States on a case stated. The Supreme Court affirmed. "By the case stated, only one question is submitted to the opinion of this court" (Chase).
- Six Justices sat. Three wrote seriatim: Chase, Paterson and Iredell, in that order. Wilson had upheld the tax below on circuit and indicated his views were unchanged without writing again; Cushing did not participate because he had been ill at argument; Ellsworth was sworn in as Chief Justice the morning the decision came down.

### Facts

The act of June 5, 1794, "An act to lay duties upon carriages, for the conveyance of persons," imposed an annual federal duty on carriages at a single rate throughout the United States. It was one of the first internal federal taxes, part of the Hamiltonian revenue program, and like the whiskey excise it fell hardest on particular regions — here on the Virginia planter class, who kept carriages in numbers out of proportion to their population. Daniel Hylton challenged the tax as a direct tax laid without apportionment. The litigation was arranged: the parties stipulated the facts, stipulated that Hylton owned 125 carriages (a figure that conveniently met the $2,000 jurisdictional minimum and was almost certainly false), and the United States appears to have paid Hylton's counsel. Hamilton argued in support of the tax.

### Issue

- Is the federal tax on carriages a "direct Tax" within Art. I, § 2 and § 9, and therefore void because it was laid by the rule of uniformity rather than apportioned among the states according to the census?

### Holding

- No. The tax is not direct, because apportioning a carriage tax would produce "great inequality and injustice" and in some states would be impossible; the apportionment requirement reaches only taxes that can reasonably be apportioned, essentially capitations and taxes on land. The judgment upholding the tax is affirmed.

### Rule

- Whether a tax is "direct" is decided by whether apportionment can reasonably be applied to it, and "the subject taxed, must ever determine the application of the rule" (Chase).
- The direct taxes contemplated by the Constitution are a capitation "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance" and a tax on land (Chase, tentatively; Paterson, as "the principal, I will not say, the only, objects").
- "Tax" is the genus; its species are direct taxes, duties, imposts and excises, and indirect taxes falling outside all of those (Paterson). A tax that is not a duty, impost or excise is therefore not automatically direct.
- "All taxes on expenses or consumption are indirect taxes" (Paterson).
- The apportionment rule is a compromise in favor of the slaveholding states and "ought not to be extended by construction" (Paterson).

### Reasoning

**(Chase, J.)**

- Starts from a burden of proof: counsel "did not satisfy my mind," and "if I only doubted, I should affirm the judgment of the Circuit Court."
- Adds a rule of deference for the doubtful case: Congress "did not consider a tax on carriages a direct tax, but thought it was within the description of a duty," and that "deliberate decision of the National Legislature" would settle a doubtful question.
- Textual argument from surplusage: if the Constitution contemplated only four species of tax, the general power "to lay and collect Taxes" would be "unnecessary," and the framers would have drafted the clause the other way, which he sets out in the words they would have used.
- Functional test: apportionment applies only "where it can reasonably apply," and a carriage tax fails that test.
- Fallback classification: a carriage tax may be a "duty," a term that "is the most comprehensive next to the generical term tax" and in Britain "embraces taxes on stamps, tolls for passage, etc." — the closest any opinion comes to classifying the tax.

**(Paterson, J.)**

- Breaks the circle: counsel on each side argued from the other category's boundaries, and both arguments "turn[] in a circle." The words "duty" and "excise" cannot do the work because they "present no clear and precise idea to the mind."
- Structural premise: "it was . . . obviously the intention of the framers of the Constitution, that Congress should possess full power over every species of taxable property, except exports," and "taxes" was used as a genus "to vest in Congress plenary authority in all cases of taxation."
- Original purpose, stated frankly: the apportionment clause protected the southern states, who had "a large number of slaves" and "extensive tracts of territory, thinly settled, and not very productive," from a Congress that "might tax slaves, at discretion or arbitrarily, and land in every part of the Union after the same rate or measure."
- Normative move: because the clause is a bargain and "radically wrong," it gets no expansive construction. Numbers are "a very uncertain and incompetent sign of opulence," so apportionment does not even serve its supposed purpose.
- Economic classification: consumption and expenditure taxes are indirect; the taxpayer in effect taxes himself by choosing to spend. He supports this with Adam Smith on the impossibility of taxing revenue by capitation.

**(Iredell, J.)**

- Deduces the definition from the remedy: because "all direct taxes must be apportioned," only what can be apportioned can be direct. The consequence, not the nature of the subject, fixes the category.
- Works the arithmetic to a reductio: identical carriages would pay $3.80 in Virginia and $35 in Connecticut, and a state with no carriages could not be apportioned at all.
- Fills the gap: if a tax is neither direct nor a duty, impost or excise, the Constitution says nothing, and uniformity supplies the default, because the Constitution "was particularly intended to affect individuals, and not states" — "the leading distinction between the articles of Confederation and the present Constitution."
- Refuses a general rule: "There is no necessity, or propriety, in determining what is or is not, a direct, or indirect, tax in all cases."

### Dissent / concurrence

- There is no dissent. All three writing Justices upheld the tax, and Wilson, who had upheld it on circuit, agreed without writing. The opinions are seriatim rather than a single opinion of the Court, which is the practice Marshall abandoned a few years later.
- The disagreement that matters is among the majority. Chase's test is reasonableness of apportionment plus deference to Congress; Paterson's is a taxonomy of taxes grounded in the Convention's purpose and in political economy; Iredell's is the pure impossibility argument, with an express refusal to say anything more than the case requires.

### Cold-call notes

- **What is the test for a "direct Tax" after Hylton?** Not a definition but a filter: a tax is direct only if apportionment can reasonably be applied to it. Chase and Iredell both reason from the consequences of apportionment back to the classification. Paterson alone offers an affirmative account — consumption and expenditure taxes are indirect — and that account is doing independent work.
- **Is the reasonableness test circular? (Note 2)** It looks circular, because the constitutional consequence is being used to fix the constitutional category. The defense: the apportionment clause is a rule about how certain taxes must be collected, and a rule that cannot be complied with for a given subject was evidently not meant to cover it. The problem the note presses is that apportioning a **land** tax is nearly as unfair as apportioning a carriage tax — land is also distributed unevenly relative to population, so an acre in thinly settled Virginia and an acre in crowded Connecticut would pay very different rates — and yet all three Justices agree a land tax is direct. The reductio proves too much.
- **Why isn't a carriage tax a land tax in miniature?** Because Paterson classes it as a tax on expense: you own a carriage by choosing to spend on one, and you can stop. Land in 1796 was the thing everyone had to hold to live on. That is the honest distinction, and it is economic rather than textual.
- **What falls in the gap? (Note 3)** Paterson's third class: taxes that are neither direct nor duties, imposts or excises. Iredell says the Constitution supplies no rule for them and uniformity should apply by default. The framers may have wanted the gap because they wanted the taxing power to be plenary and were legislating two specific protections — apportionment for the slave states, uniformity against interstate favoritism — rather than a complete taxonomy.
- **Different methods, same result (Note 1).** Chase: text, structure, congressional practice. Paterson: original purpose (what the Convention was doing for the southern states), structure, and political economy. Iredell: consequences and a comparison with the Articles of Confederation. The core of agreement is narrow — capitations and land taxes are direct, carriage taxes are not — and everything beyond it is dictum. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Paterson says out loud what the clause was for.** "The provision was made in favor of the southern States." Apportionment and the three-fifths rule are two halves of the same bargain: representation and direct taxation are measured by the same number so that inflating the slave count for one purpose raises the tax bill for the other. Being candid about this lets Paterson argue that the clause is "radically wrong" and deserves a narrow reading — an originalist argument used to *shrink* a provision. Compare [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], where the same candor about the founding's treatment of slaves produces the opposite conclusion.
- **Why is apportionment unworkable for anything but capitations and land?** Apportionment fixes each state's total share by population, so the rate must then be adjusted state by state to hit that total. Any base distributed unevenly relative to population produces wildly different rates, and a base absent from a state produces an impossible one. A capitation escapes this because its base *is* population. A land tax escapes it only in the loose sense that every state has land; the rates still differ.
- **Hypo: Congress taxes all carriages at a flat rate, but only in the five states where carriages are common.** The uniformity rule ("uniform throughout the United States") now fails on its own terms, whatever the tax is called. Geographic uniformity is the limit that survives a holding that the tax is indirect.
- **Hypo: a federal tax of 2 percent on the net worth of every individual above $50 million.** Not an income tax, so the Sixteenth Amendment does not help. Under Hylton it is direct only if apportionment can reasonably apply, and it cannot — wealth is concentrated in a handful of states, so apportionment would mean crushing rates in poor states. Under Pollock's reasoning it is plainly direct: a tax on property "merely because of ownership." This is the casebook's own hypothetical, and the two cases give opposite answers. [[Moore v. United States]] (2024) left it open.
- **Hypo: a tax on the ownership of a carriage, measured by its value rather than a flat sum.** Now it looks like Chase's reserved case — "a general assessment of personal property." Chase doubted, and Pollock later held such taxes direct. Say which way you would go and why the measure of the tax should or should not change its character.
- **Why does the first exercise of judicial review upholding a statute matter?** Because [[Marbury v. Madison]] is usually taught as the origin of the power. Hylton shows the Court reviewing an act of Congress for constitutionality in 1796 without anyone treating the power as controversial — but also shows it in a collusive case, so the practice it establishes is not a clean one.
- **The reported text is unreliable.** Dallas's report is short, and Pollock said so: "[t]he case is badly reported." Be ready to say that Hylton's authority is limited by what the Justices actually said, that Chase disclaimed giving "a judicial opinion" on the two-category rule, and that Iredell refused to state any general rule at all.
- **Memorable details.** Hamilton argued for the tax. The 125 stipulated carriages. Ellsworth sworn in the morning the decision was announced. Iredell's $3.80 against $35.
