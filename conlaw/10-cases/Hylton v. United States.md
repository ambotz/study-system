> [!note] Quotation — Paterson's circular argument reads "it is not tax, and therefore must be a duty or excise" in both editions (5th ed. p. 599). The wording is the casebook's own and is not an extraction error, although the sense requires *it is not a direct tax*. Quote the sentence as printed.

## Snapshot

Congress laid an annual duty on carriages at one rate throughout the country, unapportioned by population. Hylton, in a test case on stipulated facts, argued that the tax was "direct" and void for want of apportionment. On a writ of error the Court affirmed, three Justices writing seriatim: a tax is direct only if apportionment can reasonably apply to it, and a carriage tax cannot be apportioned without absurdity. Article I's apportionment rule meets the uniformity rule of § 8.

## Issue

The casebook frames the unit around the label's consequence: "it can be very important to figure out what makes a tax 'direct' in the constitutional sense, since this determines whether the apportionment requirement applies." Hylton answers it for a tax on a thing people own.

- Is the carriage tax a "direct Tax" within Article I, § 2 and § 9, and so void for want of apportionment?

## Rule

Whether a tax is "direct" turns on whether apportionment can sensibly be applied to it; on that test the direct taxes are capitations and taxes on land, and a carriage tax may be laid by uniformity. "Tax" is the genus, so a tax that is not a duty, impost or excise is not thereby direct.

- "The rule of apportionment is only to be adopted in such cases where it can reasonably apply; and the subject taxed, must ever determine the application of the rule" (Chase).
- "As all direct taxes must be apportioned, it is evident that the Constitution contemplated none as direct but such as could be apportioned" (Iredell).
- Direct taxes "are only two . . . a capitation, or poll tax . . . and a tax on Land" (Chase).
- "All taxes on expenses or consumption are indirect taxes. A tax on carriages is of this kind" (Paterson).

## Operative facts

- **The apportionment clause was written to shield the southern states, which held "a large number of slaves" and "extensive tracts of territory, thinly settled, and not very productive," from a Congress free to tax slaves and land at any rate.** *Paterson says so out loud, which licenses his narrow reading.*
- **The act of June 5, 1794, "An act to lay duties upon carriages, for the conveyance of persons," imposed an annual federal duty on carriages at a single rate throughout the United States and without apportionment.** *An early internal federal tax, part of the Hamiltonian revenue program; the only question submitted was whether it was "direct," and if so the want of apportionment was fatal.*
- **Carriages were distributed very unevenly relative to population, falling hardest on the Virginia planter class.** *This makes apportionment absurd, and it is the whole of Chase's and Iredell's reasoning.*
- **A state might have no carriages at all.** *"If any state had no carriages, there could be no apportionment at all" (Iredell) — which defeats the rule rather than merely making it awkward.*
- **Congress deliberately treated the carriage tax as a duty rather than as a direct tax.** *Chase would defer to that "deliberate decision" in a doubtful case; he decides it is not doubtful.*
- **Daniel Hylton challenged the tax on stipulated facts, including that he owned 125 carriages — a figure that met the $2,000 jurisdictional minimum exactly and was almost certainly false.** *A contrived test case, which is why Hylton is thin authority on judicial review.*
- **The United States appears to have paid Hylton's counsel, and Alexander Hamilton argued in support of the tax.** *No genuinely adverse party, so nothing was tested by opposition.*
- **Judgment went for the United States on a case stated in the Circuit Court for the District of Virginia, and the case came up on a writ of error.** *"By the case stated, only one question is submitted to the opinion of this court" (Chase).*
- **Of the six sitting Justices, Chase, Paterson and Iredell wrote seriatim; Wilson had upheld the tax below and said his views were unchanged; Cushing had been ill at argument; and Ellsworth was sworn in as Chief Justice the morning of the decision.** *Three rationales and no opinion of the Court, so the holding is only as wide as their overlap.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (judgment for the United States; seriatim opinions by Chase, Paterson and Iredell, each upholding the tax; Wilson concurring in the result without a substantive opinion; Cushing took no part)

- The carriage tax is not a "direct Tax" within Article I, § 2 and § 9, so it need not be apportioned, and the judgment of the Circuit Court is affirmed.
- No opinion holds that the tax is a duty or an excise. Chase "think[s]" it "may be considered as within the power granted to Congress to lay duties," while Paterson says "duty" and "excise" "present no clear and precise idea to the mind" and Iredell sees no "propriety, in determining what is or is not, a direct, or indirect, tax in all cases."

**Chase, J.**

- Begins from a burden of proof — counsel "did not satisfy my mind," and "if I only doubted, I should affirm" — then adds that in a doubtful case the "deliberate decision of the National Legislature" settles it.
- Argues from surplusage: on a closed list of four species the general power "to lay and collect Taxes" would be "unnecessary," so other taxes "may be laid by the rule of uniformity, or not."
- Applies the functional test: a carriage tax "cannot be laid by the rule of apportionment, without very great inequality and injustice."

**Paterson, J.**

- Breaks the circle. Each side argued from the other category's boundaries, so "[t]he argument on both sides turns in a circle," and neither "duty" nor "excise" can carry the weight.
- Sets the taxonomy: direct taxes; "Duties, imposts, and excises"; and "All other classes of an indirect kind, and not within any of the classifications enumerated under the preceding heads."
- Reads the clause's purpose frankly and then holds it to that purpose, the compromise being "radically wrong," and classifies consumption taxes as "circuitous modes of reaching the revenue of individuals, who generally live according to their income."

**Iredell, J.**

- Deduces the definition from the remedy: because all direct taxes must be apportioned, only what can be apportioned is direct, so the consequence rather than the subject fixes the category.
- Works the arithmetic to a reductio. Identical carriages would pay "3 dollars, 80 cents" in Virginia and "35 dollars" in Connecticut, which is "too manifestly absurd to be supported."
- Fills the gap. Where the Constitution provides nothing "one way or another," uniformity is the default, because it "was particularly intended to affect individuals, and not states."

## Arguments rejected

- *A carriage tax is not a duty, impost or excise, and those are the only indirect taxes the Constitution names, so it must be direct.* Paterson: the premise is false, because the Constitution leaves room for taxes "of an indirect kind" under none of the named heads.
- *Apportionment should be favored over uniformity, so "direct" should be construed broadly.* Paterson: this compromise "is radically wrong," and "[t]he rule, therefore, ought not to be extended by construction."
- *A tax on a man's own property, unavoidable except by giving the property up, is direct in substance whatever it is called.* Paterson: taxes "on expenses or consumption" are indirect, and "the individual may be said to tax himself." Chase reserves the harder case, doubting whether "a general assessment of personal property" is direct.
- *A tax in neither category has no constitutional rule, so apportionment supplies the default.* Iredell: uniformity is the default, because the Constitution was "intended to affect individuals, and not states."

## Where it sits

**The first review of a federal statute, and a weak precedent for it.** Hylton is the first case in which the Court passed on an act of Congress, seven years before [[Marbury v. Madison]], and it sustained the act with Hamilton arguing for the government. Stipulated facts, an invented carriage count and a government-paid challenger make it an unclean precedent. The mechanics are in [[Express Limits on the Power to Tax]].

**Ninety-nine years, then contradiction.** In *Pollock* (1895) Fuller, C.J., held a tax on "a person's entire income" from real or personal property direct, distinguished Hylton and remarked that "[t]he case is badly reported." His structural argument mirrors Paterson's, and Harlan dissented for four.

**Amendment rather than overruling.** The Sixteenth Amendment (1913) removed income taxes from the apportionment rule — "the third time in history that the country had reversed a Supreme Court decision with a constitutional amendment," after *Chisholm v. Georgia* and [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]. Article V did the correcting.

**The modern line.** [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] reads Hylton as confining direct taxes to capitations and land taxes, and the shared responsibility payment was no capitation, which falls "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance."

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Consider the similarities and differences between the three opinions. . . . Do they use the same methods of constitutional argument to get there?"**

- The core is narrow: capitations and land taxes are direct, carriage taxes are not, and everything past that is dictum.
- The methods diverge — Chase from text and practice, Paterson from original purpose and political economy, Iredell from consequences. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].

**Note 2. "What do you make of the argument that a tax should be direct only if it could reasonably be apportioned? Is that common sense, or circular? . . . How would a tax on land be apportioned more fairly than a tax on carriages?"**

- Circular on its face, since the consequence fixes the category; the defense is that apportionment is a rule of collection never meant to reach a subject it cannot fit.
- Land is where the note bites. It too is unevenly distributed relative to population, yet all three Justices call a land tax direct, so the reductio proves too much.

**Note 3. "Are all federal taxes subject to either uniformity or apportionment? . . . what falls in the gap between them? Why might the framers . . . have wanted for some taxes to be subject to neither?"**

- Paterson's third class of taxes "of an indirect kind" is the gap, and Iredell makes uniformity the default.
- The framers likely wanted a plenary taxing power, with two targeted protections rather than a taxonomy.

**Note 4. "The line between direct and indirect taxes remains elusive."**

- Hylton "became an early precedent establishing broad national power to impose unapportioned taxes on commodities, consumption, and conduct," and it held until Pollock struck the income tax as unseverable.
- Pollock distinguishes Hylton rather than overruling it. The line stays elusive because Chase and Iredell supply a filter, not a definition.

**Note 5. "Does the Sixteenth Amendment moot the question of which taxes are 'direct'? . . . Suppose Congress were to adopt a tax on all forms of wealth . . . . What is the best answer?"**

- It moots only income, which is why [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] still had to ask whether the shared responsibility payment was a capitation. The amendment reads as a concession to Pollock, not a correction of it.
- A wealth tax splits the opinions: grotesque to apportion under Chase and Iredell, and so indirect; the paradigm direct tax under Paterson's account. *Moore* (2024) left it open.

### Further drilling

- **Vary the measure.** A carriage tax assessed on value is Chase's reserved case of "a general assessment of personal property," which Pollock later held direct.
- **Why apportionment fits only capitations.** It fixes each state's total by population, so rates must move state by state; an unevenly spread base yields wild rates, an absent one an impossible rate.
- **Corpus linguistics.** A 5th-edition note argues the direct-indirect line "was much less elusive than modern readers believe." Usage frequency evidences ordinary meaning, not a term of art.
- **Memorable details.** Hamilton argued for the tax. The 125 stipulated carriages. Iredell's $3.80 against $35.
