## Rule

When war is made on the United States, by foreign invasion or by organized rebellion, the President "is not only authorized but bound to resist force by force" without waiting for Congress, and his judgment that a state of war exists binds the courts. A war may exist in fact, and carry the full rights of war under the law of nations, without any declaration.

## Facts

- **The South attacked Fort Sumter, and war came before Congress acted.** *The war was "thrust" on the government; had Lincoln begun it, Grier's premise ("He has no power to initiate or declare a war") would have cut the other way.*
- **Lincoln proclaimed the blockade while Congress was out of session.** *Congress had neither authorized nor forbidden it: category two, not three.*
- **Statutes of 1795 and 1807 authorized force to repel invasion and suppress insurrection.** *For Grier, standing authority; for Nelson, they "did not, and could not under the Constitution," let the President declare war.*
- **Congress later ratified everything, and the Act of July 13, 1861 authorized captures.** *Grier treats ratification as a fallback; Nelson refuses to make it retroactive.*
- **All four vessels were captured before July 13, 1861; two were foreign and two American.** *Under Nelson's rule each is "illegal and void"; neutrals challenged the blockade, domestic owners the "enemies' property" label.*
- **Congress never declared war on the Confederacy.** *Grier says Congress "cannot declare war against a State," so a declaration cannot be the test for civil war.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (5–4; Grier for the Court; Nelson, with Taney, C.J., Catron and Clifford, dissenting)

- **The blockade was lawful.** A civil war "is never solemnly declared; it becomes such by its accidents." When organized rebels hold territory, raise armies and commence hostilities, the contest is a war, and "the President had a right, jure belli, to institute a blockade of ports in possession of the States in rebellion, which neutrals are bound to regard."
- **The President decides whether the resistance amounts to war.** Whether the Commander in Chief faces a civil war of such proportions as to accord the rebels belligerent character "is a question to be decided by him," and "this Court must be governed by the decisions and acts of the political department." The blockade proclamation is "official and conclusive evidence" of the state of war.
- **Congress ratified in any event.** If legislative sanction were necessary, it is found in the acts of the 1861 special session and in the act approving the President's acts as if done under "previous express authority."
- **Property of residents of rebel territory is enemies' property.** Liability to capture "does not in any manner depend on the personal allegiance of the owner"; "It is the illegal traffic that stamps it as 'enemies' property.'" Residents of the territory "are none the less enemies because they are traitors."

**Rejected**

- *Only Congress can put the nation in a state of war; until it acts, the country is legally at peace, and a blockade (a belligerent right) is unavailable to the President.* Grier: war is a fact, not a legal status conferred by declaration. "The President was bound to meet it in the shape it presented itself, without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name; and no name given to it by him or them could change the fact."
- *The government called the conflict an "insurrection" and refused to recognize the Confederacy, so it cannot claim belligerent rights.* Grier: the law of nations contains "no such anomalous doctrine" that rebels "are not enemies because they are traitors." The sovereign "may exercise both belligerent and sovereign rights."
- *Southern residents remain citizens owed the protection of municipal law; their property can be confiscated only after conviction for an offense.* Grier: the argument would make the ocean commerce that funds the rebellion immune from capture because it is "'unconstitutional!!!'" Capture at sea is one of "the milder modes of coercion" the law of war allows, and the owner "pro hac vice, is an enemy."
- *Many Southerners are loyal and were coerced, as Lincoln's own proclamation admits.* Answered by the territorial rule: residence and trade within the line "marked by lines of bayonets," not personal loyalty, determines enemy character.

## Context

The Prize Cases are the principal Supreme Court decision on who may begin a war, and they are the source of the defensive-war doctrine that runs from Madison's "repel sudden attacks" in [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]] to WPR § 2(c)(3) ("a national emergency created by attack upon the United States . . . or its armed forces"). They reach that result by treating the defensive power as a power to respond, not to initiate; every later claim of unilateral presidential power (Korea, Kosovo, Libya, Syria) tries to stretch the response category.

**Youngstown mapping.** Read with [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]], the case sits in category two or one, never three:

- Ex ante, Congress was absent and silent: category two, where the result turns on the imperatives of events, and the event here was an attack.
- The 1795 and 1807 Acts arguably authorized force against insurrection in advance: category one.
- The 1861 ratification moved the whole operation into category one after the fact.
- Jackson's contrast: the Commander in Chief's widest latitude is force turned against an enemy. The blockade was force turned outward against a belligerent, even though the belligerent was domestic; Truman's seizure was force turned inward against American property owners in a labor dispute. The case is also a warning against over-reading that line, since the Prize Cases sustained the capture of Americans' property.

The **earlier cases in Note 4** point the other way, toward congressional control: [[Talbot v. Seeman]] (1801) ("The whole powers of war being . . . vested in congress"), [[Bas v. Tingy]] (1800) (the Quasi-War with France governed by Congress's enactments), [[Little v. Barreme]] (1804) (capture rules set by statute override presidential orders), and [[Brown v. United States]] (1814) (confiscation of enemy property requires statute). [[Durand v. Hollins]] (1860) is the outlier sustaining a unilateral bombardment. Little v. Barreme is also Clark's authority in Youngstown: where Congress has prescribed the method, the President must follow it.

The **Nelson dissent** is the formalist pole of the war-powers unit, as Black's opinion is in Youngstown. It returns in the WPR's insistence on "a declaration of war" or "specific statutory authorization," and in the concurrent-resolution problem after [[INS v. Chadha]].

Lincoln's other unilateral acts of spring 1861, the call for volunteers and the suspension of habeas corpus, are the subject of [[Ex parte Merryman]] (Class 13), and the war-measure theory is extended to emancipation in [[The Emancipation Proclamation]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Prize proceedings: the four vessels and cargoes were captured for breach of blockade or as enemies' property, and decrees of condemnation were entered. The owners appealed.
- The Supreme Court affirmed the condemnations, 5–4. The casebook omits Part III, which applies the principles to each vessel.

### Facts

After the attack on Fort Sumter in 1861, Lincoln proclaimed a blockade of Southern ports; under the law of nations, a vessel breaching a blockade could be captured and condemned as prize. Four seizures reached the Court: the Amy Warwick, carrying coffee from Rio de Janeiro to Hampton Roads, whose commander "was ignorant of the war"; the Crenshaw and the British Hiawatha, each carrying Virginia tobacco to Liverpool; and the Brilliante, a Mexican schooner carrying flour from New Orleans to the Yucatán. Congress met in special session on July 4, 1861, and was "wholly employed in enacting laws to enable the Government to prosecute the war." Grier defines war as "That state in which a nation prosecutes its right by force," and relies on the Mexican War, where battles were fought before Congress recognized "a state of war as existing by the act of the Republic of Mexico."

### Issue

- Did the President have power, before Congress acted, to institute a blockade of ports held by the rebellion, which neutrals were bound to respect?
- Was property of persons residing in the rebel states subject to capture at sea as "enemies' property"?

### Holding

- Yes. A civil war existed as a matter of fact once the rebellion held territory and commenced hostilities; the President, bound to resist force by force, could exercise the belligerent right of blockade, and his determination that war existed binds the Court. Congress's 1861 legislation supplies sanction if any were needed.
- Yes. Enemy character at sea turns on residence in and trade with hostile territory, not on the owner's personal allegiance, so residents of the rebel states are enemies "though not foreigners."

### Rule

- The President "has no power to initiate or declare a war," but if war is made by invasion or organized rebellion he is "bound to accept the challenge without waiting for any special legislative authority."
- War may exist "without a declaration on either side"; a civil war "is never solemnly declared."
- Whether the resistance has reached the level of war is "a question to be decided by him" (the President), and the courts defer to "the political department."
- Congress may ratify presidential war measures after the fact, "as if" done under prior authority.
- A sovereign facing civil war "may exercise both belligerent and sovereign rights."

### Reasoning

**(Grier, J., for the Court)**

- Neutrals may challenge a blockade, but must respect one where "a war must exist de facto" and they have notice.
- War is defined by facts: territory held, independence declared, allegiance cast off, armies organized, hostilities commenced. Foreign nations acknowledged the war by declaring neutrality.
- Congress "alone has the power to declare a national or foreign war," but cannot declare war on a State; the President cannot initiate war; the Militia Acts of 1795 and 1807 authorize him to use force against invasion and insurrection.
- Once war is made on the nation, the President "must determine what degree of force the crisis demands." The rebellion sprang forth "a Minerva in the full panoply of war."
- The Mexican War precedent: Congress's 1846 act was "a vindication and ratification" of the President's response.
- Congress's 1861 acts, passed "ex major cautela," would supply legislative sanction if needed.
- On enemy property: the claimants' theory lets the rebels exercise belligerent rights while denying them to the government. Under "the very peculiar Constitution of this Government," citizens owe a qualified allegiance to their State, and the seceding States acted as States; south of the line "is enemies' territory."

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Nelson, J., with Taney, C.J., and Catron and Clifford, JJ., dissenting)**

- A state of war is a legal status, not merely a fact. Its consequences are sweeping: all commercial intercourse becomes unlawful, contracts are suspended, all property of the enemy people becomes capturable, treaties are annulled. Because of those consequences, "the right of making war belongs exclusively to the supreme or sovereign power of the State."
- Quoting Kent: "as war cannot lawfully be commenced on the part of the United States without an act of Congress, such act is, of course, a formal notice to all the world."
- A civil war can carry belligerent rights, but only once it is "recognized or declared by the war-making power of the Government." "There is no difference in this respect between a civil or a public war."
- The Militia Acts could not confer on the President "the power of declaring war against a State of this Union, or of deciding that war existed." That power "is reserved to the legislative department by the express words of the Constitution. It cannot be delegated or surrendered to the Executive."
- Until Congress acts, no citizen can be punished in person or property except for an offense against a prior law of Congress. "The penalty of confiscation for the acts of others with which he had no concern cannot lawfully be inflicted."
- The Act of July 13, 1861 "recognized a state of civil war" and made it territorial, like the Act of Parliament of 1776 against the colonies. Captures before that date are "illegal and void."

### Cold-call notes

- **What divides the majority and the dissent?** Note 1. Whether congressional action is required before the President may exercise belligerent rights. Grier: no, war is a fact the President must meet. Nelson: yes, war as a legal condition exists only when the war-making power says so. Both agree the President may use force to suppress insurrection; they disagree about the legal consequences of that force for property.
- **What is the third position?** Note 1. That Lincoln's actions were forbidden even with congressional authorization. The Confederate version: Congress cannot make war on a State (Grier concedes Congress "cannot declare war against a State"), and coercing a State is beyond federal power. Nobody on the Court takes it because it presupposes the legality of secession.
- **Was Lincoln inconsistent?** Note 2. Lincoln insisted the conflict was a rebellion, not a war, and would not recognize the Confederacy, yet claimed the belligerent right of blockade. Grier's answer is that the sovereign has both belligerent and sovereign rights; it can treat rebels as enemies at sea and as traitors at home. The inconsistency is a feature of civil war, not a constitutional defect.
- **When can the President use force without Congress?** Note 3. On the Prize Cases, when force is made against the nation, by invasion or organized rebellion.:
  - A foreign attack on U.S. territory: same result, and more easily.
  - An attack on U.S. forces abroad: probably the same (WPR § 2(c)(3) agrees), though the Prize Cases involved attack at home.
  - No attack, but a threat the President judges grave: outside the holding. "He has no power to initiate" is Grier's line, and the OLC "national interest" theory in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] needs more than this case gives.
  - Congress in session and refusing to act: category three; the case would be much harder, and Nelson's view gains force.
- **Why isn't this category three?** Congress had not spoken against the blockade; it was out of session and then ratified. Contrast Youngstown, where Congress had rejected seizure in 1947.
- **Is ratification enough to cure a capture made before authority existed?** Grier uses it only as a fallback. Nelson implicitly says no: confiscation for acts before the law existed would be punishment without prior law. Be ready to argue that retroactive authorization of property forfeiture raises an ex post facto or due process problem even if it cures the separation-of- powers problem.
- **How far does "a question to be decided by him" go?** It is deference on whether the facts amount to war, not a holding that the President decides what war powers he has. Keep this separate from political-question arguments in the Vietnam and Gulf War cases in [[Declarations of War]] and [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]].
- **Formalism and functionalism.** Nelson is formalist: the Constitution assigns the change from peace to war to Congress, and nothing else will do. Grier is functional and fact-based: the constitutional question follows the real world. The same split recurs in the unit between the WPR's categories and OLC's "nature, scope and duration" test.
- **Original meaning check.** Grier's rule matches the Convention carve-out: "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks." Nelson's rule matches Mason's "clogging rather than facilitating war." Both are faithful to part of the debate; the majority's version has the better claim because the carve-out was the stated purpose of the "declare" amendment.
- **Memorable lines.** "A civil war is never solemnly declared; it becomes such by its accidents." "Without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name." "A Minerva in the full panoply of war." "None the less enemies because they are traitors." "'Unconstitutional!!!'"
