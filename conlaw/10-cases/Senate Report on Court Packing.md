## What it is

The Senate Judiciary Committee's adverse report on President Franklin Roosevelt's 1937 bill to reorganize the federal judiciary (S. 1392), Reorganization of the Federal Judiciary, S. Rep. No. 75-711 (1937), reporting the bill back "with the recommendation that it do not pass." The identification is presumed from the syllabus entry "(canvas) Senate Report" and should be confirmed against the Canvas packet, which may excerpt the report. Roosevelt announced the plan on February 5, 1937, after the Court had struck down much of the early New Deal: for each Justice over 70 who did not retire, the President could appoint an additional Justice, up to six, for a Court of as many as fifteen. The Senate then had 76 Democrats out of 96, and the committee had a Democratic majority, yet it voted 10–8 to report the bill adversely; on July 22, 1937, the Senate recommitted the bill, 70–20. The quotations below are from the report as reproduced by Teaching American History and are pending a check against the printed report.

## What to notice

- **The verdict.** "We recommend the rejection of this bill as a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle." "It is a measure which should be so emphatically rejected that its parallel will never again be presented to the free representatives of the free people of America."
- **The mechanism, and why the committee called it a disguise.** "The bill does not provide for any increase of personnel unless judges of retirement age fail to resign or retire." The report then takes the announced purposes one by one: "It would not banish age from the bench nor abolish divided decisions." "It would not reduce the expense of litigation nor speed the decision of cases." "It is a proposal without precedent and without justification."
- **The purpose is the objection.** Relying on the President's own March 9 fireside chat, the report finds "the frank acknowledgment that neither speed nor 'new blood' in the judiciary is the object of this legislation," but "a change in the decisions of the Court." The bill would "increase the number of Justices for the express purpose of neutralizing the views of some of the present members" (as quoted by the Heritage Foundation). This is the key move: Congress has undoubted power to set the Court's size, so the objection has to be to purpose, the very inquiry [[Ex parte McCardle]] says a court will not make.
- **The principle at stake.** "This amounts to nothing more than the declaration that when the Court stands in the way of a legislative enactment, the Congress may reverse the ruling by enlarging the Court." "It would subjugate the courts to the will of Congress and the president and thereby destroy the independence of the judiciary." "Its ultimate operation would be to make this government one of men rather than one of law."
- **Independence as the premise.** "It is essential to the continuance of our constitutional democracy that the judiciary be completely independent of both the executive and legislative branches." "Independent courts are the last safeguard of the citizen." Compare [[The Federalist No. 78|Federalist No. 78]] on the judiciary as the "least dangerous" branch that needs permanent tenure to resist the others.
- **The argument from reciprocity.** "If we yield to temptation now to lay the lash upon the Court, we are only teaching others how to apply it to ourselves and to the people." The Democratic majority is voting against its own President partly because the next majority would use the same tool.
- **The remedy is amendment or patience, not statute.** "The way to achieve that result is by constitutional amendment fixing definite terms for the members of the judiciary." "Even if every charge brought against the so-called 'reactionary' members of this Court be true, it is far better that we await orderly but inevitable change of personnel than that we impatiently overwhelm them with new members" (as quoted by the Heritage Foundation).
- **Why it failed even with a supermajority.** The report is the institutional reason; the political reasons were the Court's own shift. [[West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish]] (March 29, 1937) upheld a state minimum wage law, the Court then upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security, Justice Van Devanter announced his retirement on the day of the committee vote (May 18, 1937), and Majority Leader Joseph Robinson, the bill's floor manager, died on July 14. The "switch in time" took away the emergency, and a vacancy took away the need.
- **Constitutional or merely political norm?** The Court's size is set by statute and has changed seven times (six in 1789; five under the 1801 Act, never taking effect; back to six in 1802; seven in 1807; nine in 1837; ten in 1863; seven by attrition in 1866; nine in 1869). Nothing in Art. III fixes nine. The case for a constitutional norm is structural: a Court enlarged to change its answers is not independent in the sense Art. III tenure and salary protections assume. The case for a political norm is textual and historical: several changes (1801–02, 1866, 1869) had partisan motives, and the power over size is plainly Congress's. The report's language ("abandonment of constitutional principle") claims the first; its own remedy (amendment for term limits) and the fact that the check is enforced only by the Senate's vote support the second.
- **Hypotheticals.**
  - Congress adds two seats to handle a genuine caseload rise, with no link to any decision: within the power; the report's objection is to purpose.
  - Congress adds seats the day after a decision striking down its statute, openly to reverse it: the report's paradigm case; still no judicial remedy, because courts do not review motive (compare [[Ex parte McCardle]]).
  - Congress shrinks the Court to block a President's appointments (1866): same power, reverse direction; the report's reciprocity argument applies with equal force.
  - Term limits by statute (rotating Justices to lower courts): arguably consistent with "good Behaviour" tenure because the judge keeps an Art. III office; the report itself assumed an amendment would be required.
- **Departmentalism from Congress's side.** Jefferson and Lincoln ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]], [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]) argued that the political branches need not accept the Court's constitutional views as final; Roosevelt's plan tried to act on that view by changing the Court rather than defying it. The report rejects the method without rejecting judicial review, and it stands with [[Cooper v. Aaron]]'s premise that the Court's constitutional judgments must not be overridden by ordinary legislation.
