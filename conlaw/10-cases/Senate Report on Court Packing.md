## What it is

The 1937 court-packing episode in three documents: President Roosevelt's two public cases for the Judiciary Reorganization Bill (S. 1392) — the **Letter to Congress** of February 9 and the **Fireside Chat** of March 9 — and the **Senate Judiciary Committee's Adverse Report** of June 14, which reported the bill back "with the recommendation that it do not pass." The bill would have let the President appoint an additional Justice for each sitting Justice over 70 who did not retire, up to a Court of fifteen. Roosevelt proposed it after the Court had struck down much of the early New Deal; the Senate then held a large Democratic majority, yet the committee — with its own Democratic majority — buried the bill. The three documents are a single argument running in sequence: the efficiency case, the candid admission that the real target is the Court's decisions, and the institutional rebuttal that fastens on exactly that admission. This is the Class 15 companion on the Court's **size**, read with [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (removing Justices) and [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] (jurisdiction).

## What to notice

### Roosevelt's Letter to Congress (Feb. 9, 1937) — the efficiency case

- **The pitch is caseload, not ideology.** Roosevelt recommends reorganizing the Judiciary so it may "function in accord with modern necessities," resting on the President's duty to recommend measures and Congress's "direct responsibility in the creation of courts." The stated problem: "the personnel of the Federal Judiciary is insufficient to meet the business before them," with citizens complaining "of the complexities, the delays, and the expense of litigation." The age-tied appointment mechanism is dressed as relief for an overworked, aging bench.
- **Size has always been Congress's to set — the letter's precedent list.** "The Supreme Court was established with six members in 1789; it was reduced to five in 1801; it was increased to seven in 1807; it was increased to nine in 1837; it was increased to ten in 1863; it was reduced to seven in 1866; it was increased to nine in 1869." This recitation is Roosevelt's strongest card, and the Senate report answers it directly.

### Roosevelt's Fireside Chat (Mar. 9, 1937) — the candor that sank it

- **The three-horse team.** Government is "a three horse team provided by the Constitution" — Congress, Executive, Courts; "Two of the horses are pulling in unison today; the third is not," and "It is the American people themselves who are in the driver's seat." The President casts himself as one of the horses, not the driver.
- **The real objection drops out here.** "The Court... has improperly set itself up as a third house of the Congress — a super-legislature... reading into the Constitution words and implications which are not there." "We have... reached the point... where we must take action to save the Constitution from the Court and the Court from itself. We must find a way to take an appeal from the Supreme Court to the Constitution itself." This is Roosevelt admitting the bill is about the Court's *decisions* — the sentence the Senate report will quote back.
- **The age rationale, restated.** He proposes "only the addition of Justices... in accordance with a clearly defined principle relating to a clearly defined age limit," citing a Court in which "five Justices will be over seventy-five years of age before next June." And "Is it a dangerous precedent for the Congress to change the number of the Justices? The Congress has always had, and will have, that power" — changed under Adams, Jefferson, Jackson, Lincoln, Grant.

### The Senate Judiciary Committee's Adverse Report (June 14, 1937) — the rebuttal

- **The verdict.** "We recommend the rejection of this bill as a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle." "It is a measure which should be so emphatically rejected that its parallel will never again be presented to the free representatives of the free people of America."
- **"A MEASURE WITHOUT PRECEDENT."** "This bill is an invasion of judicial power such as has never before been attempted in this country." The 1801 Adams reorganization was "charged" with using the judiciary "for a political purpose," and its repeal was "the first task of the Jefferson administration" — but "[n]either the original act nor the repealer was an attempt to change the course of judicial decision." Every later change in the Court's size, "save one, [was] made for purely administrative purposes in aid of the Court, not to control it."
- **The one bad precedent cuts the other way.** The 1866 reduction from ten to seven, under Andrew Johnson — Thaddeus Stevens feared Johnson's appointees might vote to strike reconstruction legislation — was a reduction "to prevent a President from influencing the Court," and "is scarcely a precedent for the expansion of the Court now." So the report concedes the history Roosevelt cites and reclassifies it: administrative changes are not precedent for a change aimed at decisions.
- **The purpose is the objection.** "The only argument for the increase which survives analysis is that Congress should enlarge the Court so as to make the policies of this administration effective" — "a bill to increase the number of Justices for the express purpose of neutralizing the views of some of the present members." "This amounts to nothing more than the declaration that when the Court stands in the way of a legislative enactment, the Congress may reverse the ruling by enlarging the Court. When such a principle is adopted, our constitutional system is overthrown!" This is the key move: <u>Congress's power over size is undoubted</u>, so the objection must run to *purpose* — the very inquiry [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] says a court will not make. The check here is political because it cannot be judicial.
- **The precedent-and-reciprocity argument.** By insisting the past size changes justify this one, the proponents "show how important precedents are and prove that we should now refrain from any action that would seem to establish one which could be followed hereafter whenever a Congress and an executive should become dissatisfied with the decisions of the Supreme Court." The Democratic majority votes against its own President partly because the next majority would wield the same tool.
- **Independence as the premise.** "It is essential to the continuance of our constitutional democracy that the judiciary be completely independent of both the executive and legislative branches," and "independent courts are the last safeguard of the citizen." The report declares "for the continuance and perpetuation of government and rule by law, as distinguished from government and rule by men," and — writing in 1937 — notes that "[t]he condition of the world abroad must of necessity cause us to hesitate." Compare [[The Federalist No. 78|Federalist No. 78]] on the judiciary as the "least dangerous" branch that needs secure tenure to resist the others.
- **Patience over packing.** "Even if every charge brought against the so-called 'reactionary' members of this Court be true, it is far better that we await orderly but inevitable change of personnel than that we impatiently overwhelm them with new members." "We are not the judges of the judges. We are not above the Constitution." The remedy the report endorses is attrition and restraint, not statute.
- **The summary indictment.** The bill "would subjugate the courts to the will of Congress and the President and thereby destroy the independence of the judiciary, the only certain shield of individual rights"; its "ultimate operation would be to make this Government one of men rather than one of law"; it is "acknowledged by its proponents as a plan to force judicial interpretation of the Constitution."

### The doctrinal frame

- **Constitutional norm or merely political?** The Court's size is set by statute and has changed repeatedly (six in 1789; five under the 1801 Act; back to six in 1802; seven in 1807; nine in 1837; ten in 1863; seven by attrition from 1866; nine in 1869). Nothing in Article III fixes nine. The case for a *constitutional* norm is structural: a Court enlarged to change its answers is not independent in the sense Article III's tenure and salary protections assume — the report's "abandonment of constitutional principle." The case for a *political* norm is textual and historical: the power over size is plainly Congress's, several past changes had partisan motives, and the norm is enforced only by the Senate's own vote. The honest reading matches McCardle: the limit is real but policed politically, because no court will test Congress's motive for setting the number of seats.
- **After 1937**: The new Deal provisions were largely passed as the Court's interpretation got broader, even though new justices were not added to the Court. 
	- Court-packing _failed_ and still _won_, because the threat (plus attrition) produced the “switch” and the personnel turnover the Senate report predicted — which is why the episode is Exhibit A that the size-of-the-Court check is policed politically, not judicially.
- **Why it failed even with a supermajority.** The report is the institutional reason; the political reasons were the Court's own retreat. *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish* (Mar. 29, 1937) upheld a minimum-wage law, the Court then upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security, Justice Van Devanter announced his retirement (giving Roosevelt a seat), and Majority Leader Joseph Robinson, the bill's floor manager, died in July. The "switch in time" took away the emergency and a vacancy took away the need.
- **Hypotheticals.**
  - Congress adds two seats to handle a genuine caseload rise, with no link to any decision: within the power; <u>the report's objection is to purpose</u>.
  - Congress adds seats the day after a decision striking down its statute, openly to reverse it: the report's paradigm case; <u>still no judicial remedy, because courts do not review motive </u>(compare [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]]).
  - Congress shrinks the Court to block a President's appointments (1866): same power, reverse direction; the report's reciprocity argument applies with equal force.
  - Statutory term limits (rotating Justices to lower courts): arguably consistent with "good Behaviour" tenure because the judge keeps an Article III office — but the independence objection in the report reaches any device whose point is to change the Court's answers.
- **Departmentalism from Congress's side.** Jefferson and Lincoln ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]], [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]) argued the political branches need not accept the Court's constitutional views as final; Roosevelt's plan tried to act on that view by changing the Court rather than defying it. The report rejects the method without rejecting judicial review, standing with [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]]'s premise that the Court's constitutional judgments must not be overridden by ordinary legislation.
