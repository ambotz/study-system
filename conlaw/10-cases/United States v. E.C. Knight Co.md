## Rule

Manufacturing is not "commerce," so the Sherman Act could not reach a monopoly of sugar refining without carrying the commerce power into "every branch of human industry": "Commerce succeeds to manufacture and is not a part of it."

## Facts

- **The United States invoked the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 when the American Sugar Refining Company acquired the stock of four other refineries.**
- **The acquisition gave the company control of 98% of the country's sugar supply.** *Market dominance did not matter; the Court held that control of manufacture affects commerce "only incidentally and indirectly."*
- **What the company monopolized was refining, which is manufacturing, not the sale or shipment of sugar across state lines.** *The manufacturing character of the monopoly decided the case; control of production was control of commerce only in "a secondary and not the primary sense."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Fuller, C.J., for a 5–4 Court)

- Manufacturing is not within the meaning of "commerce": "Commerce succeeds to manufacture and is not a part of it."
- A monopoly of manufacture may bring "the operation of commerce into play," but the monopoly "does not control it, and affects it only incidentally and indirectly."
- The Sherman Act therefore did not reach the sugar-refining monopoly.

**Rejected**

- *A company that controls 98% of a product's manufacture controls how that product is sold across the country, so a manufacturing monopoly restrains interstate commerce.* "Doubtless the power to control the manufacture of a given thing involves in a certain sense the control of its disposition, but this is a secondary and not the primary sense."
- *Goods made to be sold across state lines are part of interstate commerce from the moment they are made.* If the commerce power reached "all such manufactures as are intended to be the subject of commercial transactions in the future," Congress would regulate, "to the exclusion of the States," "agriculture, horticulture, stock raising, domestic fisheries, mining—in short every branch of human industry."

## Context

E.C. Knight starts the narrowing line in the casebook's bridge note "After [[Gibbons v. Ogden]]." The expanding line ran the other way within two decades: *Champion v. Ames* (1903) let Congress ban the interstate shipment of lottery tickets, and the Shreveport Rate Case, *Houston E. & W. T. Ry. Co. v. United States* (1914), let Congress reach intrastate railroad rates. [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] followed E.C. Knight's production/commerce line. [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] (1935) and *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.* (1936) carried the line into the New Deal, Carter Coal holding that mining, like manufacturing, is not commerce.

The line ended with [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]] (1937), which sustained labor laws applied to manufacturing facilities, and with [[Wickard v. Filburn]], which refused to let labels like "production" and "indirect" decide federal power. Kennedy's concurrence in [[United States v. Lopez]] says Wickard "disapproved" E.C. Knight "and the entire line of direct-indirect and manufacture-production cases." Thomas's Lopez concurrence would return to a meaning of "commerce" as trade, in contrast to manufacturing and agriculture, which is close to E.C. Knight's. See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].
