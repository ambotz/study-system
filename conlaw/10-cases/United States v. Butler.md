## Rule

The spending power "is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution" (Hamilton's reading, not Madison's), but Congress may not use taxing and spending "to purchase compliance" with a regulatory plan it could not enact directly, a restrictive half the Court later abandoned.

## Facts

- The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to contract with farmers to reduce production in exchange for direct benefit payments.
- The benefit payments were financed by a tax on the "first domestic processing" of the commodity.
- Under the Court's precedents at the time, Congress could not regulate agricultural production under the Commerce Clause, so the government defended the Act only under the taxing and spending powers. *O'Connor later called this the real error in Butler: the Act was regulation that the modern commerce power would likely sustain.*
- Farmers received the payments only by contracting to submit to production controls. *The Court treated that contractual submission as the difference between a spending condition and a regulation.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Roberts, J., for the Court)

- The taxing and spending power's "confines are set in the clause which confers it, and not in those of section 8 which bestow and define the legislative powers of the Congress."
- The spending power "is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution."
- The purpose of federal spending "must be general, and not local."
- The Court did not decide whether an appropriation in aid of agriculture serves the "general welfare of the United States."
- The Act "invades the reserved rights of the states," and the tax, the appropriation and the disbursement "are but means to an unconstitutional end."
- Because Congress cannot command the farmer directly, "it may not indirectly accomplish those ends by taxing and spending to purchase compliance."
- There is "an obvious difference between a statute stating the conditions upon which moneys shall be expended and one effective only upon assumption of a contractual obligation to submit to a regulation which otherwise could not be enforced."

**Rejected**

- *The government: Congress may spend for the "general welfare," a phrase that "should be liberally construed to cover anything conducive to national welfare"; the decision "rests with Congress alone," and courts may not review it.* The Court accepted the Hamiltonian premise but held that a separate principle, the powers reserved to the states, barred the Act regardless of the general welfare.
- *Local agricultural conditions throughout the nation have created a problem of national concern.* The Court answered that "this is but to say that whenever there is a widespread similarity of local conditions, Congress may ignore constitutional limitations upon its own powers and usurp those reserved to the states."
- *(Stone, J., joined by Brandeis and Cardozo, JJ., dissenting) "[C]ourts are concerned only with the power to enact statutes, not with their wisdom." The only limit on spending is "that public funds shall be spent for a defined purpose, the promotion of the general welfare," so spending may reach ends outside Congress's regulatory powers.* The majority answered that spending cannot become "the instrument for total subversion of the governmental powers reserved to the individual states."

## Context

Butler settles the dispute between [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]] and [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]] in Hamilton's favor, then strikes down the statute anyway. The casebook calls the opinion seemingly "self-contradictory": the spending power is "not limited" by the enumeration, yet it may not "indirectly accomplish" what the enumeration forbids. [[Helvering v. Davis]] (1937) adopted the broad half and "did not discuss the restrictive portion." [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|South Dakota v. Dole]] repudiated the restrictive half: Rehnquist cited Butler for the point that spending limits are "less exacting" than limits on direct regulation. O'Connor, dissenting in Dole, called Butler "the last case in which this Court struck down an Act of Congress as beyond the authority granted by the Spending Clause"; she located its error in a "crabbed view" of the commerce power that [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]] later corrected.

In [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]], Roberts listed Butler among the cases that "policed these limits aggressively, invalidating punitive exactions obviously designed to regulate behavior otherwise regarded at the time as beyond federal authority." Citing Butler for a narrow spending power is a trap: its surviving holding is the broad one. See [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
