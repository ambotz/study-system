## Rule

State officials may not delay compliance with a desegregation decree because of violence and disorder that other state officials brought about. In dicta signed by all nine Justices, the Court read [[Marbury v. Madison]] as declaring "that the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution," so that the Court's interpretation in [[Brown v. Board of Education|Brown]] "is the supreme law of the land" binding every state officer under Article VI.

## Facts

- **The School Board sought to suspend its own desegregation plan for two and a half years because of violence and hostility at Central High School.** *The request was framed as a good-faith educational judgment, not defiance.*
- **The District Court found the conditions "intolerable," and the Supreme Court accepted those findings and the Board's good faith "without reservation."** *The holding cannot rest on doubting the harm.*
- **The disorder was "directly traceable to the actions of legislators and executive officials of the State of Arkansas."** *This is the decisive fact: the State cannot plead conditions it created as an excuse for delaying the rights it is violating.*
- **The Governor and Legislature acted on the premise "that they are not bound by our holding in Brown."** *That premise is what provoked the supremacy passage, which the Court says it did not need to decide the case.*
- **The President sent federal troops to secure the students' admission.** *Note 3: the Court's claim of supremacy was backed by executive force it did not command.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (9–0; opinion of the Court jointly signed by all nine Justices; Frankfurter concurring separately)

- **The postponement was properly denied.** The judgment of the Court of Appeals reversing the District Court is affirmed. "The constitutional rights of respondents are not to be sacrificed or yielded to the violence and disorder which have followed upon the actions of the Governor and Legislature."
- **The School Board acts for the State.** A State "acts by its legislative, its executive, or its judicial authorities" ([[Ex parte Virginia]]); local officials "stand in this litigation as the agents of the State," so the Board cannot shelter behind its own good faith when other state officials created the obstacles.
- **State officials are bound by Brown.** Article VI makes the Constitution supreme; Marbury established that "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution"; so Brown's interpretation "is the supreme law of the land," and every state officer's Article VI oath forbids him to "war against the Constitution."

**Rejected**

- *The Board acted in entire good faith, the educational harm is found and real, and a court of equity may give a public body time to restore order before enforcing a right.* The Court accepts every premise and rejects the conclusion: the harm was caused by the State itself, and "regardless of the Board's good faith, the actions of the other state agencies responsible for those conditions compel us to reject the Board's legal position." Nor may rights be defeated through "evasive schemes for segregation."
- *The School Board is a local body distinct from the Governor and Legislature, and should not bear the consequences of their conduct.* "[F]rom the point of view of the Fourteenth Amendment, they stand in this litigation as the agents of the State."
- *Brown binds only the parties to Brown; Arkansas officials may test its principle in fresh litigation, and meanwhile act on their own reading of the Fourteenth Amendment.* This is Lincoln's position on [[Dred Scott v. Sandford|Dred Scott]]. The Court answers with Article VI, Marbury and the oath: the interpretation itself binds, and a Governor claiming power to nullify a federal order would make "the fiat of a state Governor, and not the Constitution of the United States," the supreme law ([[Sterling v. Constantin]]).

## Context

Cooper is, in the casebook's words, the Court's "first clear-cut claim to judicial supremacy in constitutional interpretation." It adopts Douglas's side of [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] a century later, in the service of the Fourteenth Amendment rather than slavery. The casebook's Note 1 presses the consistency problem: a theory of the Court's authority has to "last you through good decisions and bad ones."

The supremacy passage rests on a reading of [[Marbury v. Madison]] (Class 11) that Marbury does not state. Marshall held that a court deciding a case must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute; "the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is" was a claim about the duty of a court in a case. Cooper converts it into a claim that the Court's exposition binds everyone. Cooper's other authorities speak of "judgments": [[United States v. Peters]] (1809) forbids state legislatures to "annul the judgments of the courts of the United States," and [[Sterling v. Constantin]] (1932) concerns a Governor nullifying "a federal court order." Cooper needed only those judgment-based authorities, because the Arkansas officials were resisting a decree in a case to which the Board was party.

Cooper also cites Taney in [[Abelman v. Booth]] (1859), a unanimous opinion rejecting Wisconsin's attempt to free a prisoner held under the Fugitive Slave Act. The Court's two great federal-supremacy precedents against state resistance, Abelman and Cooper, arose in a fugitive-slave case and a desegregation case respectively: the principle is neutral about whose rights it enforces.

The Southern Manifesto (1956) is the backdrop. Justin Driver argues (Note 4) that its debate revealed "widespread adherence to notions of judicial supremacy" before Cooper said so.

Cooper is a federal-state case. The harder question is whether its principle binds the coordinate federal branches, which is the question of [[Ex parte Merryman]] and [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]]. After the casebook, [[Trump v. CASA]] (2025) held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress has given the federal courts, confining relief to the parties; the decision bears on the line between the binding judgment and the persuasive opinion.

## Class layer

### Posture

- After Brown, the Little Rock School Board adopted a desegregation plan approved by the federal courts. On February 20, 1958, the Board and the Superintendent petitioned the District Court to postpone the plan; the District Court granted the postponement.
- The Court of Appeals reversed. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals.

### Facts

After Brown held school segregation unconstitutional, the Little Rock School Board began a desegregation plan at Central High School. When nine black students tried to enter on September 4, 1957, the Arkansas National Guard, acting on Governor Faubus's order, "stood shoulder to shoulder" and kept them out for three weeks; the United States obtained an injunction against the state officials. On September 23 the students entered under police protection but were removed because of a large, demonstrating crowd. President Eisenhower then sent federal troops, and regular army soldiers and federalized Guardsmen kept the students in school for the rest of the year. Citing chaos, violence against the students, financial strain and a hostile public stirred up by the Governor and Legislature, the Board sought to send the students back to segregated schools and postpone desegregation for two and a half years.

### Issue

- May a federal court postpone a school board's desegregation plan because of violence and disorder, when the disorder is attributable to the actions of the State's Governor and Legislature?
- Are state legislators and executive officials bound by the Supreme Court's interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment in Brown?

### Holding

- No. The rights of the black students cannot be "sacrificed or yielded to the violence and disorder" the State's own officials created; the Board is the agent of the State and its good faith is no excuse.
- Yes. Article VI makes the Constitution supreme, Marbury makes the federal judiciary "supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution," and state officers are bound by oath to support the Constitution as the Court has interpreted it. The Court treats this holding as unnecessary to the disposition ("What has been said, in the light of the facts developed, is enough to dispose of the case").

### Rule

- A State acts through all its officers; conduct of any state agency counts against the State under the Fourteenth Amendment.
- Hostility and disorder, especially when stirred up by state officials, do not justify delaying the vindication of constitutional rights.
- The Supreme Court's interpretation of the Constitution "is the supreme law of the land" and binds state officials under Article VI and the oath of Article VI, cl. 3.

### Reasoning

**(Opinion of the Court, signed by Warren, C.J., and Black, Frankfurter, Douglas, Burton, Clark, Harlan, Brennan and Whittaker, JJ.)**

- The Court accepts the Board's good faith and the District Court's findings, then relocates the cause: the conditions "are directly traceable to the actions of legislators and executive officials of the State of Arkansas." The Board's own certiorari petition says so.
- If the school were run directly by the State, those in charge could not plead their own good faith as an excuse for delay caused by other state officials; local status changes nothing.
- The Fourteenth Amendment's command runs to the "State," which acts only through its officers ([[Ex parte Virginia]]); rights cannot be nullified "ingeniously or ingenuously" ([[Smith v. Texas]]).
- Having disposed of the case, the Court answers the premise that state officials are not bound by Brown: Article VI; Marbury's "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is"; the principle of judicial supremacy "has ever since been respected by this Court and the Country as a permanent and indispensable feature of our constitutional system."
- The oath: "No state legislator or executive or judicial officer can war against the Constitution without violating his undertaking to support it."
- Precedent: [[United States v. Peters]] (state legislatures may not annul federal judgments or "the constitution itself becomes a solemn mockery"); [[Sterling v. Constantin]] (a Governor's fiat is not supreme law); [[Abelman v. Booth]] (the oath reflects the framers' anxiety to guard against state resistance).
- Brown's authority is reinforced by its process and its reaffirmation: unanimous, twice argued, and now unanimously reaffirmed by a Court including three new Justices.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous. Frankfurter wrote a separate concurrence, which the casebook omits.

### Cold-call notes

- **Did Marbury say what Cooper says it said?** No, not in terms. Marbury holds that a court deciding a case must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute, which is judicial review. Cooper adds a proposition Marbury never states: that the judiciary is "supreme in the exposition" of the Constitution, so that its interpretation binds officials who were not parties. The claim that this principle "has ever since been respected by this Court and the Country" is hard to square with Jefferson, Jackson and Lincoln, whose views appear on the pages just before Cooper.
- **Was the supremacy passage necessary to the decision?** No, on the Court's own account: "What has been said, in the light of the facts developed, is enough to dispose of the case. However, we should answer the premise." The supremacy passage is a deliberate dictum, and it is also the only part of the opinion anyone remembers.
- **Note 2: do Marshall and Hughes go as far as Cooper?** No. [[United States v. Peters]] speaks of state legislatures annulling "the judgments of the courts of the United States"; [[Sterling v. Constantin]] of a Governor nullifying "a federal court order." Cooper claims authority for "our holding." The difference is the judgment/opinion line: Peters and Sterling support judgment supremacy; Cooper asserts opinion supremacy.
- **Could Cooper have been written on judgment supremacy alone?** Yes. The Little Rock officials were resisting a desegregation decree in a case to which the School Board was party, and the United States had an injunction against the state officials. Every obligation at issue flowed from a federal judgment. The broader claim was a response to the Governor's and Legislature's stated premise, not a need of the case.
- **Is Douglas's view the same as the Court's? Is Lincoln's view Faubus's?** (Note 1.) Douglas's view is the Court's: decisions bind "every good citizen." Lincoln's is not Faubus's: Lincoln disclaimed resisting the judgment ("we, as a mob, will decide him to be free"); Faubus used troops to block a federal decree. A departmentalist who respects judgments condemns Faubus without adopting Cooper's dictum.
- **Hypothetical: Arkansas passes a new statute requiring segregation in a district not party to any suit.** On judgment supremacy, no decree yet binds that district; officials must be sued. On Cooper, the statute is void and officers violate their oath by enforcing it. On Lincoln's view, Brown is "settled" under his criteria (unanimous, reaffirmed), so it should "control ... the general policy of the country." Lincoln's criteria and Cooper converge on Brown; they diverge on a 5–4 decision.
- **Is state defiance different from federal-branch departmentalism?** Yes, and the Supremacy Clause is the reason. Article VI binds state judges and puts federal law above state law "any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding"; the Constitution establishes a hierarchy between nation and State, and state resistance to federal judgments is nullification. Between Congress, the President and the Court there is no textual hierarchy; that is Bates's co-ordinacy argument in [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Bates]]. Cooper can be right about Arkansas without settling [[Ex parte Merryman]].
- **Note 3: what if Eisenhower had not sent troops?** The Court has, in Hamilton's phrase in [[The Federalist No. 78]], "merely judgment," and must "depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments." The Court's supremacy claim was made from a position of executive support; the question of what happens when the executive declines is Merryman's.
- **Note 4: the Southern Manifesto.** If Driver is right that Southern officials themselves accepted the premise of judicial supremacy, Cooper is less a power grab than a codification. The counterpoint is that Arkansas officials, in the case itself, explicitly denied being bound.
- **Consequences argument.** Cooper is a strong consequentialist case for judicial supremacy: without it, each State relitigates every right. Lincoln's case is the strongest consequentialist case against: supremacy entrenches a Dred Scott. A theory that must survive both is the test the casebook sets.
- **Type of argument.** Text (Article VI, the oath), precedent (Marbury, Peters, Sterling, Abelman), and structure (a State acts through all its officers). The claim that the principle has always been respected is an argument from practice. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Signed by all nine.** A unique form: each Justice listed by name as author. Frankfurter concurred separately all the same.
