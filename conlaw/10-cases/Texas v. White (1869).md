## Snapshot

The United States issued bearer bonds to Texas; the insurgent state government sold them during the rebellion, and the Reconstruction government sued in the State's name in the Court's original jurisdiction to recover them. The defendants answered that Texas was not a State of the Union, so secession had to be decided to decide jurisdiction. The Court held the ordinance absolutely null, Texas a State throughout, and the Guarantee Clause the source of the restoring power — Congress's.

## Issue

- Did Texas cease to be a State, or a member of the Union, by the ordinance of secession and the acts giving it effect, so that original jurisdiction fails?
- Did the insurgent government's contract divest the State's title to the bonds?
- From what power does the authority to restore a rebel State's relations derive, and in which branch?

## Rule

A State cannot leave the Union by its own act: the ordinance of secession and every act giving it effect are "absolutely null," so the State keeps its obligations and its title to its property. But rebellion suspends the State's rights as a member, and restoring its relations to the Union is a Guarantee Clause power, which "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress."

- The Constitution, "in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States."
- Admission "was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final" — exit only by "revolution, or . . . consent of the States."
- Rebellion suspends a State's rights as a member and its citizens' rights without destroying either: "Obligations often remained unimpaired, while relations are greatly changed."

## Operative facts

- **1851: the United States issued bonds payable after 1864 to the State of Texas or bearer, in settlement of Texas's boundary claims.** *Bearer form is why the case turns on the State's title rather than on the mechanics of transfer.*
- **1861: a Texas convention adopted an ordinance of secession, a majority of citizens ratified it, and the legislature passed acts to carry it into effect, breaking off relations with the Union and establishing new relations with the Confederacy.** *Chase concedes the premise entirely — "the relations of Texas to the Union were broken up" — so everything turns on legal operation. Had the acts had force, Texas was no State and could not sue here.*
- **During the war the insurgent government contracted the bonds away to White and others to raise money.** *Title passes only if the insurgent legislature's acts were valid, and the purpose of the sale puts it on the rebellion side of Chase's unpublished line.*
- **Emancipation changed who "the people" of Texas were: "[t]he new freemen . . . part of the people."** *Why the pre-war government could not simply resume, which forces the Guarantee Clause analysis.*
- **After the surrender the President appointed a provisional governor and called a convention; the constitution was amended, elections were held, and a government acknowledging its obligations to the Union took office.** *Executive-created, which forces Chase to decide whose power Reconstruction is.*
- **Congress afterwards treated that government as provisional under the Reconstruction Acts, which declared Texas a "rebel State" and placed her in the fifth military district, "subject, not to the civil authority, but to the 'military authorities of the United States.'"** *The same statutes supply the majority's confirmation and the dissent's refutation.*
- **That government sued in the State's name to recover the bonds and restrain their payment.** *Original jurisdiction runs to suits by a State, so secession had to be decided to decide the Court's power.*
- **By the December 1868 Term Texas had no senators and her voice had not been heard "in the late election of President."** *(Grier.) The political facts the dissent rests on, all undisputed.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (5–3; Chase, C.J.; Grier dissenting; Swayne, joined by Miller, dissenting on jurisdiction and agreeing on the merits)

- **Texas remained a State of the Union.** The ordinance and implementing acts were "utterly without operation in law," so she "continued to be a State, and a State of the Union," and may sue.
- **Title was not divested.** "[T]he title of the State [to the bonds] was not divested by the act of the insurgent government in entering into this contract."
- **Restoration belongs primarily to Congress.** The President "might institute temporary government within insurgent districts, occupied by the National forces," but the guarantee power "resides in Congress," so his action is only "provisional."

**Chase, C.J., for the Court**

- History: the Union "began among the Colonies," was "solemnly declared to 'be perpetual'" by the Articles, then ordained "to form a more perfect Union." "What can be indissoluble if a perpetual Union, made more perfect, is not?"
- Structure runs both ways: powers not delegated are reserved, "the people of each State compose a State," and "without the States in union, there could be no such political body as the United States."
- Nullity is not no change: "the rights of the State as a member, and of her people as citizens of the Union, were suspended," and her government and citizens "assumed the character of enemies."
- Allocation: executive action was a war measure and so provisional; the guarantee itself is legislative, and the Reconstruction Acts confirm it. Means are tested as in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]: "[A] discretion in the choice of means is necessary allowed."

**Grier, J., dissenting**

- "Is Texas one of these United States? . . . This is to be decided as a political fact, not as a legal fiction. This court is bound to know and notice the public history of the nation."
- That history answers it: no senators, no voice in the late presidential election, and "[i]s she not now held and governed as a conquered province by military force?" Federal courts sit in Dakota and among the Indian tribes too: "[w]herein does the condition of Texas differ?"
- "I am not disposed to join in any essay to prove Texas to be a State of the Union, when Congress have decided that she is not." On the merits she relies on "one fiction" and then its opposite: "[s]he now sets up the plea of insanity."

**Swayne, J., joined by Miller, J.**

- Agrees with Grier that Texas in "her present condition" cannot "maintain an original suit in this court," a question on which "this court is bound by the action of the legislative department," and agrees on the merits.

## Arguments rejected

- *Texas's acts accomplished what they set out to accomplish, so she had ceased to be a State and cannot sue here.* Chase accepts the premise in full; the acts had no legal operation whatever. The consequence of the contrary view is the real argument: "the State must have become foreign, and her citizens foreigners. The war must have ceased to be a war for the suppression of rebellion, and must have become a war for conquest and subjugation."
- *An indissoluble Union is incompatible with State sovereignty.* "[T]he perpetuity and indissolubility of the Union, by no means implies the loss of distinct and individual existence, or of the right of self-government by the States."
- *If Texas never left, her wartime legislation binds her like any other.* "No one has been bold enough to contend that... senators chosen by her legislature, or representatives elected by her citizens, were entitled to seats in Congress."
- *Congress's own Reconstruction Acts treat the Southern governments as outside the Union.* The Acts "show that the governments . . . were recognized by Congress as provisional, as existing, and as capable of continuance."

## Where it sits

**Lincoln's theory ratified.** The three steps of [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln's]] argument reappear unchanged: the Articles' "perpetual" Union, the Preamble's "more perfect Union," and the inference that perfecting cannot have destroyed perpetuity. [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] took them in 1860, to the opposite conclusion about remedy; against [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]]: admission was "more than a compact," so a sovereign has nothing to revoke.

**Indestructible States.** The opinion is nationalist on [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell's]] map but careful; "the people of each State" borrows [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|Marshall's]] location of the constituent power in the people. The anti-commandeering cases quote the other half — [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] and the [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] dissenters.

**The Guarantee Clause engine.** *Luther v. Borden* (1849) left enforcement to the political branches; Chase turns it into an affirmative grant to Congress and a basis for Reconstruction. The collision produced the Reconstruction Acts and [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]], and [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] shows the Court's appetite for testing Congress.

**The nullity seam.** In a passage the casebook omits Chase drew the line himself: acts necessary to peace and good order are generally valid, acts in furtherance of the rebellion void. *Horn v. Lockhart* (1873) applied it. The gap is the weakest seam.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Does Article IV explicitly provide for the division of a state? . . . The act of Congress admitting Texas as a state permits it to divide into as many as five states; these terms have never been expressly repealed."**

- Not explicitly, and only by consent: Article IV, § 3 bars a new State "formed or erected within the Jurisdiction of any other State" without that legislature's consent and Congress's. Texas's 1845 admission arguably gave it in advance, so the indestructible State may be destructible on its own terms (Kesavan & Paulsen, *Let's Mess with Texas*) — which distinguishes the holding rather than contradicting it: Chase bars only unilateral exit.

**Note 2. "Article IV, Clause 3 provides a way for new states to join the Union, but it does not expressly provide for any mechanism for states to leave . . . . Or could a state leave with Congress's consent, under the theory that the power to undo is incident to the power to do?"**

- The rhetorical half is the live one: if an exit must first be added by amendment, even consensual secession is unconstitutional and Chase's reservation of exit by consent is empty.
- Against expressio unius: Congress admits States, so undoing may be incident to doing, and Article IV grants power rather than listing every fate a State may have.

**Note 3. "[I]f the people of Quebec voted for secession the central government would be obligated to enter into negotiations with it on the terms of secession, including how to apportion the national debt and where to draw the new nation's boundary lines . . . ."**

- A third answer, between nullity and a right to secede: no unilateral right, but a duty to negotiate once a clear majority has spoken.
- Chase has no equivalent: nullity leaves a seceding State no standing to demand anything, so the American routes stay revolution, consent, or amendment.

### Further drilling

- **What was actually at stake.** A suit over bonds: the most famous statement of the nature of the Union decided whether a party could sue.
- **"[A]bsolutely null" cannot mean what it says.** Void every Texas statute from 1861 to 1865 and no marriage is valid, no deed recorded. The bond sale financed the war; a bona fide bearer purchaser has no place in the theory at all.
- **Why did Texas need re-admission?** She did not — only restoration of a lawful government. The awkward fact is the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified while the South was unrepresented.
- **Consistent with *Luther v. Borden*?** Luther made a State's lawful government a political question, yet the Court decides for itself that the Reconstruction government is Texas's; the dissenters would have applied it all the way down.
