## What it is

A settlement agreement, effective May 18, 2026, resolving *Trump v. Internal Revenue Service*, No. 1:26-cv-20609-KMW (S.D. Fla.). President Trump, his sons, and the Trump Organization sued the IRS and Treasury under 26 U.S.C. §§ 6103 and 7431 and the Privacy Act over Charles Littlejohn's disclosure of their tax returns, and the President separately filed Federal Tort Claims Act claims over the 2022 Mar-a-Lago search and the Russia investigation.

The named plaintiffs receive "a formal apology from the United States, but will not receive any monetary payment or damages of any kind." In exchange, the Attorney General agrees to create "The Anti-Weaponization Fund" to hear claims from others alleging similar "Lawfare and Weaponization." An Attorney General's order, to issue within thirty days, "shall establish funding." The Fund has five Members appointed by the Attorney General, one chosen in consultation with congressional leadership, each removable by the President without cause. It may issue apologies and "monetary relief owed to claimants as a result of their legal rights," reports quarterly to the Attorney General, stops processing claims by December 1, 2028, and sweeps any remaining balance to Commerce, Interior, or another account the President designates.

## What to notice

- **The plaintiffs take no money, and that is the point.** Paying them would require an appropriation or the Judgment Fund. The apology costs nothing from the Treasury, so the Appropriations Clause is satisfied as to them. The constitutional question moves to the Fund.
- **"Shall establish funding" names no source.** Article I, § 9, cl. 7 permits money to be drawn from the Treasury only "in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." A settlement and an Attorney General's order are neither. Ask what appropriation the corpus comes from, and whether a settlement can create a spending programme Congress never enacted.
- **The sweep provision is the sharper problem.** Transferring a leftover balance to Commerce or Interior, as the President designates, is the executive moving appropriated money between purposes — the transfer-and-reprogramming question, not merely the spending one.
- **The Fund looks like an agency.** Five Members with tenure, claims procedures of their own devising, authority to grant or deny relief, and removal at will. If they exercise significant authority under the laws of the United States, the Appointments Clause requires appointment as officers, and an Attorney General's order is not that. Consultation with congressional leadership on one seat does not cure it.
- **Claimants must give up judicial relief to take Fund relief**, which routes claims that Congress channelled into court into an executive process instead.
- **The recitals are a partisan account**, attributing "Lawfare and Weaponization" to "Democrat elected officials" and listing contested examples. Read them as the agreement's framing, not as findings.

It is the executive-side counterpart to [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]]: there the House alleged the executive spent without an appropriation, and here the executive creates a fund whose source is unnamed. On the officer question compare [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)]], and on structuring a body outside the ordinary appointment and removal rules compare [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)]] and [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]]. Pair it with [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] as the two modern instances of a President acting against an appropriations constraint.
