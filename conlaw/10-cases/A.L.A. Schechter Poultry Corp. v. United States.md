## Rule

Congress may not give the President an unguided power to write codes of conduct for trade and industry, and Congress may not regulate the wages and hours of a local business whose effect on interstate commerce is "merely indirect"; Schechter is one of only two cases in which the Court has struck down a statute as an excessive delegation.

## Facts

- **Section 3 of the National Industrial Recovery Act let trade groups propose "codes of fair competition" that the President could make legally binding.** *The Court held that § 3 "supplies no standards for any trade, industry, or activity."*
- **The President's required findings were only that the group was "truly representative" and the code "not designed to promote monopolies or to eliminate or oppress small enterprises," and he could add conditions and exceptions "as the President in his discretion deems necessary."** *These findings did not count as a standard; the Court called the President's discretion "virtually unfettered."*
- **The Live Poultry Code set a forty-hour work week and a minimum wage of fifty cents an hour, and the Schechters were convicted of nineteen violations.**
- **The Schechters' Brooklyn slaughterhouse bought chickens shipped from other states, then slaughtered them and resold them to butchers within New York.** *The chickens' interstate origin did not help the government; the persons employed in slaughtering and selling in local trade "are not employed in interstate commerce."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Hughes, C.J., for a unanimous Court)

- **Delegation.** "Section 3 of the Recovery Act is without precedent." Instead of prescribing rules of conduct, "it authorizes the making of codes to prescribe them," and "the code-making authority thus conferred is an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power."
- **Commerce.** "Where the effect of intrastate transactions upon interstate commerce is merely indirect, such transactions remain within the domain of state power." The wage and hour provisions govern "the details of defendants' management of their local business."
- **Disposition.** The convictions and fines were overturned.
- **Cardozo's concurrence (as quoted in [[United States v. Lopez|Lopez]]).** "There is a view of causation that would obliterate the distinction between what is national and what is local in the activities of commerce."

**Rejected**

- *Congress did confine the President: he could approve a code only on findings that the proposing group was representative and the code was not monopolistic or oppressive to small enterprises.* Those findings prescribe no rules of conduct; § 3 leaves the President "enacting laws for the government of trade and industry throughout the country" with discretion that "is virtually unfettered."
- *Wages and hours in the poultry business drive the costs and prices of poultry that moves in interstate commerce.* That effect is indirect, and if the federal government could set wages "because of their relation to costs and prices and their indirect effect on interstate commerce," then "a similar control might be exerted over other elements of cost, also affecting prices."

## Context

Schechter and *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, both decided in 1935, are the only cases in which the Court has held a delegation unconstitutional ([[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]]). The [[Gundy v. United States]] plurality cites the two as the lone exceptions to the [[J. W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States|J. W. Hampton]] intelligible-principle test, each failing because "Congress had failed to articulate any policy or standard." Gorsuch's Gundy dissent answers that if a slaughterhouse code was "delegation running riot," a criminal code written by the Attorney General cannot be valid, and notes that Congress responded to the 1935 cases with a second wave of New Deal statutes "[c]arefully crafted" to avoid them. White's dissent in [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] treats the two cases as the exceptions in a doctrine whose restrictions "diminished and all but disappeared" in practice. The casebook leaves open whether the administrative state "could survive the rigorous application of Schechter Poultry." Schechter was decided the same day as [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]]. See [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]].

On the commerce side, Schechter and *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.* (1936) carried [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]]'s production and "indirect effects" reasoning into the New Deal. [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]] rejected aspects of Schechter in 1937, and [[Wickard v. Filburn]] discarded the direct/indirect distinction. [[United States v. Lopez]] revived Cardozo's concurrence as a limit on effects reasoning. See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].
