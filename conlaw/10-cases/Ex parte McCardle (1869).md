## Snapshot

The military held a Mississippi newspaper editor for trial before a military commission over articles attacking Reconstruction. He lost habeas in circuit court and appealed under the Habeas Corpus Act of 1867; after argument, Congress repealed the appeal provision, expressly including pending appeals. The Court dismissed for want of jurisdiction, holding the repeal a valid exception under Article III, § 2's Exceptions Clause and refusing to inquire into Congress's motive. The lawfulness of military Reconstruction, the question everyone expected, went undecided.

## Issue

- Does Congress have power under Article III, § 2 to withdraw the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over habeas appeals taken under the 1867 Act?
- Does the repeal apply to an appeal already pending and argued?
- Does the repeal take away all of the Court's appellate jurisdiction in habeas cases?

## Rule

Congress may withdraw the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over a class of cases under the Exceptions Clause of Article III, § 2, even while a case in that class is pending and has been argued, and the Court will not inquire into Congress's motive. A court whose jurisdiction has been repealed must dismiss. But a jurisdictional repeal reaches only what it names: the 1868 Act closed the 1867 appeal route and left the 1789 Act's habeas-with-certiorari route and the Court's original habeas power standing.

- Appellate jurisdiction is "strictly speaking, conferred by the Constitution," but "'with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make.'"
- "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law," and once it is withdrawn "the only function remaining to the court is that of announcing the fact and dismissing the cause."
- "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature. We can only examine into its power under the Constitution."

## Operative facts

- **Under the Military Reconstruction Acts, federal military authority arrested McCardle, a Mississippi newspaper editor and not a member of the military, and held him for trial before a military commission on charges based on articles "alleged to be incendiary and libellous."** *A civilian before a military commission is the sharpest possible test of military Reconstruction, so a merits ruling threatened the whole program.*
- **The Habeas Corpus Act of February 5, 1867, itself a Reconstruction statute, expanded federal habeas and gave an appeal to the Supreme Court.** *Congress built the door it later closed, and that Act is the only jurisdictional basis McCardle ever invoked.*
- **The Circuit Court for the Southern District of Mississippi denied habeas corpus and remanded him to military custody; he appealed under the 1867 Act.** *With no alternative basis invoked, repealing that one provision could dispose of the case.*
- **The Court denied the Government's motion to dismiss at the prior term and heard argument on the merits.** *The withdrawal therefore hit a pending, argued case and not only future ones — the hard version of the question, and the reason the case is cited for stripping at all.*
- **Congress repealed the 1867 appeal provision by the Act of March 27, 1868, over President Johnson's veto, while his impeachment trial was under way.** *This is the motive the Court refused to examine; change the timing and nobody doubts the power.*
- **The repealing act reached "the exercise of any such jurisdiction by said Supreme Court, on appeals which have been, or may hereafter be taken."** *The express reach to appeals already taken left no room to read the repeal as prospective only.*
- **The repeal excepted only appeals from Circuit Courts under the 1867 Act.** *Other habeas routes survived, which Ex parte Yerger then used; this is what makes McCardle narrow authority for total stripping.*
- **The Court held the case over once the repeal passed and then decided only the jurisdictional question, dismissing on April 12, 1869, in the December Term 1868.** *Which is why the casebook (4th ed. p. 375) cites the case as "(1868)."*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (unanimous, 8–0; Chase, C.J., for the Court; no separate opinions)

- **Power to withdraw: yes.** Appellate jurisdiction exists "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make," and "[i]t is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception"; motive is not examined.
- **Application to a pending, argued appeal: yes.** A repealed jurisdictional statute is treated as if it never existed except as to closed transactions, and the Act expressly reached pending appeals; the only remaining function is "announcing the fact and dismissing the cause."
- **Withdrawal of all habeas jurisdiction: no.** The repeal excepts only appeals from Circuit Courts under the 1867 Act and "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised."
- Appeal "DISMISSED FOR WANT OF JURISDICTION."

**Chase, C.J., for the Court**

- **Jurisdiction first.** "The first question necessarily is that of jurisdiction"; without it, "it is useless, if not improper, to enter into any discussion of other questions."
- **Source of the jurisdiction.** It is "not derived from acts of Congress" and is "strictly speaking, conferred by the Constitution" — but conferred "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make."
- **The negative pregnant of the Judiciary Acts.** Following *Durousseau v. United States*, Congress has "described affirmatively, its jurisdiction, and this affirmative description has been understood to imply a negation" of jurisdiction not described.
- **The repeal.** The 1867 provision affirming appellate habeas jurisdiction "is expressly repealed" — "[i]t is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception."
- **Motive.** "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature. We can only examine into its power under the Constitution; and the power to make exceptions to the appellate jurisdiction of this court is given by express words."
- **Pending cases.** "When an act of the legislature is repealed, it must be considered, except as to transactions past and closed, as if it never existed."
- **Judicial duty.** "[J]udicial duty is not less fitly performed by declining ungranted jurisdiction than in exercising firmly that which the Constitution and the laws confer."
- **The saving paragraph.** "The act of 1868 does not except from that jurisdiction any cases but appeals from Circuit Courts under the act of 1867. It does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised."

## Arguments rejected

- *Appellate jurisdiction comes from the Constitution itself, not from Congress, so a statute cannot take it away once the Court has lawfully acquired a case.* Chase: it is indeed "not derived from acts of Congress," but the same clause confers it only "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make."
- *Congress's purpose was to prevent a ruling on the constitutionality of Reconstruction, and a withdrawal aimed at one pending case is an attack on the judicial power, not an "exception."* Chase: motive is not open to inquiry; the Court "can only examine into its power under the Constitution."
- *The Court had already taken jurisdiction and heard argument, so the repeal came too late to affect this case.* Chase: under *Norris v. Crocker* and *Insurance Company v. Ritchie*, no judgment can be rendered after the repeal of the act under which the suit was brought.
- *Giving the repeal effect would deny the whole appellate power of the Court in habeas cases.* Chase: counsel "seem to have supposed" so, "[b]ut this is an error"; the repeal excepts only "appeals from Circuit Courts under the act of 1867."

## Where it sits

**Congress's three levers.** McCardle is the leading case for jurisdiction stripping and the Class 15 companion to the other two checks on the Court: impeaching Justices ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]) and changing the Court's size ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]]). The repeal passed over Johnson's veto during his own impeachment trial ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]).

**The limits the sequels drew.** Outside the assigned reading. *Ex parte Yerger* (1869) held the Court still had appellate habeas jurisdiction through the 1789 Judiciary Act, the route McCardle's last paragraph reserved. *United States v. Klein* (1872) held that Congress may not use jurisdictional form to dictate outcomes; Chase, writing again, distinguished McCardle as a valid denial of appeal in "a particular class of cases."

**Klein read narrowly.** *Bank Markazi v. Peterson* (2016) upheld a statute changing the law for identified pending cases, and a *Patchak v. Zinke* (2018) plurality upheld one directing dismissal of suits about a single parcel. *Felker v. Turpin* (1996) upheld AEDPA's habeas restrictions because, as in *Yerger*, original habeas remained.

**The open question.** Henry Hart's view that exceptions may not destroy the Court's essential role, against Akhil Amar's reading of Article III's "all Cases," is a fight over whether McCardle's language holds when Congress closes every route — which McCardle itself did not face.

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

- **What exactly did Congress take away?** One statutory route: appeals from circuit courts under the 1867 Act. It left the 1789 Act's habeas-with-certiorari route and the Court's original habeas power untouched. So McCardle is weaker authority for total stripping than its language suggests.
- **Distinguishing fact from Ex parte Yerger.** Yerger, another prisoner of the military, came up through the route McCardle reserved, and the Court took jurisdiction. Same Court, same year: the only difference is the statutory door. Together they mean Congress never cut off review of military detention.
- **Distinguishing fact from United States v. Klein.** McCardle's statute removed a class of cases; Klein's left the cases in court but told the Court how to decide them. Chase drew the line himself: denying appeal in "a particular class of cases" is valid; "prescrib[ing] a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way" is not.
- **Hypo: Congress strips all federal jurisdiction, the Supreme Court's and the lower courts', over challenges to a statute.** McCardle's text supports it; Yerger and the saving paragraph show the Court never faced it. Against: Article III says the judicial power "shall extend" to "all Cases"; the claim would have no forum (the Suspension Clause for habeas — see [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] and *Boumediene v. Bush*); and state courts would be left as final interpreters of federal law.
- **Hypo: Congress strips jurisdiction only over cases where the claimant challenges Reconstruction and wins.** One-sided stripping dictates outcomes, so it looks like Klein, not McCardle. The line is between removing a class of cases and removing the Court's ability to rule one way.
- **Hypo: the repeal comes after decision but before the mandate issues.** "[T]ransactions past and closed" are untouched, so the rule reaches only undecided cases. Compare *Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm*, which forbids Congress to reopen final judgments.
- **Motive.** The Court refuses to look at motive although everyone knew it. The [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] condemns the 1937 bill precisely for its purpose, "a change in the decisions of the Court." A court applying McCardle could not reach that objection; the Senate could.
- **Departmentalism from Congress's side.** [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] and [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] make the Court's judgments binding, but McCardle shows Congress can decide which cases the Court gets: stripping does not overrule the Court's view of the Constitution, it removes the occasion for stating it. Compare the 1802 repeal of the circuit judgeships, upheld in [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] without inquiry into purpose.
- **The original-meaning argument.** The "exceptions and regulations" clause was arguably about fact review, not about removing constitutional questions; [[The Federalist No. 78|Federalist No. 78]] and [[Brutus No. 11]] assume the Court will pass on constitutionality. McCardle's text-first answer is formalist; the "essential functions" view is structural.
- **Memorable lines.** "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law"; "a plainer instance of positive exception"; "declining ungranted jurisdiction."
