## What it is

The casebook's section-by-section overview of Article I, opening Part A (Legislative Power). It is preceded on p. 62 by the chapter's closing passage on Madison's The Federalist Nos. 48 and 51, which frames the whole separation-of-powers unit.

## What to notice

- **Parchment barriers are not enough.** Federalist 48: "Will it be sufficient to mark, with precision, the boundaries of these departments . . . and to trust to these parchment barriers against the encroaching spirit of power?" Madison's answer is no.
- **Madison's solution is incentives.** Federalist 51: give each department "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others." "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place."
- **"If men were angels, no government would be necessary."** Government must "first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself." The casebook asks you to look for the ambition of each branch in every case that follows.
- **§ 1 carries three principles.**
  - Enumeration: Congress has only the legislative powers "herein granted," mostly listed in § 8.
  - Vesting: the powers are vested in Congress specifically. The powers were already delegated once, from the people to Congress. The question is whether Congress can "subdelegate" them. That is the nondelegation question in Class 3.
  - Bicameralism: a bill must pass both houses (§ 7). That is Class 4.
- **§§ 2–3: the House and the Senate.** Election, qualifications, apportionment and officers. The House has "the sole power of impeachment"; the Senate has the power to "try all Impeachments."
- **§ 4: elections.** States set the times, places and manner of congressional elections, subject to congressional alteration. A federalism principle inside Article I.
- **§ 5: each house governs itself.** It judges its members' elections and qualifications, sets its own rules, may punish and expel members, keeps a journal, and cannot adjourn for more than three days without the other's consent.
- **§ 6: independence of members.** Salary "ascertained by law," limited by the Twenty-seventh Amendment; privilege from arrest except for "Treason, Felony and Breach of the Peace"; the Incompatibility Clause (no executive or judicial officer may sit in Congress); and the Ineligibility Clause (no appointment to an office created, or whose "Emoluments" were increased, during the member's term).
- **§ 7: lawmaking.** Revenue bills originate in the House; every bill passes both houses and goes to the President; a veto can be overridden by two-thirds of both houses.
- **§ 8: eighteen enumerated powers.** Some are very broad. The federalism questions are Chapter 3.
- **§ 9: limits on Congress.** The slave-trade clause (to 1808), the Suspension Clause, bills of attainder and ex post facto laws, export taxes, preferences among ports, and, "anticlimactically," titles of nobility.
- **§ 10: limits on the states.** Many things the states may not do are the things § 8 lets Congress do (coin money), and some the states may do only with congressional consent. That explains the section's placement in Article I.
