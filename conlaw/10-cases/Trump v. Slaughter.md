## Rule

An officer who exercises executive power must be removable by the President at will, whether the agency has one head or five, so the FTC Act's for-cause protection is unconstitutional. [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] survives, at most, as the "observation" that an agency exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the rule; "[i]f anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it."

## Facts

- **The President fired two FTC commissioners without claiming statutory cause.** He told them their service was "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities" and invoked Article II. *No inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance was alleged, so the statute was squarely violated and the constitutional question could not be avoided.*
- **The FTC Act allows removal only "for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office."** *The same clause Humphrey's upheld in 1935. The case is a direct challenge to that precedent, not an extension of it.*
- **Today's FTC makes binding rules, investigates and adjudicates, and sues in federal court.** *Decisive: each power is "executive" under the modern cases. A body that only advised Congress or assisted courts could still fit what is left of Humphrey's.*
- **The FTC is a five-member, multimember commission.** *Irrelevant after this case. [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] turned on the single head; Slaughter turns on the kind of power.*
- **The FTC is not the Federal Reserve and not a non-Article III court.** *Both are expressly reserved in Part III-B, which Thomas did not join.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (6–3; Roberts, C.J., for the Court; Thomas joined all but Part III-B; Gorsuch concurring; Sotomayor dissenting, joined by Kagan and Jackson)

- The FTC's for-cause removal provision "is contrary to the separation of powers enshrined in the Constitution." Judgment of the District Court reversed and case remanded.
- Vesting the executive power in one President "was to establish a hierarchy"; "[t]o remain accountable to the President, those officers must be removable by the President."
- The [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]] and early practice "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the removal power, and [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] confirmed it.
- "Humphrey's framework, in short, has not withstood the test of time." "If anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it."
- Every stare decisis factor (quality of reasoning, consistency, workability, reliance) "counsels in favor of letting Humphrey's go."
- "The FTC unquestionably exercises executive power, and must therefore be controlled by the Chief Executive, in whom such power is vested."
- Reserved (Part III-B): offices with no executive power at all; "the Federal Reserve, to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States"; and tenure protection for judges of "non-Article III courts."

**Rejected**

- *Humphrey's squarely held that Congress may protect FTC commissioners from at-will removal, Congress has built dozens of independent agencies on that holding for ninety years, and stare decisis should protect that reliance.* Roberts: Congress relied on Humphrey's to create agencies "insulated from presidential control," and "[t]hat is precisely the problem"; independent agencies are insulated "from the President," "not from politics," and "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere."
- *The Decision of 1789 settled only that the President has a default removal power when Congress is silent, not that Congress cannot limit it.* Roberts: "The two, however, are one and the same." If removal is part of the executive power, it is as far outside Congress's control as the pardon or the veto, and Madison said the legislature "has no right to diminish or modify" it.
- *The Federalist itself contemplated limits: Madison said tenure would be a subject of legal regulation (No. 39), and Hamilton said the Senate's consent would be needed "to displace as well as to appoint" (No. 77).* Roberts: these are "two passing comments" that cannot override the logic of The Federalist as a whole, still less the Decision of 1789.
- *The First Congress created multimember bodies (the Sinking Fund Commission, the Mint Board, a war-debt commission) not fully under the President's control.* Roberts: their members "were removable by the President at will"; most were Cabinet secretaries.
- *Longstanding modern practice of for-cause commissions deserves weight.* Roberts: "We have never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power"; [[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]] struck down the legislative veto despite hundreds of statutes using it.
- *Courts should police removal limits only for reasonableness, deferring when Congress reasonably decides some officers need independence.* Roberts: the principle "is neither limiting nor much of a principle"; it would let Congress insulate most of the government.
- *[[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] "applied and expanded Humphrey's."* Roberts: Morrison abandoned the "quasi" categories and acknowledged that the FTC's powers "would at the present time be considered 'executive,' at least to some degree."

## Context

Slaughter completes the line that began when [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] (2020) confined [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]] to "its own terms" and [[Collins v. Yellen|Collins]] (2021) applied Seila Law to another single-director agency. Those cases turned on a single head; Slaughter turns on executive power, so the number of heads no longer matters. The opinion effectively restores the reasoning of [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] (1926) and relies on [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] (2010) and on the later cases treating litigation ([[Buckley v. Valeo|Buckley]]), agency rulemaking ([[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]]) and statutory interpretation ([[Bowsher v. Synar|Bowsher]]) as executive.

Three exceptions remain. [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] is not overruled, and its rule for inferior officers with limited duties is untouched. [[Wiener v. United States|Wiener]] (1958), the only case applying Humphrey's, involved "a solely adjudicatory body," and Part III-B reserves non-Article III courts. And the Federal Reserve is reserved on a historical rationale tied to the First and Second Banks, the institutions whose constitutionality [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] upheld (Class 17). [[Trump v. Cook]], decided the same day, took up the reservation and held the Fed's protection constitutional.

Slaughter and Cook are the Class 7 Canvas readings, following Class 6's [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]], [[The Decision of 1789]], [[The Federalist No. 70]] and [[Buckley v. Valeo]]. Part II-A is an originalist argument built from the Convention, The Federalist, and the First Congress; Part II-B is a precedent argument; the stare decisis section is where the two meet.

## Class layer

### Posture

- The District Court (D.D.C.) granted Slaughter summary judgment, declared the removal ultra vires, and permanently enjoined interference with her duties; a divided D.C. Circuit denied the Government a stay pending appeal.
- The Supreme Court stayed the District Court's order and granted certiorari before judgment (2025); argued December 8, 2025; decided June 29, 2026.

### Facts

When President Trump began his second term in January 2025, the FTC had two Republican and three Democratic commissioners. He designated a new Chair, and the former Chair later resigned. In March 2025 he fired the two remaining Democratic commissioners, Rebecca Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya, citing no statutory cause and relying instead on his authority under Article II. Slaughter sued the President and other officials for declaratory and injunctive relief restoring her to office, alleging that the removal was ultra vires and violated the APA and the Constitution. The District Court recognized that [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] generally allows at-will removal of executive officers but held that [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] carved out an exception for the FTC.

### Issue

- Does the FTC Act's limit on removing commissioners to "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" violate the separation of powers?
- Does [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] require a different answer, and if so, should it be overruled?

### Holding

- Yes. The FTC exercises executive power through rulemaking, adjudication and enforcement suits, so its commissioners must be removable at will; the removal limit is unconstitutional. Reversed and remanded.
- No, and to the extent it would, it is overruled. What survives of Humphrey's is only the observation that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the removal rule; the stare decisis factors all favor overruling anything more.

### Rule

- The President "may remove his subordinates at will"; "those who fall within the President's 'general administrative control' must be removable by the President at will."
- The test is the character of the power, not the structure of the agency: rulemaking, investigation and in-house adjudication, and "the 'discretionary power to seek judicial relief'" are all executive.
- There is no category of "quasi-legislative" or "quasi-judicial" power: when an agency "executes" a statute against private parties, "it exercises executive power—no ifs, ands, or quasis about it."
- Open: non-executive offices, the Federal Reserve, and non-Article III judges.

### Reasoning

**(Roberts, C.J., for the Court)**

- **Text and structure.** Article II vests "[t]he executive Power" in one President and charges him to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." One President meant a hierarchy of "assistants or deputies" who "derive their offices from his appointment" and remain "subject to his superintendence" (The Federalist No. 72).
- **Why unity.** The Framers had watched state governors who were "little more than chairmen of their executive boards" and rejected that model. James Wilson answered the charge that unity was "the foetus of monarchy": unity was "the best safeguard against" monarchy. Hamilton in [[The Federalist No. 70]]: plurality in the executive "tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility."
- **Removal as a corollary.** Unity would be "destroyed" if the executive power were vested "ostensibly in one man, subject in whole or in part to the control and co-operation of others" (Federalist No. 70). "Text and structure thus both taught that the President had to be able to remove those who fail to live up to their duties, lest he fail to live up to his."
- **The Decision of 1789.** Three camps: Senate consent (Gerry), congressional choice (Sedgwick), and removal as part of "the Executive power" that "the Legislature has no right to diminish or modify" (Madison). Madison's side won. The House deleted "to be removable by the President" so as not to appear to confer the power, and substituted language assuming it, to "establish a legislative construction of the Constitution."
- **Liquidation.** Marshall, Kent and Story treated the question as settled; Washington through John Quincy Adams accepted it; even Jackson's critics (Webster) conceded it was "settled by construction, settled by precedent, settled by the practice of the Government, and settled by statute."
- **Precedent.** [[Ex parte Hennen]] (1839) and [[Parsons v. United States|Parsons]] (1897) reaffirmed the point, and [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] struck down Senate participation in removing a postmaster, following the "constitutional construction . . . reached by the First Congress."
- **Humphrey's, then and now.** Sutherland held the FTC's duties "neither political nor executive, but predominantly quasi-judicial and quasi-legislative." That was "a highly circumscribed and almost fictional view of the FTC's role." [[Buckley v. Valeo|Buckley]] (litigation), [[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]] (delegated rulemaking) and [[Bowsher v. Synar|Bowsher]] (interpretation) later put each function on the executive side, and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] conceded in footnote 28 that the FTC's powers were executive "at least to some degree."
- **Stare decisis.** Quality: only "blind feats of definition" made executive powers "quasi," and Justice Jackson's "counterpane to conceal a disordered bed" (dissenting in [[FTC v. Ruberoid Co|Ruberoid]]) captures it. Consistency: Humphrey's was applied only once, in [[Wiener v. United States|Wiener]]. Workability: "no one knows how to apply Humphrey's in practice." Lower courts split over whether it covers all multimember agencies, only partisan-balanced ones, or only agencies "materially indistinguishable from the 1935 FTC." Reliance: not "legitimate."
- **Application.** The FTC administers "some 80 statutes" and makes rules (the power to "flesh out such statutory regimes . . . is executive through and through"), adjudicates in-house, and sues for penalties and restitution. "[T]his is not a close case."
- **Limits (Part III-B).** Not every congressionally chartered entity has executive power (the Boy Scouts, Georgetown University). Myers "placed 'the greatest weight'" on early practice, and the First and Second Banks "influenced monetary policy and neither of which were subject to plenary Presidential control." "Our prior cases do not necessarily implicate the constitutionality of such arrangements. Our opinion today should not be read to do so either."
- **The closing.** "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President." "The answer, then as now, is that these officers exercise the President's power, not their own."

### Dissent / concurrence

- **Gorsuch, J., concurring.** Agrees with the holding ("With all this, I agree") but presses its consequences. Independent agencies exercise delegated "legislative and judicial powers" as well as executive ones, and "after today's decision, the President can effectively exercise all those powers too." He asks whether Congress would have delegated so much had it known the President would control the agencies, how Congress will respond, "[a]nd what, if anything, will this Court do about it?" He traces the independent agency to Woodrow Wilson and James Landis, whose "[p]ublic administration" model rejected the "simple tripartite form of government." The thrust is a call to revive [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle|nondelegation]] ([[Gundy v. United States|Gundy]]) now that removal is settled. Only the opening pages of the concurrence were retrievable.
- **Sotomayor, J., dissenting (joined by Kagan and Jackson, JJ.).** The dissent's text was not retrievable. Its positions, as the majority quotes and answers them, are:
  - Stare decisis and ninety years of congressional reliance on independent agencies (post, at 34–40).
  - There is "no evidence that those who shaped or ratified the Constitution adopted the . . . general rule of at-will removal" (post, at 14). On the majority's account, the dissent would reject [[Myers v. United States|Myers]], [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] and [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] as well as the majority's reading of 1789 ("sweeps the chess pieces off the board").
  - The First Congress settled only a default rule (post, at 13, 21), and Federalist Nos. 39 and 77 contemplated legislative regulation of tenure (post, at 15–17).
  - Founding-era multimember bodies and modern practice support independence.
  - [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] "applied and expanded Humphrey's" (post, at 7).
  - The majority's reply: "At least we have accorded Humphrey's a respectful burial; the dissent would cast Myers aside without a second thought."

### Cold-call notes

- **What is left of Humphrey's?** Only its "observation" that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" need not be removable at will. That is [[Mistretta v. United States|Mistretta]]'s "branchless" agency (Scalia, dissenting there), not the FTC. The overruling is conditional in form ("[i]f anything more is left") but total in effect for any agency that enforces a statute.
- **Distinguishing fact from Humphrey's.** Humphrey's rested on the Court's characterization of the 1935 FTC as quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial. Slaughter does not say the FTC changed so much as that the characterization was always wrong. Morrison n.28 said the 1935 FTC's powers would "at the present time be considered 'executive.'" The operative facts are the same; the legal classification changed.
- **Distinguishing fact from Seila Law and Collins.** Those cases invalidated protections for single directors and preserved multimember commissions by treating Humphrey's as a multimember-expert-body exception. Slaughter drops the structural line: number of heads is irrelevant once the agency wields executive power. Be ready to say that Collins's structure-over-power approach is, in a sense, reversed. The question now is power, as in Humphrey's own "character of the office" test.
- **Tension with Myers.** Myers involved a statute giving the Senate a role in removal, which the Decision of 1789 rejected. The FTC Act gave Congress no role, only limited the grounds. Slaughter treats the difference as immaterial: a for-cause limit, like a Senate veto, leaves officers exercising "the President's power" who are not responsible to him. Pressing the point: is a cause requirement (which leaves the President the sole remover) really the same intrusion as Senate participation?
- **Tension with Morrison.** Slaughter uses Morrison's footnote 28 against Humphrey's but does not touch Morrison's holding for inferior officers. Hypo: Congress gives an inferior officer in the FTC (say, an ALJ) for-cause protection removable by the commissioners. Morrison governs, and [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] bars two layers. After Slaughter, the commissioners are at will, so a single layer below them may survive under Morrison. Scalia's Morrison dissent (Humphrey's "swept . . . into the dustbin") is now the Court's view of Humphrey's, though not yet of Morrison.
- **Vary the agency.**
  - A multimember commission that only investigates and reports to Congress: no executive power (Buckley, 424 U.S. at 137–138), so it falls outside the rule and could be independent.
  - A purely adjudicatory claims body ([[Wiener v. United States|Wiener]]): arguably not executive; the Court calls Wiener's constitutional analysis "one brisk paragraph" and does not decide it.
  - Tax Court or Court of Federal Claims judges: expressly reserved.
  - NLRB or MSPB members: they enforce statutes and adjudicate, so Slaughter's logic makes them removable at will.
  - The Federal Reserve Board: reserved "to the extent" it follows the First and Second Bank tradition; [[Trump v. Cook]] upheld its protection.
- **The Federal Reserve carve-out.** The rationale is historical, not functional. Myers gave "the greatest weight" to early congressional "exposition," and the First Congress chartered a bank that was not under "plenary Presidential control." Test it against the opinion's own premises: if the Board makes rules and brings enforcement actions like the FTC, why does history save it? The majority's answer is the conditional "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition." That leaves open whether the Board's bank-supervision and consumer-regulation functions are within the tradition or only monetary policy is. Thomas declined to join Part III-B; his dissent in [[Trump v. Cook|Cook]] argues that the Banks "possessed no sovereign power" and the Board is an executive agency.
- **McCulloch connection (Class 17).** [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] upheld Congress's power to charter the Second Bank under the Necessary and Proper Clause. Slaughter and Cook turn the Banks into Article II evidence as well: the First Congress created a national bank not controlled by the President. Note the irony that the opinion also quotes Marshall in [[Osborn v. Bank of the United States|Osborn]], a Bank case, for the proposition that the Constitution "vests the whole executive power in the President." Question to be ready for: was the Bank an executive officer at all, or a private corporation serving public ends? If the latter, the Fed carve-out rests on an analogy between a private corporation and a federal agency.
- **Originalist premises.** The majority's case is Federalist 70 (unity, "conceal faults and destroy responsibility") plus the Decision of 1789 as liquidation. The contest is over 1789. On the majority's reading the First Congress recognized a constitutional power, so "default" and "indefeasible" are "one and the same." On the dissent's reading it chose a default that Congress may vary. Madison's "no right to diminish or modify" is the best evidence for the majority. Laurance and Sedgwick's congressional-choice view, and Hamilton's "displace" in Federalist No. 77, are the best evidence against. Compare the Class 6 materials: [[The Decision of 1789]] note sets out the four House positions.
- **Doctrine versus original meaning.** For ninety years the Court's answer (Humphrey's) and the originalist answer (Myers, 1789) diverged; Slaughter closes the gap for executive agencies and reopens it for the Fed, which is saved by history rather than by text. Baude's two questions ("what the Supreme Court has said" and "what the Constitution actually requires") now come apart mainly at the Fed and the non-Article III courts.
- **Formalism and functionalism.** Humphrey's was formalist in method with a functionalist fourth box; [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] was openly functional; Slaughter is formalist with one box ("executive power belongs to the President"). The stare decisis section is functional in a different way, stressing workability and the political reality that independence yields "increased subservience to congressional direction." Gorsuch's concurrence shows the formalist cost: if the President now controls delegated quasi-legislative power, the [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle|nondelegation]] problem sharpens.
- **Remedy.** The grant of certiorari also posed whether a court may prevent a removal from office. The majority holds the removal lawful and does not separately resolve that question. [[Trump v. Cook|Cook]] answers it for protected officers: equity may protect a de facto officer pending litigation.
- **Memorable lines.** "[A] result in search of a rationale"; "no ifs, ands, or quasis about it"; "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere"; "a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power"; "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."
