## Snapshot

The President fired two Federal Trade Commissioners without alleging statutory cause; one sued to be restored to office and won below, and the Court stayed that ruling and granted certiorari before judgment. It held the FTC Act's for-cause removal provision contrary to the separation of powers, and left [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] standing at most as an observation about bodies exercising no executive power. Article II's Vesting and Take Care Clauses run against the FTC Act's removal limit.

## Issue

- Does the FTC Act's limit on removing commissioners to "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" violate the separation of powers?
- Does [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] require a different answer, and if so, should it be overruled?
- **Argued, not decided.** May a court prevent a removal from office, the second question on which certiorari was granted?

## Rule

An officer who exercises executive power must be removable by the President at will, whether the agency has one head or five. Rulemaking, investigation, in-house adjudication and enforcement litigation are each executive, so a for-cause limit protecting an officer who wields any of them is unconstitutional. What survives of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] is the observation that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the rule.

- The President "may remove his subordinates at will"; those within his "general administrative control" must be removable at will.
- The character of the power governs, not the structure of the agency; even "the 'discretionary power to seek judicial relief'" is executive.
- There is no "quasi-legislative" or "quasi-judicial" power; an agency executing a statute against private parties "exercises executive power—no ifs, ands, or quasis about it."
- "If anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it."

## Operative facts

- **Since 1914 the FTC Act has allowed removal of a commissioner only "for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office," and [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] upheld that clause in 1935.** *The same words are at issue, so this is a direct challenge to the precedent, not an extension of it.*
- **The modern Commission administers "some 80 statutes": it makes binding rules, investigates, adjudicates in house, and sues in federal court for penalties and restitution.** *Decisive, because each power is executive under the modern cases; a body that only advised Congress could still fit what is left of Humphrey's.*
- **The FTC is a five-member commission with a partisan-balance requirement.** *Irrelevant after this case: Seila Law turned on the single agency head, Slaughter on the kind of power exercised.*
- **January 2025: the second Trump term opened with two Republican and three Democratic commissioners; the President designated a new Chair, and the former Chair later resigned.** *That leaves the two Democrats as the only check on control of the agency.*
- **March 2025: the President removed those two, Rebecca Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya, telling them their service was "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities" and invoking Article II.** *No statutory ground was alleged, so the statute was squarely violated and the constitutional question could not be avoided.*
- **Slaughter sued for declaratory and injunctive relief restoring her to office, alleging that the removal was ultra vires and violated the APA and the Constitution.** *She sought her office rather than back pay, which is what puts the remedial question on the table.*
- **The District Court granted her summary judgment and enjoined interference with her duties, holding that *Myers* allows at-will removal of executive officers but that Humphrey's carved out an exception for the FTC.** *That exception is the only thing holding the judgment up.*
- **A divided D.C. Circuit denied the Government a stay, after which the Supreme Court stayed the District Court's order and granted certiorari before judgment.** *Slaughter was out of office while the appeal ran, so the Court reached the merits.*
- **Argued December 8, 2025; decided June 29, 2026, the same day as [[Trump v. Cook]].** *One opinion day produces the rule and its single historical exception.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (6–3; Roberts, C.J., for the Court; Thomas joined all but Part III-B; Gorsuch concurring; Sotomayor dissenting, joined by Kagan and Jackson)

- **Unconstitutional.** The FTC's for-cause removal provision "is contrary to the separation of powers enshrined in the Constitution." Reversed and remanded.
- **Humphrey's goes.** Every stare decisis factor "counsels in favor of letting Humphrey's go," and "[i]f anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it."
- **Remedy not reached.** Part III-B reserves offices with no executive power, "the Federal Reserve, to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks," and tenure protection for judges of "non-Article III courts."

**Roberts, C.J., for the Court**

- **Text and structure.** Vesting the executive power in one President "was to establish a hierarchy" of assistants "subject to his superintendence," and "[t]o remain accountable to the President, those officers must be removable by the President."
- **1789 and liquidation.** Madison's position — removal is part of the executive power, which "the Legislature has no right to diminish or modify" — prevailed; Marshall, Kent and Story treated the question as settled; and *Myers* confirmed it.
- **Humphrey's, then and now.** Sutherland's "predominantly quasi-judicial and quasi-legislative" FTC was "a highly circumscribed and almost fictional view of the FTC's role," and [[Buckley v. Valeo|Buckley]], [[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]] and *Bowsher* later put litigation, delegated rulemaking and statutory interpretation on the executive side.
- **Stare decisis.** Only "blind feats of definition" made executive powers "quasi"; Humphrey's was applied once, in *Wiener*; and "no one knows how to apply Humphrey's in practice."
- **Application.** The power to "flesh out such statutory regimes . . . is executive through and through," so "this is not a close case." "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."

**Gorsuch, J., concurring**

- Agrees with the holding ("With all this, I agree") but presses its consequences: independent agencies also exercise delegated legislative and judicial powers, and "after today's decision, the President can effectively exercise all those powers too."

**Sotomayor, J., dissenting (joined by Kagan and Jackson, JJ.)**

- Her text was not retrievable. As the majority reports it, there is "no evidence that those who shaped or ratified the Constitution adopted the . . . general rule of at-will removal." The reply: "At least we have accorded Humphrey's a respectful burial; the dissent would cast Myers aside without a second thought."

## Arguments rejected

- *Humphrey's held that Congress may protect FTC commissioners, and Congress has built dozens of independent agencies on that holding for ninety years.* Roberts: Congress relied on Humphrey's to create agencies "insulated from presidential control," and "[t]hat is precisely the problem."
- *The Decision of 1789 settled only a default removal power for cases of congressional silence.* Roberts: "The two, however, are one and the same" — removal lies as far outside Congress's control as the pardon or the veto.
- *The Federalist contemplated limits: tenure as a subject of legal regulation (No. 39), and Senate consent "to displace as well as to appoint" (No. 77).* Roberts: "two passing comments" cannot override the logic of The Federalist as a whole.
- *Practice deserves weight, from the First Congress's multimember bodies to the modern for-cause commissions.* Roberts: those early members "were removable by the President at will," and "[w]e have never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power."
- *Courts should review removal limits only for reasonableness, and Morrison "applied and expanded Humphrey's."* Roberts: that principle "is neither limiting nor much of a principle," and *Morrison* abandoned the "quasi" categories rather than extending them.

## Where it sits

**The end of a line that turned on structure.** *Seila Law* (2020) confined Humphrey's to "its own terms" and *Collins* (2021) applied it to another single-director agency, both resting on the single agency head. Slaughter rests on the character of the power instead, so the number of heads no longer matters.

**What survives.** *Morrison* is not overruled, and its rule for inferior officers with limited duties is untouched. *Wiener* (1958), the only case ever to apply Humphrey's, involved "a solely adjudicatory body," and Part III-B reserves the judges of non-Article III courts.

**The exception rests on banks.** The Federal Reserve is reserved on a historical rationale tied to the First and Second Banks, whose constitutionality [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] upheld in Class 17. [[Trump v. Cook]], decided the same day, took up the reservation and upheld the Governors' protection.

**Method.** Part II-A argues from the Convention, The Federalist and the First Congress, Part II-B from precedent. These Class 7 readings follow Class 6's [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]], [[The Decision of 1789]], [[The Federalist No. 70]] and [[Buckley v. Valeo]].

## Cold-call notes

The casebook notes for this reading were not available; these questions are drawn from the opinion.

- **What is left of Humphrey's?** Only its "observation" that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" need not be removable at will. The overruling is conditional in form and total in effect for any agency that enforces a statute.
- **Is the case distinguished from Humphrey's on the facts?** No. Humphrey's rested on a characterization of the 1935 FTC as quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial, and Slaughter says that characterization was always wrong — *Morrison*'s footnote 28 had already conceded the powers would "at the present time be considered 'executive.'"
- **Is a cause requirement really the same intrusion as Senate participation?** *Myers* involved a statute giving the Senate a role in removal; the FTC Act gives Congress no role and limits only the grounds. Slaughter treats the difference as immaterial, because either way officers exercising "the President's power" are not answerable to him.
- **Does *Morrison* still protect anyone?** Its holding for inferior officers with limited duties is untouched. Hypo: Congress protects an FTC administrative law judge from removal by the commissioners. *Morrison* governs, *Free Enterprise Fund* bars two layers, and because the commissioners are now at will, a single layer below them may survive.
- **Vary the agency.**
    - A commission that only investigates and reports to Congress exercises no executive power ([[Buckley v. Valeo|Buckley]], 424 U.S. at 137–138), so it may still be independent.
    - A purely adjudicatory claims body (*Wiener*) is arguably not executive; the Court calls its constitutional analysis "one brisk paragraph" and decides nothing.
    - Tax Court and Court of Federal Claims judges are expressly reserved.
    - NLRB and MSPB members enforce statutes and adjudicate, so they are removable at will.
- **Why does history save the Fed when text condemns the FTC?** *Myers* gave "the greatest weight" to early congressional exposition, and the First Congress chartered a bank not under "plenary Presidential control." The conditional "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition" leaves open whether bank supervision counts or only monetary policy. Thomas, who declined to join Part III-B, argues in [[Trump v. Cook|Cook]] that the Banks "possessed no sovereign power."
- **Where is the originalist contest?** Over 1789. For the majority the First Congress recognized a constitutional power, so default and indefeasible are "one and the same"; for the dissent it chose a default Congress may vary. Madison's "no right to diminish or modify" is the best evidence one way, Hamilton's "displace" in Federalist No. 77 the best the other.
- **Formalism or functionalism?** Humphrey's was formalist in method with a functionalist fourth box, *Morrison* was openly functional, and Slaughter is formalist with a single box, though its stare decisis section is functional in stressing workability. Gorsuch shows the cost: presidential control of delegated quasi-legislative power sharpens the nondelegation problem ([[Gundy v. United States|Gundy]]).
- **Memorable lines.** "[N]o ifs, ands, or quasis about it"; "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere"; "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."
