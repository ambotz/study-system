## What it is

Optional pre-quarter background, pp. 19–27: the English and colonial antecedents of the Constitution, the Articles of Confederation, and the Philadelphia Convention. It explains why the Constitution's features were live choices in 1787 rather than inevitable ones.

## What to notice

- **Four distinctive features.** Separation of powers among co-equal branches; federalism; a written bill of rights; and popular sovereignty through representative government. The Constitution was the first written constitution to create a national government, to be adopted by the people, and to be designed as supreme law binding all who exercise power.
- **The English inheritance.** The Glorious Revolution of 1688 and Locke's social contract gave the colonists the premise that legitimate government rests on consent, including a right to alter or abolish it. The English constitutional documents run from the Charter of Liberties (1100) and Magna Carta (1215) to the Bill of Rights (1689) and the Act of Settlement (1701), which gave judges life tenure.
- **The decisive difference.** None of the English documents was supreme law, none recognized the sovereignty of the people, and none limited Parliament, which could change the constitution by ordinary statute. In the United States, sovereignty rests in the people, the Constitution is supreme (Art. VI), and it can change only through Art. V.
- **Text versus tradition.** In England, written documents are "islands of text in a sea of unwritten tradition." In the United States, the text is "a great continent" with "scattered lakes and ponds and rivers of tradition." Hence the two central questions: what does the text mean, and who interprets it?
- **Colonial charters as a proto-constitution.** Horizontal division between royal governors and elected lower houses (proto-separation of powers); vertical division between London and the colonies (proto-federalism).
- **The state constitutions reacted to executive tyranny.** Governors lost the veto, appointment and pardon powers and served short terms. Power concentrated in the legislatures.
- **The failure of the Articles of Confederation.**
  - A league of sovereign states: "Each state retains its sovereignty" (Art. II).
  - A unicameral Congress with one vote per state; members paid and recallable by their states.
  - No power to tax directly; revenue depended on state requisitions, and Georgia "never paid a cent."
  - No power over interstate or foreign commerce.
  - Nine of thirteen states needed for significant measures; unanimity for amendment.
  - Marshall: "congress was a corps of ambassadors" who "could, by their own power, execute nothing."
- **The crisis.** Shays' Rebellion (1786–87), inflationary paper money, debtor-relief legislation and an army of some 625 men. By 1786 the experiment seemed on the verge of failure.
- **The five decisions at Philadelphia.**
  - Greatly expanded national power: commerce, tax and spend, and direct operation on citizens.
  - The Great Compromise: equal representation in the Senate, population in the House.
  - Separate executive and judicial branches, with a single, independently chosen President who has a veto.
  - The three-fifths compromise and the fugitive slave clause, "the great tragedy in the work of the framers."
  - Ratification by nine states (Art. VII), contrary to the Articles' unanimity rule.
- **The legality question the casebook presses.** Was ratification an unlawful amendment of the Articles, a revolution, or states withdrawing from a treaty? The Federalist Nos. 40 and 43 (after this range) are Madison's defense.
