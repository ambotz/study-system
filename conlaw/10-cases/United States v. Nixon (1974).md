## Snapshot

A grand jury investigating Watergate indicted seven of the President's former aides and named the President an unindicted coconspirator. The Special Prosecutor subpoenaed tapes of the President's conversations for their trial, the President moved to quash on executive privilege and lost, and the case came up on certiorari before judgment. The Court held the privilege constitutionally based but qualified: a generalized claim of confidentiality yields to a specific need for criminal evidence. Article II's executive power meets Article III's duty to do justice.

## Issue

- Is the order reviewable, and is a dispute between the President and a Special Prosecutor serving under an Executive regulation justiciable?
- Did the subpoena satisfy Rule 17(c)'s relevancy, admissibility and specificity?
- Is the privilege absolute, so that the President alone decides whether his communications must be produced?
- If qualified, does it yield to a subpoena for evidence in a pending criminal trial?

## Rule

The President has a constitutionally based privilege for confidential communications, and those communications are presumptively privileged, but the privilege is qualified rather than absolute. The courts, not the President, decide its scope, because the judicial power cannot be shared with the Executive. A claim resting on military, diplomatic or national-security secrets draws far greater deference, and an Executive that has delegated authority by regulation is bound by that regulation while it stands.

- The privilege is "fundamental to the operation of Government and inextricably rooted in the separation of powers," which justifies "a presumptive privilege for Presidential communications."
- "[N]either the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances."
- "[T]he legitimate needs of the judicial process may outweigh Presidential privilege"; in the full opinion, "[t]he generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."
- "So long as this regulation remains in force the Executive Branch is bound by it."

## Operative facts

- **After the 1972 Watergate break-in, the Acting Attorney General appointed a Special Prosecutor under a Justice Department regulation giving him "plenary authority" over offenses arising out of the 1972 election and "allegations involving the President."** *The delegation makes this a case rather than an internal argument; a dispute with an ordinary at-will subordinate would have been the President's alone to resolve.*
- **The regulation gave him "full authority . . . to contest the assertion of 'Executive Privilege,'" barred the Attorney General from countermanding his decisions, and allowed removal only "for extraordinary improprieties" and only with the "consensus" of eight congressional leaders — a limit adopted on the President's own assurances.** *Decisive on justiciability: the Executive had bound itself, and the Court holds it to that.*
- **In March 1974 a grand jury indicted seven men, including Mitchell, Haldeman and Ehrlichman, for conspiracy to defraud the United States and to obstruct justice, and named the President an unindicted coconspirator.** *It tied the President's own conversations to the charged conspiracy, which bears on relevance under Rule 17(c).*
- **The Special Prosecutor obtained a Rule 17(c) subpoena for tapes and documents of "precisely identified meetings" between the President and others, returnable before the September 9 trial date.** *The evidence was sought for someone else's criminal trial, which is why the holding is confined to criminal prosecutions.*
- **The President's counsel moved to quash, asserting an absolute privilege and arguing that the dispute was an intra-branch matter and a political question under *Baker v. Carr*.** *Every theory is about who decides rather than about what the tapes contained.*
- **The claim of privilege rested on a generalized interest in confidentiality and nothing more.** *Decisive: there was no claim "to protect military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets." Change this fact and the balance changes with it.*
- **The District Court (Sirica, J.) denied the motion to quash and ordered the materials delivered for in camera examination.** *The privilege is protected by a judge's private review rather than defeated by public disclosure, which keeps the intrusion small enough to justify.*
- **The Court granted cross-petitions for certiorari before judgment, heard argument on July 8 and decided on July 24, 1974, issuing the mandate "forthwith."** *The compressed schedule ran alongside the House Judiciary Committee's impeachment inquiry.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (unanimous, 8–0; Burger, C.J., for the Court; Rehnquist, J., who had served in the Nixon Justice Department, took no part)

- **Appealable.** Forcing the President to defy the order and risk contempt just to obtain review "would be unseemly."
- **Justiciable.** "The mere assertion of a claim of an 'intra-branch dispute,' without more, has never operated to defeat federal jurisdiction."
- **Rule 17(c) satisfied** (summarized by the editors).
- **The courts decide the scope of privilege, and the privilege is not absolute.**
- **The privilege yields here.** The materials go to the District Court for in camera examination. Affirmed.

**Burger, C.J., for the Court**

- **Appealability.** The usual route to review of a subpoena is to refuse and be held in contempt. For a President that route is "peculiarly inappropriate": it would present "an unnecessary occasion for constitutional confrontation between two branches," and whether a President can be held in contempt "could itself engender protracted litigation."
- **Justiciability.** "[C]ourts must look behind names that symbolize the parties" (*United States v. ICC*). The proceeding is a federal prosecution "brought in the name of the United States as sovereign"; the Attorney General conducts it by statute and delegated it by regulation.
- **The regulation has the force of law.** Under *Accardi* (1954), an officer who delegates discretion by regulation "denie[s] himself the authority to exercise" it while the regulation stands. The Attorney General could revoke it, "[b]ut he has not done so," so the Executive Branch, "and indeed the United States as the sovereign composed of the three branches," is bound.
- **Who decides.** Each branch "must initially interpret the Constitution," and that interpretation "is due great respect," but [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]] settles that "[i]t is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." The Court construes express immunities (Speech or Debate, *Gravel*) and so may construe implied ones.
- **The judicial power cannot be shared.** It "can no more be shared with the Executive Branch than the Chief Executive, for example, can share with the Judiciary the veto power."
- **Source of privilege.** Two grounds: the need for candor ("those who expect public dissemination of their remarks may well temper candor with a concern for appearances") and the separation of powers. The privilege derives "from the supremacy of each branch within its own assigned area of constitutional duties."
- **Why not absolute.** "Absent a claim of need to protect military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," confidentiality is not "significantly diminished" by in camera production. An absolute privilege would "plainly conflict with the function of the courts under Art. III," because "the separate powers were not intended to operate with absolute independence."
- **Respect for the President.** The duty to resolve the conflict "does not free the Judiciary from according high respect to the representations made on behalf of the President" (*Burr*, Marshall, C.J., on circuit, 1807).
- **The balance** (summarized by the editors). The privilege "must be considered in light of our historic commitment to the rule of law," weighed against "the fair administration of criminal justice." In the full opinion the Court finds the Fifth and Sixth Amendment interests in "full disclosure of all the facts" decisive, and doubts that "advisers will be moved to temper the candor of their remarks by the infrequent occasions of disclosure."
- **Reserved** (full opinion). Civil litigation, "congressional demands for information," and state secrets.

## Arguments rejected

- *The order is not final; the President must defy it and be held in contempt to get review.* Burger: requiring that of a President "would be unseemly," and a federal judge "should not be placed in the posture of issuing a citation to a President."
- *This is an intra-branch dispute between a superior and his subordinate, like a dispute between two congressional committees, and the President's word is final on what evidence is used.* Burger: names do not decide justiciability, and the regulation, which has the force of law, gave the Special Prosecutor power to contest the privilege.
- *The demand is a political question because Article II textually commits the privilege to the President.* Burger: the Court construes claims under express powers and so may construe claims to powers derived from them.
- *The separation of powers makes each branch the final judge of its own privilege.* Burger: the judicial power "can no more be shared with the Executive Branch" than the President can share the veto with the courts.
- *The Executive's independence "within its own sphere" (citing [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]]) insulates the President from a judicial subpoena.* Burger: independence does not mean the branches "operate with absolute independence," and an absolute privilege would impede the courts' duty "to do justice in criminal prosecutions."

## Where it sits

**Between Youngstown and Trump.** [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] asked whether the President could act without Congress; Nixon asks whether he can withhold evidence from the courts. Nixon cites Youngstown alongside *Powell v. McCormack* as instances of the Court invalidating another branch's action.

**The judicial half of a removal in fact.** The tapes the Court compelled were released in early August 1974, and Nixon resigned on August 9 before the House voted on the articles his Judiciary Committee had approved. Compare [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]], where the political process alone failed.

**The Executive bound by itself.** The Special Prosecutor was shielded only by a revocable regulation, and the Court held the Executive bound while it stood. Congress later created a statutory independent counsel, upheld in *Morrison v. Olson* (1988). [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] and [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] treat control of prosecution and removal as core executive power, straining how far such a regulation can bind the President.

**Cited both ways in 2024.** The Trump majority quotes Nixon for the Executive's "exclusive authority and absolute discretion" over prosecution, and reads it as rejecting only an absolute privilege against producing evidence. Both Trump opinions cite Nixon n.16: "the silence of the Constitution on this score is not dispositive."

**The settings reserved.** *Nixon v. Fitzgerald* (1982) gave absolute civil immunity for official acts; *Clinton v. Jones* (1997) denied temporary immunity for unofficial conduct; *Trump v. Vance* (2020) allowed a state criminal subpoena for private records; *Trump v. Mazars* (2020) set its own test for congressional subpoenas.

## Cold-call notes

These questions follow the casebook's Notes 1–5 in the Canvas excerpt.

- **The caption (Note 1).** How can the United States sue the President, who heads the branch that represents the United States? The opinion's answer is the regulation: the Executive divided itself, and "the United States as the sovereign composed of the three branches" is bound by that division. Push it further: if the President disobeyed, could he be held in contempt and jailed like any litigant? The Court avoided that question by making the order appealable — the contempt route was "unseemly" precisely because no one wanted to answer it.
- **Justiciability and judicial power (Note 2).** Are the Court's reasons for deciding persuasive? Its argument runs from express immunities (Speech or Debate) to implied ones: if courts construe the first, they construe the second. The alternative is leaving privilege to the political branches — subpoena, refusal, and impeachment for refusal, which is roughly what the House Judiciary Committee was already doing. Be ready to say whether that process would have been better or worse.
- **Who controls whom (Note 3).** Does the President control the Attorney General? Does the Attorney General control the Special Prosecutor? Is the regulation "a law"?
    - The Court says the regulation "has the force of law" under *Accardi*, but only "[s]o long as this regulation is extant" — the Attorney General could revoke it.
    - The removal limit was the President's own promise, routed through his subordinate, so Nixon is about the Executive binding itself, not Congress limiting removal.
    - That is how it survives [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]]. But it sits badly with [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump]], which calls removal of the Attorney General "conclusive and preclusive." If removal is that core, can a regulation the President approved bind him at all?
    - October 1973 is the counterexample: Nixon had the first Special Prosecutor, Cox, fired, and the regulation was rescinded and reissued.
- **Compliance (Note 4).** At argument it was unclear whether Nixon would obey, and Justice Marshall pressed his lawyer on it. Nixon obeyed. Why? Not because the Court had any means of enforcement; the likelier answers are unanimity (Rehnquist out, all three Nixon appointees in the majority), the pending impeachment, and public opinion. Compare [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]], which avoided issuing an order the Executive might ignore.
- **"Bad Presidents make bad law" (Note 5).** Paulsen, a casebook coauthor, argues Nixon is bad law because the facts were bad: "Richard Nixon was a crook," so the Court built a doctrine "harmful to the institution of the Presidency." Do you agree?
    - The strongest version: the privilege's scope should not depend on who claims it, and the opinion's balancing invites exactly that.
    - The reply: the holding is narrow — a generalized claim, in camera review, a criminal trial — and every reserved setting (national security, Congress, civil suits) was later decided on its own terms.
    - Note the parallel with [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump]]: its majority says it must not fixate on "transient results," and its critics say it is the same mistake in reverse.
- **Vary the claim.** Had the President asserted "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," the opinion signals much more deference. A congressional demand is reserved (*Mazars* later answered it); a civil demand is reserved (*Clinton v. Jones* later let the suit proceed).
- **Nixon against Trump v. United States.** Nixon makes a President surrender evidence for someone else's trial under a balance. [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump]] holds that a former President cannot be prosecuted for core acts and is presumptively immune for other official acts. The distinguishing facts are the defendant and the burden: the President himself, facing a prosecution rather than a subpoena.
- **Originalism against doctrine.** The privilege is not in the text; the Court infers it "from the nature of enumerated powers," much as [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]] infers implied congressional powers. Be ready to say whether an implied presidential privilege is more or less legitimate than McCulloch's implied powers.
- **Formalism and functionalism in one opinion.** The judicial-power holding is formalist: courts say what the law is, and that power cannot be shared. The privilege holding is functional: confidentiality is weighed against criminal justice. Contrast [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]] and Black in Youngstown, which refuse to balance.
- **Memorable lines.**
    - "[U]nseemly" (Nixon, Burger, on forcing a President into contempt).
    - "[T]he United States as the sovereign composed of the three branches is bound" (Nixon, Burger).
    - "[P]resumptive privilege . . . inextricably rooted in the separation of powers" (Nixon, Burger).
    - "[B]ad Presidents make bad law" (Paulsen).
