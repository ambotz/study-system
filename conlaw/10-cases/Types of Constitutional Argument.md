## What it is

The casebook's list of five types of constitutional argument, adapted from Philip Bobbitt's
modalities in Constitutional Fate (1982). It recurs throughout the book, and the notes to
[[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] ask you to identify which types each opinion
uses. The syllabus says Baude will test both what the Court has said the Constitution means and
what the Constitution actually requires; these five types are the tools for the second.

## What to notice

- **Text.** The meaning of the words "in their literary context, to informed readers," as enacted.
  Evidence includes contemporaneous writings, dictionaries, and legal texts the drafters borrowed.
  A **term of art** has an established technical legal meaning, and prior legal usage matters most
  there.
- **Historical context.** The setting, the problem the text was meant to solve, and the intentions
  or goals of the drafters. Sources are the ratification debates, The Federalist and the
  Convention records. For later amendments, such as the Reconstruction Amendments, the relevant
  history is different: those framers were reacting to earlier understandings of the Constitution.
- **Structure.** Three variants:
  - the relationship of provisions to one another;
  - a provision's location (with the powers, or with the constraints on powers);
  - the institutional arrangement the Constitution creates as a whole.
- **Precedent and practice.** Earlier interpretations by the courts **or the other branches**. A
  single case or episode, or a practice "sufficiently early, long, or settled" to "fix" or
  "liquidate" the meaning of an ambiguous provision. The rationale given counts, not just the
  result.
- **Consequences.** Which reading produces the best results on a stated view of the good
  (egalitarian justice, autonomy, efficiency, natural law). Usually argued at a higher level of
  abstraction: whether it is desirable for Congress to have this kind of power, not whether this
  statute is good policy. Consequences often combine with structure or practice.
- **The questions the casebook leaves open**, which are the questions to have a view on:
  - Are all five legitimate?
  - How should they rank against one another?
  - Do some draw their content from others?
  - Are all five always in play, or do some apply only when others run out?
- **"Liquidation"** is Madison's term (The Federalist No. 37) for settling ambiguous meaning by
  practice. It is Baude's own scholarly subject, and it will recur in the removal and war-powers
  classes.
