## What it is

The casebook's editorial note closing Part C, which follows the Mellon notes and runs to the start of Part D on the political question doctrine. It states the modern standing test that grew out of [[Ex parte Levitt]] and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Mellon]], illustrates each element with a case, and adds the two timing doctrines, mootness and ripeness.

It is assigned because the older cases state the principle ("direct injury," not an interest "common to all members of the public") and this note states the test a modern court applies. The three-part formulation is usually cited to [[Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife]] (1992), which the note does not name.

## What to notice

- **The test.** Standing "has hardened into a three-part test": plaintiffs must show "a concrete injury"; that the injury "is fairly traceable to the defendant"; and that it "would be redressable by a favorable ruling." These three "must be satisfied in every single federal case."
- **Injury and traceability: [[Clapper v. Amnesty International]] (2013, 5–4).** Lawyers and organizations challenging FISA's warrantless surveillance of some overseas communications lacked standing. The threatened injury "was too speculative," since it was unclear whose communications were targeted, and it was not "fairly traceable" to the statute, since the government might rely on "some other legal authority."
- **Redressability: [[Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment]] (1998).** An organization alleging "only past infractions . . . and not a continuing violation or the likelihood of a future violation" could not get an injunction, because "injunctive relief [would] not redress its injury."
- **Damages redress the past.** "By contrast, past violations of legal rights can be redressed with damages, even with nominal damages," per [[Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski]] (2021). A plaintiff who loses on Steel Co. for an injunction may still have standing to seek nominal damages for the same past violation.
- **Who versus when.** Standing asks "who"; mootness and ripeness ask "when." All three ask "is there a case or controversy that a federal court can resolve?" from different angles.
- **Mootness defined.** A case is moot "when the requisite personal injury that gave rise to the case or controversy ceases for some reason to exist": death of a party, settlement, or repeal or expiration of the challenged action.
- **[[Powell v. McCormack]]: not moot.** The Congress that excluded Powell had ended and the next one had seated him, but his claim to back salary kept the case alive.
- **[[DeFunis v. Odegaard]] (1974): moot.** A law-school applicant rejected allegedly on the basis of race was admitted provisionally under a preliminary injunction and was about to graduate when the case was argued. "[N]othing concrete turned on resolving the constitutional issue." The dissenters pointed to the chance something might still go wrong ("failing a final, perhaps?").
- **The three mootness exceptions.** Collateral consequences (Powell's back pay); injuries "capable of repetition as to that party, but likely to evade final judicial review"; and voluntary cessation by a defendant who "remains free to resume it later." There is no exception because a case is "especially interesting or important."
- **Ripeness: a two-part test.** First, "the hardship to the parties from withholding court consideration," usually shown by (a) a likelihood of prosecution with substantial consequences unless the plaintiff gives up allegedly lawful conduct, (b) certain enforcement not yet begun, or (c) substantial collateral hardship. Second, "the fitness of the issues and of the record for judicial review": the more "purely legal" the question, the likelier it is ripe.
- **Apply the test to Levitt.** Levitt fails on injury: his interest is generalized. A litigant whose case Black decided would have injury (the judgment), traceability (Black's vote) and redressability (vacatur). The three elements restate Levitt's "direct injury" rule and explain who could have raised the question.
- **Apply the test to Mellon.** Frothingham fails on injury and traceability: her tax bill cannot be traced to one appropriation, and enjoining it would not measurably lower her taxes, so redressability fails too. Massachusetts fails on injury: it lost nothing by declining the grant.
- **Apply the test to Burwell.** The House's claimed injury was institutional: loss of its constitutional role if money is spent without an appropriation. Traceability and redressability were easy (the Secretaries' payments, an injunction against them). The contested element was whether an institutional injury to a legislative chamber is "concrete" at all; see [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] and [[Raines v. Byrd]].
- **Apply mootness to Burwell.** When the Trump administration stopped the cost-sharing payments in 2017 and the parties settled, the appeal was dismissed. Voluntary cessation by the executive, coupled with settlement, is exactly how inter-branch suits tend to end without an appellate ruling.
- **Is standing constitutional or prudential?** The note treats the three elements as Article III requirements, but they appear nowhere in the text. The originalist question is whether "Cases" and "Controversies," read with the 1793 correspondence, require an injured plaintiff, or only an adverse, real dispute; private qui tam actions and relator suits at the founding are the usual evidence for the looser view.
- **The tension with Tocqueville.** Tocqueville praised the case requirement for keeping review obscure and particular. The modern three-part test is how the Court enforces that requirement, yet it leaves clauses like the Ineligibility Clause with no enforcer at all. The cost Tocqueville admitted ("it may happen that no one cares to bring it") is now a doctrinal rule.
