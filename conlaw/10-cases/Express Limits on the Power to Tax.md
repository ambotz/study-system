## What it is

The casebook's opening note to the taxing-power unit (Paulsen, McConnell, Bray & Baude, 4th ed., pp. 596–597). It sets out the constitutional text on taxation, including Article I, § 2's and § 9's apportionment rules, the Uniformity Clause, the Export Clause and the Sixteenth Amendment. It then explains why the difference between a "direct" tax and every other tax matters, and sets up [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]]. It is assigned to fix the mechanics before the cases. The taxing power returns in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] (Class 23), where the individual mandate survives as a tax.

## What to notice

- **The taxing power comes first in the list.** Taxation is "the first power listed in the Article I, Section 8 enumeration of eighteen national legislative powers." The casebook opens with [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s line: "the power to tax is the power to destroy."
- **The Constitution states four express limits.** 
	- Direct taxes must be apportioned, a rule stated in both Art 1. § 2 Cl. 3 and Art 1. § 9. Cl. 4
	- "Duties, Imposts and Excises" must be uniform. Article I, Section 8, Clause 1
	- Taxes on exports are banned. Article I, Section 9, Clause 5
	- Since 1913, income taxes are exempt from apportionment (16th amendment)
- **Uniformity and apportionment are two different rules.** 
	- Uniform tax: same rate in every state. 
	- Apportioned tax: same average amount per person in every state (per capita amoount payment equal, per state). Note: the state itself can tax different people inside its state differently, but apportionment requires the state per-capita amount to reflect equally, in proportion to other states. 
- **The two rules are "mutually inconsistent."** In the whiskey-still example, Wisconsin has twice Maryland's stills and about the same population. A flat $10-per-still tax is uniform, but Wisconsin pays twice as much per person, so the tax is not apportioned. A $10 rate in Wisconsin and a $20 rate in Maryland is apportioned, but the rates differ, so it is not uniform.
- **Only a capitation tax satisfies both rules.** The casebook asks "(Can you see why?)". A head tax has the same rate everywhere, so it is uniform. <u>Its yield from each state is rate times population, so each state's share tracks its census count automatically</u>, which is apportionment. Any tax on something distributed unevenly relative to population breaks one rule or the other.
- **Classification decides everything.** Once a tax is classed as "direct," apportionment is mandatory, and for most things other than heads apportionment is unworkable. The fight over the meaning of "direct" is really a fight over which taxes Congress can levy at all.
	- Direct = apportionement = hard to tax (besides capitation/person-based tax) because it would require different rates in different states to achieve the same per-capita effect universally.
- **Hylton was a contrived test case.** The facts were stipulated. The United States "apparently paid Hylton's attorneys." The parties stipulated that Hylton owned 125 carriages, "which was almost certainly untrue," because that number reached the jurisdictional minimum. Alexander Hamilton argued for the tax. 
	- Keep this in mind when reading the case as precedent on judicial review: the first challenge to an act of Congress, seven years before [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury]], was not a genuinely adversarial dispute.
