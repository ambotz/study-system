## What it is

The casebook's clause-by-clause overview of Article III, opening Part III ("Article III: The Judicial Power," Assignment 17), followed by the short introduction to Part A ("Judicial Review") that sets up the Brutus–Hamilton exchange and [[Marbury v. Madison]]. It is the map for the whole judicial-power unit: judicial review (Class 11), [[Dred Scott v. Sandford|Dred Scott]] (Class 12), departmentalism (Class 13), justiciability (Class 14) and congressional control of the courts (Class 15).

## What to notice

- **"The judicial Power" is a law-applying power.** Historically it was "the authority to decide cases of a judicial character in accordance with pre-existing governing law," and so "a law-applying power (and a law-interpreting and law-recognizing power), but not a law-making power." The casebook concedes the "inevitable reality" that applying a rule to new facts partly makes it. Hamilton's "neither Force nor Will, but merely judgment" and Marshall's "say what the law is" both rest on this conception.
- **Justiciability doctrines all grow out of the ban on advisory opinions.** Standing ("something must turn on the court's decision"), ripeness, mootness and adversity each rest on the idea that a court speculating about a dispute that is not live "would merely be giving advice." The "Cases" and "Controversies" language of § 2 reinforces the point. These are Class 14: [[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]] and [[The Correspondence of the Justices]].
- **The political question doctrine is contested at its root.** Matters the Constitution leaves "to political judgment or discretion," or on which it supplies no rule of law, are outside the judicial power. The casebook asks whether the doctrine is principled restraint "commanded by the Constitution's separation of powers" or "activist judicial restraint." Its seed is Marbury's line between acts "only politically examinable" and ministerial duties on which individual rights depend.
- **The supremacy question is posed before Marbury is read.** Madison in The Federalist No. 49: "neither of them, it is evident, can pretend to an exclusive or superior right of settling the boundaries between their respective powers." Hamilton in [[The Federalist No. 78]] calls the judiciary "the least dangerous." Yet judicial supremacy "has become a widespread view." Keep the distinction from the start: judicial review (courts decide constitutionality in cases) is not judicial supremacy (everyone must treat the courts' view as the Constitution). Class 13 turns on it.
- **The Madisonian Compromise.** Article III leaves to Congress whether to create lower federal courts at all, a compromise between those who wanted "a complete system of national courts" and those who wanted only a Supreme Court. Congress has always created some; the Judiciary Act of 1789 made sixteen district judgeships. A consequence: many federal questions are decided in state courts, reviewable by the Supreme Court under [[Martin v. Hunter's Lessee]] (1816) and [[Cohens v. Virginia]] (1821).
- **Congress sets the size of the Supreme Court.** Article III is silent; the number has ranged from six to ten and has been nine for most of the past century. Roosevelt's 1937 plan produced "a political backlash," and no one has tried since ([[Senate Report on Court Packing]], Class 15). The power over the lower courts is the power [[Stuart v. Laird]] tests: may Congress abolish courts whose judges hold office "during good Behaviour"?
- **Tenure and salary protect independence; impeachment is the reserved check.** Hamilton in The Federalist No. 81 answered the fear of judicial "usurpations" with impeachment: "This is alone a complete security." That is the answer to Brutus's complaint that judges cannot be removed "for making ever so many erroneous adjudications." [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (Class 15) tests whether the security is real.
- **Section 2's nine heads of jurisdiction.** The first three ("Cases") are defined by subject: federal law, ambassadors, admiralty. The last six ("Controversies") are defined by party and have an interstate or international aspect.
- **Menu or command?** Is § 2 a list of cases federal courts must hear, or a menu Congress may draw from? Two features point to "menu." First, if Congress need not create lower courts, it may create them with limited jurisdiction ([[Sheldon v. Sill]], 1850); until 1875 lower federal courts had no general federal-question jurisdiction. Second, the Exceptions Clause lets Congress withdraw cases from the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction, as [[Ex parte McCardle]] (1868, Class 15) seems to accept.
- **The "not both" position.** Some argue Congress may limit the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction or the lower courts' existence and jurisdiction, "but not both." Test it: Congress strips all federal courts of jurisdiction over a class of federal constitutional claims. On the "menu" view, state courts decide finally and may give different "final" answers in different states; on the "not both" view, the statute is invalid because some federal court must have the last word.
- **The original-jurisdiction clause is the clause Marbury construes.** Original jurisdiction covers cases "affecting Ambassadors, other public Ministers and Consuls, and those in which a State shall be Party"; everything else is appellate, "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." Notice that the Exceptions Clause attaches only to appellate jurisdiction. Marshall reads the original list as a ceiling Congress cannot raise; the Exceptions Clause is the strongest textual hint that the allocation is not wholly fixed.
- **Jurisdiction is not lawmaking.** A grant of jurisdiction "does not ordinarily confer a power to create the substantive law." Since [[Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins]] (1938), diversity cases are governed by state law. The same law-applying conception of judicial power.
- **Procedural protections close the article.** Jury trial, venue, and the Treason Clause's two-witness rule. Marshall uses the two-witness rule in Marbury as a rule "addressed especially to the courts."
- **The casebook's list of checks on the judiciary.** Appointment; Congress's control over the creation of lower courts and the size of the Supreme Court; Congress's control of jurisdiction and budget; and impeachment, "the blunt, difficult, rarely used, and controversial last resort." Classes 11–15 take each in turn.
- **Marbury was not the Court's first constitutional stand.** Before 1803 the Justices had refused to advise Washington on questions not presented in a case ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]); in [[Hayburn's Case]] (1792) they objected to deciding pension claims subject to executive revision; and in [[Hylton v. United States]] (1796) the Court upheld the carriage tax against a constitutional challenge, where "no one questioned judicial review." Marbury is "the most famous exposition" of judicial review, not its invention.
- **The framing question.** "[W]hat should a reader of this exchange have expected the judicial power to be?" A good answer: both Brutus and Hamilton expected courts to refuse effect to unconstitutional statutes in the cases before them. They disagreed about whether that power would be checked and how much interpretive latitude judges would take.
