## Rule

A delegation of discretion is valid if Congress "shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to fix such rates is directed to conform" — the test every later delegation case applies.

## Facts

- **Congress authorized a person or body other than itself to fix rates.** *Rate-fixing was the delegated power, so the question was whether handing that power over was a forbidden delegation of legislative power.*
- **The statute supplied a principle to which the rate-fixer was directed to conform.** *That principle made the delegation valid; statutes supplying no "policy or standard" failed in the two 1935 cases.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Taft, C.J., for the Court)

- "If Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to fix such rates is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power."
- The rate-fixing delegation was sustained; the Court has found a delegation excessive only in the two 1935 cases.

**Rejected**

- *Fixing rates is legislative power, Article I, § 1 vests "[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress, and so Congress may not hand rate-fixing to anyone else.* Where Congress lays down an intelligible principle for the delegee to follow, the delegation "is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power."

## Context

The modern delegation doctrine rests on one sentence from Hampton. The [[Gundy v. United States]] plurality quotes that sentence (through *Mistretta v. United States*) as the governing standard, pairs it with the "general policy" and "boundaries of [his] authority" formulation of *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, and stresses that those standards "are not demanding." White's dissent in [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] quotes Taft and then concedes that "[i]n practice" restrictions on delegation "diminished and all but disappeared." Gorsuch's Gundy dissent attacks the intelligible-principle test as having "no basis in the original meaning of the Constitution, in history, or even in the decision from which it was plucked," and calls its later use a "misadventure."

The only statutes to fail the test are the two from 1935, [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] and *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan* ([[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]]). The founding-era ancestor of the test is Sedgwick's position in [[The Post Roads Debate]] that "the House should establish the principle, and then leave it to the Executive to carry it into effect."
