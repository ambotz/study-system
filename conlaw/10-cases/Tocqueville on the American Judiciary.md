## What it is

A passage from Alexis de Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* (1 Democracy in America 92, the 1839 third American edition of Henry Reeve's translation), in which the French observer explains the American judiciary to European readers. His puzzle: American judges look like judges anywhere, yet they wield "immense political power."

The casebook pairs the passage with [[The Correspondence of the Justices]] to connect the case requirement to the justification for judicial review. Tocqueville's answer is that American courts review laws only incidentally, in the course of deciding lawsuits, and that this limitation is what makes the power tolerable.

## What to notice

- **The puzzle.** A stranger "hears the authority of a judge invoked in the political occurrences of every day," yet the tribunals "offer nothing which is contrary to the usual habits and privileges of those bodies." Unlike the Parliament of Paris, which remonstrated and refused to register edicts, American courts have "carefully restricted its action to the ordinary circle of its functions."
- **First characteristic: a dispute.** "[R]ights must be contested in order to warrant the interference of a tribunal." A judge who attacks a law in a case "is in some measure obliged to decide upon the law in order to decide the case." But "if he pronounces upon a law without resting upon a case, he clearly steps beyond his sphere, and invades that of the legislative authority." That sentence is the theory behind the Justices' 1793 refusal.
- **Second characteristic: particular cases, not general principles.** A judge may destroy a general principle while "deciding a particular point." If he "directly attacks a general principle without having a particular case in view," he may exercise a "more useful" influence, "but he ceases to be a representative of the judicial power."
- **Third characteristic: passivity.** "The judicial power is by its nature devoid of action; it must be put in motion in order to produce a result." It "does not pursue criminals, hunt out wrongs, or examine into evidence of its own accord." This is the root of the rule that a court waits for a plaintiff with standing.
- **The source of American judicial power.** Nothing in the three characteristics differs from other nations. The difference is "the right of the judges to found their decisions on the constitution rather than on the laws," leaving them "at liberty not to apply such laws as may appear to them to be unconstitutional." Judicial review is a choice of law in a case, which is [[Marbury v. Madison]]'s argument: "to select that legal obligation by which he is most strictly bound is the natural right of every magistrate."
- **Why America and not France or England.** In France the constitution is "supposed to be" immutable; in England Parliament "is at once a legislative and a constituent assembly," so the constitution "does not in reality exist." The American constitution is "a detached whole" that binds the legislator as much as the citizen but "may be altered by the will of the people in predetermined cases, according to established rules." Supreme law plus an amendment process (Article V) is what makes judicial preference for the constitution coherent; compare [[The Federalist No. 78]].
- **A refused law is not abolished.** Once a judge refuses to apply a law, it "loses a portion of its moral cogency"; similar suits multiply "until it becomes powerless." "[A]lthough it is censured, it is not abolished; its moral force may be diminished, but its cogency is by no means suspended." Review operates on the case; the statute stays on the books. [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] says the same thing in 1923: the Court exercises only "the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment."
- **Why incidental review is better.** When a judge contests a law "in an obscure proceeding, the importance of his attack is concealed from the public gaze," and "legislation is protected from wanton assailants, and from the daily aggressions of party spirit." The judge "only judges the law because he is obliged to judge a case." Tocqueville calls this "at once the most favorable to liberty as well as to public order."
- **The admitted cost is the standing problem.** Judicial censorship "cannot extend to all laws indiscriminately," because some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit; and even when such a contestation is possible, it may happen that no one cares to bring it." [[Ex parte Levitt]] is the first half of that sentence and the Maternity Act in [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Mellon]] is a close cousin. "The Americans have often felt this disadvantage, but they have left the remedy incomplete, lest they should give it an efficacy which might in some cases prove dangerous."
- **Is the premise still true?** Tocqueville's defence depends on review being obscure and particular. Public-law litigation, class actions, organizational plaintiffs and nationwide injunctions make constitutional cases anything but "concealed from the public gaze." [[Trump v. CASA]] (2025) held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress gave the federal courts, a partial return to Tocqueville's picture of relief tied to the parties. The stronger the modern courts' remedies, the weaker Tocqueville's argument that review is harmless because it is incidental.
- **Vary the institution.** A constitutional court with abstract review (a legislator refers a bill for a ruling before it takes effect) is exactly what Tocqueville says a judge may not be: it "directly attacks a general principle without having a particular case in view." The American choice to have no such court is what the Justices enforced in 1793.
- **Supremacy or departmentalism?** Tocqueville's judge refuses to apply a law in one case, and the law weakens only through "the reiterated attacks of judicial functionaries." That is closer to the judgment-centred view than to judicial supremacy; compare the Class 13 material in [[Cooper v. Aaron]].
