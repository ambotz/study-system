## Rule

Congress may prohibit the interstate shipment of any article for any motive, subject only to the Constitution's specific prohibitions, and may regulate intrastate production (here wages and hours) as an appropriate means to make that prohibition effective; [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] is overruled, and the Tenth Amendment "states but a truism."

## Facts

- **The Fair Labor Standards Act barred interstate shipment of goods made by employees paid below the minimum wage or worked beyond the maximum hours.** *This is the Hammer statute's structure applied to all workers, so the case could not be decided without confronting Hammer.*
- **The Act also directly required the minimum wage (25 cents an hour) and maximum hours (44 a week) for employees engaged "in the production of goods for commerce."** *This second provision regulates the factory itself, the thing even Holmes conceded Congress could not touch directly; it forces the Necessary and Proper holding.*
- **The goods were lumber, harmless in themselves.** *Under Hammer's harmful-goods test the shipment ban would fail; Stone abandons the test.*
- **Georgia had chosen to leave wages and hours unregulated.** *The challenger's pretext argument turned on the Act overriding the policy of "the state which has elected to leave them unregulated"; Stone holds the commerce power "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or nonexercise of state power."*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (unanimous; Stone for the Court)

- The prohibition on interstate shipment of goods produced under substandard labor conditions is a regulation of commerce within Congress's "plenary" power. Motive and purpose "are matters for the legislative judgment upon the exercise of which the Constitution places no restriction, and over which the courts are given no control."
- [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] "was a departure from the principles which have prevailed in the interpretation of the Commerce Clause both before and since the decision." It "should be, and now is, overruled."
- The direct wage and hour requirements for employees producing goods for commerce are valid. The commerce power "extends to those activities intrastate which so affect interstate commerce or the exercise of the power of Congress over it as to make regulation of them appropriate means to the attainment of a legitimate end," citing [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].
- The Tenth Amendment does not limit the result. It "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered."

**Rejected**

- *The shipment ban is only nominally a regulation of commerce; its real purpose is to regulate wages and hours in manufacture, a subject reserved to the states, and to override Georgia's choice to leave them unregulated, rather than to aid the policy of the destination states as in the liquor and convict-goods cases.* Stone: the power is "complete in itself" ([[Gibbons v. Ogden]]); a regulation "is not a forbidden invasion of state power merely because either its motive or its consequence is to restrict the use of articles of commerce within the states of destination."
- *Hammer holds that Congress may exclude only articles with some harmful property, and precedent should control.* Stone: that distinction "was novel when made and unsupported by any provision of the Constitution" and "has long since been abandoned"; Hammer's "vitality, as a precedent, as it then had, has long since been exhausted."
- *Manufacture is not commerce, so Congress cannot set wages and hours in a lumber mill.* Stone concedes "manufacture is not, of itself, interstate commerce," but Congress "may choose the means reasonably adapted to the attainment of the permitted end even though they involve control of intrastate activities."
- *The Tenth Amendment reserves the regulation of local labor to the states.* Stone: the Amendment was "declaratory of the relationship between the national and state governments as it had been established by the Constitution," adopted "to allay fears," and has never deprived Congress of "all means for the exercise of a granted power which are appropriate and plainly adapted to the permitted end."

## Context

The bridge note "After Hammer v. Dagenhart" tells the path from 1918 to 1941. Congress's tax on child-labor profits failed in [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] (Class 21), and the Child Labor Amendment stalled. The Court then carried Hammer's reasoning into the New Deal. [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] (1935) struck down wage and hour codes for a Brooklyn slaughterhouse because their effect on commerce was only "indirect"; [[Carter v. Carter Coal Co|Carter Coal]] (1936) held that "Production is a purely local activity." After Roosevelt's court-packing plan (see [[Senate Report on Court Packing]], Class 15) and Justice Roberts's shift, [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]] (1937) upheld the National Labor Relations Act in a narrow 5–4 opinion by Hughes, author of the [[Houston E. & W. T. Ry. Co. v. United States|Shreveport Rate Case]].

Darby is where the shift becomes doctrine. It does three things: it adopts Holmes's [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] dissent on the shipment ban; it goes past Holmes by upholding direct regulation of production through a [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] means-end argument, the logic of the Shreveport Rate Case generalized; and it reads the Tenth Amendment as adding nothing to the enumeration. The unfair-competition argument Day rejected in Hammer becomes Stone's statement of the Act's purpose.

[[Wickard v. Filburn]] (1942) pushes Darby's second holding to production not intended for commerce at all, through aggregation. [[United States v. Lopez]] (1995) and [[Gonzales v. Raich]] (2005) (Class 19) sort the modern categories: channels (Darby's shipment ban), instrumentalities (Shreveport), and activities substantially affecting commerce (Darby's second holding and Wickard). The "truism" reading of the Tenth Amendment was tested in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] and then qualified by the anti-commandeering rule of [[Printz v. United States]] (Class 20), which rests on structure rather than the Amendment's text. [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] (Class 23) holds that the power to regulate commerce presupposes existing activity, and that a law may be "necessary" but not "proper" when it breaks with structural principles, a partial return to the idea that means must be tied to enumerated ends.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Federal criminal prosecution under the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 of a Georgia lumber manufacturer. The District Court quashed the indictment on the ground that the Act was unconstitutional as applied.
- Direct appeal by the United States; reversed.

### Facts

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set up "a comprehensive legislative scheme for preventing the shipment in interstate commerce of certain products and commodities produced in the United States under labor conditions as respects wages and hours which fail to conform to standards set up by the Act." Section 15(a)(1) made it unlawful to ship in interstate commerce goods "in the production of which any employee was employed in violation of section 6 or section 7"; sections 6 and 7 set, for the first year, a 25-cent minimum wage and a 44-hour maximum week without overtime pay for employees "engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce." Section 15(a)(2) made it unlawful to violate those wage and hour provisions directly. The defendant made lumber in Georgia, which had no wage and hour law of its own, and challenged both provisions.

### Issue

- Does Congress have power to prohibit the interstate shipment of lumber made by employees paid less than a prescribed minimum wage or worked more than prescribed maximum hours?
- Does Congress have power to prohibit the employment of workers in the production of goods "for interstate commerce" at other than prescribed wages and hours?
- Does the Tenth Amendment bar either regulation?

### Holding

- Yes. The shipment ban is a regulation of commerce, and its motive is irrelevant; [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] is overruled.
- Yes. Regulating the wages and hours of employees producing for commerce is an appropriate means, "reasonably adapted" to the legitimate end of excluding substandard goods from commerce and suppressing an "unfair" method of competition, even though it controls intrastate activity.
- No. The Tenth Amendment reserves only what was not granted; it does not limit the means of executing a granted power.

### Rule

- The commerce power "is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations other than are prescribed in the Constitution" ([[Gibbons v. Ogden]]).
- Congress's power "is plenary to exclude any article from interstate commerce subject only to the specific prohibitions of the Constitution."
- "Whatever their motive and purpose, regulations of commerce which do not infringe some constitutional prohibition are within the plenary power conferred on Congress by the Commerce Clause."
- The power "extends to those activities intrastate which so affect interstate commerce or the exercise of the power of Congress over it as to make regulation of them appropriate means to the attainment of a legitimate end."
- The Tenth Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered."

### Reasoning

**(Stone, J., for a unanimous Court)**

- **Shipment is commerce.** "While manufacture is not, of itself, interstate commerce, the shipment of manufactured goods interstate is such commerce, and the prohibition of such shipment by Congress is indubitably a regulation of the commerce."
- **Regulation includes prohibition.** The power "extends not only to those regulations which aid, foster and protect the commerce, but embraces those which prohibit it." The accepted categories (noxious articles, stolen articles, kidnapped persons, liquor, convict-made goods) are illustrations, not limits.
- **State policy is irrelevant to the scope of the federal power.** The commerce power "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or nonexercise of state power," and it is "no objection" that its exercise "is attended by the same incidents which attend the exercise of the police power of the states."
- **Motive is for Congress.** The Act's purpose, that "interstate commerce should not be made the instrument of competition in the distribution of goods produced under substandard labor conditions," is a legislative judgment the courts do not review.
- **Hammer overruled.** Decided "by a bare majority of the Court, over the powerful and now classic dissent of Mr. Justice Holmes," it "cannot be reconciled" with the conclusion reached. Its harmful-article distinction and its motive-and-effect thesis have both "long since" lost force.
- **Production regulated as a means.** Invoking [[McCulloch v. Maryland]], Stone reasons that Congress, having chosen to exclude substandard goods from commerce, "may choose the means reasonably adapted to the attainment of the permitted end even though they involve control of intrastate activities." The evil targeted is "the spread of substandard labor conditions through the use of the facilities of interstate commerce" and the resulting "dislocation of the commerce itself."
- **Tenth Amendment.** "There is nothing in the history of its adoption to suggest that it was more than declaratory of the relationship between the national and state governments as it had been established by the Constitution before the amendment."

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. The opinion is unanimous, as the casebook's "(!)" emphasizes: the Court unanimously adopted a theory of federal power that it had unanimously rejected 23 years earlier, since even the Hammer dissenters conceded Congress could not directly regulate production.

### Cold-call notes

- **What are the two holdings, and which is the bigger deal?** First, the shipment ban (channels of commerce) is valid whatever its motive. Second, direct regulation of wages and hours in production for commerce is valid as a means. The first is Holmes's dissent; the second goes beyond Holmes and is the foundation of [[Wickard v. Filburn]].
- **Note 2: Commerce Clause or Necessary and Proper Clause?** The passage about intrastate activities that "so affect interstate commerce" as to make their regulation "appropriate means to the attainment of a legitimate end" is McCulloch's test almost word for word ("legitimate end," "appropriate," "plainly adapted"), and Stone cites McCulloch at 421. It is a Necessary and Proper Clause holding in Commerce Clause clothing. It matters because the means-end framing implies limits (the end must be regulating interstate commerce, and the means must be "proper"), which is exactly the handle Scalia uses in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] and Roberts uses in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]].
- **Note 1: was Holmes's middle ground more persuasive?** Holmes: Congress may do by indirection (closing channels) what it may not do directly (regulating factories). Darby removes the incoherence by letting Congress do both. The cost is that the "direct intermeddling" limit disappears; the benefit is that the law no longer rewards drafting form. Consider which answer an originalist reading of "regulate Commerce" supports, and whether Stone offers any textual argument at all (he mostly offers precedent from [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] and McCulloch).
- **What becomes of McCulloch's pretext passage?** Stone treats motive as unreviewable; Marshall said a pretextual law would be struck down. After Darby, the only question is whether the law, on its face, regulates commerce or is an appropriate means to do so. Pretext survives only in the taxing cases ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]], Class 21) and, much later, in NFIB's distinction between a tax and a penalty.
- **Hypo: a shipment ban on goods made by non-union workers, aimed at forcing unionization in a right-to-work state.** Valid under Darby's first holding: motive is irrelevant and the state's contrary policy cannot diminish federal power. Under Hammer it fails as a pretextual regulation of local labor.
- **Hypo: a federal minimum wage for a lumber mill that sells only within Georgia.** Darby's second holding covers employees producing goods "for commerce," so a purely local seller is outside its reasoning. That gap is what [[Wickard v. Filburn]] closes with aggregation.
- **Tenth Amendment "truism."** Know the argument: the Amendment omits "expressly," as Marshall stressed in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]], and so reserves only what was not granted. The question is always whether the power was granted. The counter-argument that later prevailed in part: the structure of dual sovereignty limits how Congress may exercise granted powers against the states themselves ([[Printz v. United States]]), even if not whom it may regulate.
- **Child Labor Amendment (Note 3).** It had 28 of the 36 needed ratifications when Darby made it moot. Continuing would have entrenched the power against a future Court's reversal and given Darby's result a democratic mandate the Court's reinterpretation lacked; it also raises the question whether judicial reinterpretation is a substitute for Article V.
- **Why unanimous?** By 1941 Roosevelt appointees dominated the Court. The same unanimity recurs in [[Wickard v. Filburn]]; the next time the Court finds a commerce-power limit is [[United States v. Lopez]] in 1995.
- **Memorable lines.** "The amendment states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered"; "the powerful and now classic dissent of Mr. Justice Holmes"; "It should be, and now is, overruled."
