## Rule

States have no judicially enforced immunity from generally applicable Commerce Clause regulation for "traditional governmental functions"; [[National League of Cities v. Usery]] is overruled, and the States' protection lies principally in their role in the national political process, a limit "of process rather than one of result."

## Facts

- **The FLSA's wage and overtime rules applied to SAMTA exactly as to private employers.** *Blackmun: SAMTA "faces nothing more than the same minimum-wage and overtime obligations that hundreds of thousands of other employers, public as well as private, have to meet."*
- **SAMTA claimed immunity only as a government, not because its operations were local.** *Appellees conceded the commerce question, so the case tests state-as-state immunity alone.*
- **Lower courts had split badly on what counts as a "traditional governmental function."** *Airports and highway authorities were immune; mental health facilities and traffic regulation were not. The incoherence is Blackmun's main reason for overruling.*
- **San Antonio took over mass transit from private operators and ran it largely on federal subsidies.** *A once-private function now public shows why a historical test cannot capture what States legitimately do.*
- **The FLSA was the very statute National League of Cities had held inapplicable to States.** *Powell: nothing had changed but the Court's view; the case is a pure test of stare decisis.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (5–4; Blackmun for the Court)

- A rule of state immunity "that turns on a judicial appraisal of whether a particular governmental function is 'integral' or 'traditional'" is rejected "as unsound in principle and unworkable in practice."
- Courts have "no license to employ freestanding conceptions of state sovereignty when measuring congressional authority under the Commerce Clause." The States retain sovereignty "only to the extent that the Constitution has not divested them of their original powers and transferred those powers to the Federal Government."
- "State sovereign interests . . . are more properly protected by procedural safeguards inherent in the structure of the federal system than by judicially created limitations on federal power": state control of electoral qualifications, the Electoral College, and equal representation in the Senate.
- Applying the FLSA to SAMTA "contravened no affirmative limit on Congress' power under the Commerce Clause." Reversed; National League of Cities overruled.
- Reserved: "These cases do not require us to identify or define what affirmative limits the constitutional structure might impose on federal action affecting the States under the Commerce Clause."

**Rejected**

- *National League of Cities was never a list of functions; it was "a familiar type of balancing test," a "functional doctrine" protecting "core state functions," and courts can apply it like any other balancing test.* Blackmun: every candidate standard (historical, "uniquely" governmental, "necessary") is unmanageable, and any of them "inevitably invites an unelected federal judiciary to make decisions about which state policies it favors and which ones it dislikes."
- *The political process cannot be trusted to police its own limits; since [[Marbury v. Madison|Marbury]] it is the judiciary's province "to say what the law is," and the Court's rule makes "federal political officials . . . the sole judges of the limits of their own power."* Blackmun answers with the Framers' design: Madison and Wilson relied on the structure of the national government, and the States have in fact won grants and statutory exemptions.
- *The Seventeenth Amendment removed the Senate's role as the States' agent, so the procedural safeguard no longer exists.* Blackmun concedes the change "may work to alter the influence of the States" but holds the limit is still "one of process rather than one of result."
- *The Tenth Amendment reserves a sphere of state power that Congress may not enter.* Blackmun quotes Madison in the First Congress: "If the power was not given, Congress could not exercise it; if given, they might exercise it, although it should interfere with the laws, or even the Constitution of the States."

## Context

Garcia is the high-water mark of the view that federalism is enforced through politics, not courts. It closes the line that began with [[Maryland v. Wirtz]] (1968) upholding the FLSA's extension to state schools and hospitals, turned in [[National League of Cities v. Usery]] (1976), and was hollowed out by [[EEOC v. Wyoming]] (1983) and [[Transportation Union v. Long Island R. Co|Long Island Rail Road]] (1982) before Garcia overruled it. The casebook files it under "The Necessary and Proper Clause and State Sovereignty," directly after [[United States v. Comstock]], because O'Connor's dissent locates the limit in [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]'s requirement that means "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution."

Garcia has never been overruled, but the Court found another route. [[New York v. United States]] (1992) and [[Printz v. United States]] (1997) distinguish laws that regulate States alongside private parties (Garcia: permitted) from laws that command States to govern (forbidden). [[Gregory v. Ashcroft]] (1991) added a clear-statement rule for statutes that intrude on core state functions. [[United States v. Lopez]] (1995) revived judicially enforced limits on the commerce power itself. Justice Rehnquist's dissent (omitted in the casebook) predicted that the National League of Cities principle would again command a majority; the anti-commandeering cases are the partial fulfilment of that prediction.

The political-safeguards theory has an academic pedigree in the 1950s; O'Connor answers it with a 1954 observation that there was then a "burden of persuasion on those favoring national intervention," a burden that "has surely shifted." Stevens's [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] dissent quotes Garcia to argue that the political safeguards also answer commandeering, and cites the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 as proof they work.

## Class layer

### Posture

- SAMTA sued in federal district court for a declaratory judgment that National League of Cities barred application of the FLSA; Garcia, a SAMTA employee, intervened on the side of the Secretary of Labor.
- The District Court twice granted summary judgment for SAMTA (the second time after a remand in light of Long Island Rail Road); the Supreme Court heard the case on direct appeal and reversed.

### Facts

San Antonio regulated private transit from 1915 and began running a public system in 1959, which by 1970 depended on federal grants under the Urban Mass Transportation Act; by the time of the case, federal subsidies and local sales taxes covered about 75 percent of its budget. The FLSA as enacted in 1938 exempted state and local employees; Congress extended it step by step and reached mass-transit workers in 1974. SAMTA complied until [[National League of Cities v. Usery]] held in 1976 that the FLSA could not reach the States' "traditional governmental functions." In 1979 the Department of Labor opined that SAMTA was covered, and SAMTA sued.

### Issue

- Does the Tenth Amendment or the constitutional structure give state and local governments immunity from the FLSA's minimum-wage and overtime rules when they perform "traditional governmental functions" such as municipal mass transit?

### Holding

- No. The "traditional governmental functions" test is unworkable and inconsistent with federalism because it asks judges to rank state policies; the Constitution protects the States chiefly through their role in the federal political process, and a generally applicable wage law that treats SAMTA like any private employer breaches no affirmative limit.

### Rule

- No state immunity from Commerce Clause regulation turns on whether a function is "traditional," "integral," or "necessary" (Blackmun).
- "[T]he fundamental limitation that the constitutional scheme imposes on the Commerce Clause to protect the 'States as States' is one of process rather than one of result" (Blackmun).
- Any substantive restraint "must be tailored to compensate for possible failings in the national political process rather than to dictate a 'sacred province of state autonomy'" (Blackmun).
- Affirmative structural limits on federal action affecting the States are reserved, not denied.

### Reasoning

**(Blackmun, J., for the Court)**

- National League of Cities required four conditions; the third, whether the statute impairs "integral operations in areas of traditional governmental functions," produced lines no one could explain: "The constitutional distinction between licensing drivers and regulating traffic . . . is elusive at best."
- A historical test freezes the States in place and is "line-drawing of the most arbitrary sort"; a test of "necessary" governmental services would protect almost nothing, because a State can always "contract out."
- The deeper objection is democratic: the States "must be equally free to engage in any activity that their citizens choose for the common weal," and "the States cannot serve as laboratories for social and economic experiment if they must pay an added price" for taking up functions an earlier day left private.
- State sovereignty is what the Constitution leaves, not a pre-constitutional concept: Article I, § 10, Article I, § 8 with the Supremacy Clause, Article III review, and incorporation all subtract from it.
- The Framers protected the States through the structure of the national government: Federalist No. 46, No. 62 (equal Senate representation as "an instrument for preserving that residuary sovereignty"), No. 43, and Article V's guarantee of equal suffrage in the Senate.
- The process works: States draw large federal grants and have won exemptions from many Commerce Clause statutes. "The political process ensures that laws that unduly burden the States will not be promulgated."

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Powell, J., with Burger, C.J., and Rehnquist and O'Connor, JJ., dissenting)**

- The decision "effectively reduces the Tenth Amendment to meaningless rhetoric when Congress acts pursuant to the Commerce Clause," and "an unelected majority of five Justices" rejects "almost 200 years of the understanding of the constitutional status of federalism."
- National League of Cities was a balancing test, as [[EEOC v. Wyoming]] described it, not the function-labelling exercise the majority attacks.
- Stare decisis: the FLSA is the identical statute; Blackmun himself had called that result "necessarily correct," and no circumstance has changed.
- Members of Congress, once elected, "are Members of the Federal Government," and the Electoral College does not make the President the States' representative. "The States' role in our system of government is a matter of constitutional law, not of legislative grace."
- The holding makes "federal political officials, invoking the Commerce Clause, . . . the sole judges of the limits of their own power," contrary to [[The Federalist No. 78]] and [[Marbury v. Madison]].
- Federalist No. 45: federal powers "few and defined," state powers "numerous and indefinite." The States are a "counterpoise" because they hold citizens' loyalty through everyday services, and state and local government is more accessible and accountable than staff-drafted federal regulation.

**(O'Connor, J., with Powell and Rehnquist, JJ., dissenting)**

- [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]]'s test requires means that "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution"; the spirit of the Tenth Amendment is "that the States will retain their integrity," so it is "not enough that the 'end be legitimate'"
- Judicially enforced limits on regulating the States must "complement the judicially crafted expansion of the interstate commerce power."
- The Court "washes its hands of all efforts to protect the States"; the political process has not stopped federal rules on state officers' retirement ages or utility-commission agendas, so all that remains is Congress's "underdeveloped capacity for self-restraint."

### Cold-call notes

- **What exactly is the holding?** Narrowly: the FLSA validly applies to a municipal transit authority, and National League of Cities is overruled. Broadly: no judicially defined enclave of state functions is immune from generally applicable commerce regulation. Blackmun expressly reserves other "affirmative limits the constitutional structure might impose."
- **Is Garcia a political-question case in disguise? (Note 2)** It borrows both political-question factors: no "judicially manageable standards" (Parts II–III) and something like a commitment to Congress (the States' representation). It is not jurisdictional: the Court decides the merits and could strike down a law that shows a failure of process. Compare [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle|the nondelegation doctrine]], where the Court also pleads a lack of manageable standards; see [[Gundy v. United States]].
- **How would a court know the process failed? (Note 1)** Blackmun gives no criteria. The best reconstruction is procedural: a law passed in a way that bypasses state representation, or singles out States in a way the political process would not tolerate. That idea later surfaces as the anti-commandeering and clear-statement doctrines.
- **The Seventeenth Amendment. (Note 3)** Two readings: the People abandoned the Senate as the States' safeguard, so courts should defer; or removing the safeguard makes judicial enforcement more necessary. Blackmun acknowledges the change in one sentence and does not choose. Ask whether the amendment's proponents meant to weaken federalism or only to democratize the Senate.
- **Hypo: Congress applies a minimum wage only to state employees, not private ones.** Garcia's premise weakens: SAMTA won no immunity because it faced "the same" obligations as private employers. A law aimed at States alone is where the political-process argument is least convincing, and where later cases look for commandeering or discrimination.
- **Hypo: Congress orders SAMTA to set fares at a federal level and run federally designed routes.** That is no longer regulating the State as an employer; it directs how the State governs. Under [[New York v. United States]] and [[Printz v. United States]] that is commandeering, which Garcia does not authorize.
- **Hypo: Congress conditions transit grants on paying the federal minimum wage.** That is the spending route; see [[South Dakota v. Dole]]. Given how much of SAMTA's budget was federal money, Congress might never have needed the commerce power.
- **Stare decisis. (Note 5)** Nothing changed between 1976 and 1985 but Blackmun's vote. Rehnquist wrote National League of Cities and dissented here on stare decisis; in [[Planned Parenthood v. Casey]] the roles reversed on [[Roe v. Wade]]. Be ready to state a principled test (workability, reliance, doctrinal erosion) and apply it to both. Workability is Blackmun's strongest ground: he rests the overruling on eight years of incoherent lower-court results.
- **Formalism versus functionalism.** Blackmun rejects the function-based test as unprincipled yet puts in its place an empirical, process-based view of how Congress behaves. Powell defends balancing. Neither offers the text-based enclave that the Tenth Amendment's reservation might suggest; Scalia's [[Printz v. United States|Printz]] is the formalist answer that came later.
- **Original meaning versus doctrine.** Blackmun's Framers (Madison in Nos. 46, 62, 43; Wilson) rely on structure; Powell's Framers (No. 45) rely on separate spheres. Both are faithful to Madison, who argued both points. The textual difficulty for Powell: the Tenth Amendment reserves only powers "not delegated," and Garcia assumes the FLSA is within the commerce power.
- **Memorable lines.** Blackmun: "process rather than one of result"; "sacred province of state autonomy"; "the science of experiment." Powell: "meaningless rhetoric"; "a matter of constitutional law, not of legislative grace." O'Connor: "washes its hands"; "underdeveloped capacity for self-restraint."
