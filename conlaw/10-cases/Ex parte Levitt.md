## Snapshot

Congress let Justices retire on full salary while Hugo Black sat in the Senate; Van Devanter took the option, and Roosevelt gave Black the seat. Levitt, a lawyer, moved for an order requiring Black to show cause why he should be permitted to serve: the appointment was void under the Ineligibility Clause of Article I, § 6, cl. 2, and no lawful vacancy existed. One paragraph per curiam: a citizen and member of the Court's bar shows no direct injury, so the merits went unreached.

## Issue

- Does a citizen and member of the Court's bar have standing to challenge a Justice's appointment?
- **Argued, not decided.** Did the 1937 retirement statute increase the emoluments of the office during Black's Senate term, making him ineligible under art. 1, § 6, cl. 2?
- **Argued, not decided.** Was there a lawful vacancy, or did the statute create a new office closed to a sitting senator?

## Rule

A private individual invoking the judicial power against executive or legislative action must show a direct injury, sustained or immediately threatened; an interest shared with every citizen will not do, and membership in the bar of the Court adds nothing to it. The requirement holds however clear the violation and however high the office, so a clause protecting the public generally can go unenforced.

- "The motion papers disclose no interest upon the part of the petitioner other than that of a citizen and a member of the bar of this Court. That is insufficient."
- To "invoke the judicial power . . . he must show that he has sustained, or is immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury as the result of that action."
- "[I]t is not sufficient that he has merely a general interest common to all members of the public."

## Operative facts

- **On March 1, 1937, while Hugo Black was a senator for Alabama, Congress passed a statute allowing Supreme Court Justices to "retir[e] from active service on the bench" and keep their full salary.** *The statute is the whole case: no raise in salary, but an indirect increase through "the option of a lucrative pension," enacted during the term for which Black was elected.*
- **Justice Van Devanter retired under it, remaining an Article III judge without a new appointment or commission.** *Makes the vacancy itself contestable: either the retirement was unlawful, so no seat was open, or the statute created a new office, barred to a sitting senator.*
- **Roosevelt nominated Black; he and his Attorney General received extensive legal advice, and the Senate debated the constitutional question at length before confirming.** *The political branches did decide it, in Black's favour — Note 4 weighs that against the Court's silence.*
- **Levitt, a lawyer, moved in the Supreme Court for leave to file a petition for an order requiring Black to show cause why he should be permitted to serve.** *A head-on attack rather than a defense to something Black had done to Levitt. Raised defensively by a sanctioned party, the same claim would look like the appointments cases.*
- **The grounds were that the appointment and confirmation "were null and void by reason of his ineligibility under article 1, § 6, cl. 2," and "because there was no vacancy for which the appointment could lawfully be made."** *Both grounds are stated and neither examined.*
- **The motion papers "disclose no interest upon the part of the petitioner other than that of a citizen and a member of the bar of this Court."** *The decisive fact. Change it — a litigant whose case Black helped decide — and the result changes.*
- **The Court disposed of the motion in a one-paragraph per curiam resting on *Fairchild v. Hughes* (1922) and Massachusetts v. Mellon (1923).** *Treated as settled: the citation is the reasoning.*
- **Black then sat for 34 years, and the question "was only raised a few more times, and the Court never ruled on it."** *The ruling ended the matter rather than deferring it; the clause was left to the political branches.*

## Holding and reasoning

**Held** (per curiam, on a motion for leave to file, so there was no argument on the merits; no dissent or concurrence in the excerpt; Black, whose seat was at issue, is not recorded as taking part)

- **No standing.** Levitt's interest as a citizen and a member of the bar of the Court "is insufficient." A private individual must "show that he has sustained, or is immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury," and "a general interest common to all members of the public" is not enough.
- **The merits are not reached.** The Court says nothing about whether the 1937 statute increased the emoluments of the office, and nothing about whether a lawful vacancy existed.

**Per curiam**

- **The grounds are recited, then set aside.** The opinion states both constitutional theories — ineligibility under art. 1, § 6, cl. 2, and the absence of a vacancy — before turning to the papers. Nothing in it engages either.
- **The holding turns entirely on the petitioner's interest.** What the papers "disclose" is the only fact that matters: being a citizen, and being a member of the Court's bar. The second adds nothing to the first. Levitt's professional position — he practises before the Court and is bound by its judgments in other people's cases — is folded into the public's interest without separate discussion.
- **The rule is "an established principle," not a new one.** The authorities are *Fairchild v. Hughes* (1922), where citizens had "only the right, possessed by every citizen, to require that the government be administered according to law," and Massachusetts v. Mellon (1923), cited at 262 U.S. 488, which refused a State and a federal taxpayer the same kind of claim. The injury requirement is stated in the disjunctive — sustained, or "immediately in danger of sustaining" — and is the ancestor of the modern injury, traceability and redressability test.
- **The consequence is left standing.** Because the Ineligibility Clause protects the public at large, its violation injures no one in particular, so no private party can raise it. The per curiam neither denies that nor names a substitute enforcer. The doctrine filters not weak claims but a kind of claim: the more public the violation, the less likely anyone shows the particularized harm the rule demands.

## Arguments rejected

- *A member of the bar of the Supreme Court has a real and distinctive stake in the lawful composition of the Court before which he practises; and if a clear constitutional disqualification cannot be raised by him, it cannot be raised by anyone.* The Court: the interest is shared with "all members of the public," and the judicial power may be invoked only by someone directly injured. *Fairchild v. Hughes* and Mellon (262 U.S. at 488) settle it. The unenforceability objection goes unanswered.
- *The appointment is void on the face of the statute: the 1937 Act increased the emoluments of the office during the term for which Black was elected, and the vacancy was either unlawful or a newly created office barred to a sitting senator.* Neither ground is refuted. The motion fails at the threshold, on the petitioner's interest alone, so these arguments are not rejected on their merits but held unaskable by this plaintiff.

## Where it sits

**The showcase of an unenforceable clause.** The Ineligibility Clause protects the public in general, so a violation injures no one in particular. Tocqueville admitted the cost: some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).

**The contrast with the appointments cases.** Note 2 lines it up against [[Buckley v. Valeo]], *NLRB v. Noel Canning*, *Morrison v. Olson* and *Seila Law*. In each the challenger was a party the officer was regulating, investigating or suing. Structural provisions get enforced as a defense by someone the government is acting against; Levitt was not.

**The same result later.** *Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War* (1974) denied citizen and taxpayer standing to enforce the Incompatibility Clause; *Rodearmel v. Clinton* (D.D.C. 2009), on Secretary Clinton's appointment after a "Saxbe fix," went the same way.

**The standing unit.** The first standing case, after [[The Correspondence of the Justices]] establishes that there must be a case. [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Mellon]] explains why taxpayers and States fare no better; [[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]] gives the modern test that "direct injury" became. Black, a textualist, owed his seat to a textual problem no court would hear (compare his [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] opinion).

## Professor gloss

## Cold-call notes

**Note 1. "Consider the merits of the constitutional challenge avoided by the Court . . . Did Black's appointment to the Court violate the Emoluments Clause? . . . A stupid technicality? Both?"**

- For Black: the salary was untouched, and an emolument is "[t]he profit arising from office or employment" — a retirement option is profit on leaving. Against: a full-salary pension is earned by service, and the Clause's anti-self-dealing purpose reaches a benefit Congress votes itself.
- The vacancy argument proves too much: if Van Devanter's retirement created a new office, every retirement under the Act did. A technicality, but the kind the Clause is made of — "both" is the best answer.

**Note 2. "Why is that not enough? What would be enough? . . . Who would be in an equivalent position toward Justice Black? A lawyer threatened with discipline by the Court?"**

- Not enough because the injury is identical for everyone: the complaint is about how the government is run, and allowing it makes every citizen a plaintiff against every appointment.
- Enough: a litigant who lost a case Black's vote decided — injury, traceability and redressability all present. That is the Noel Canning and Buckley shape, the officer acting on the challenger; the threatened lawyer is the same.

**Note 3. "Hugo Black sat on the Court for 34 years, but the question was only raised a few more times, and the Court never ruled on it. Is that odd?"**

- Not odd once the holding is taken seriously: it did not postpone the question, it removed every plaintiff who could ask it. Thirty-four unreviewed years is the predictable output.

**Note 4. "Even if nobody with standing ever challenged Justice Black's appointment, does that mean that it was lawful, or just that it was unchallenged? . . . shouldn't he have been required to immediately consider, on his own, the constitutionality of his own appointment?"**

- Unchallenged, not lawful: non-justiciability relocates the question to the other interpreters, it does not answer it. Black's oath is an argument that he had to decide it himself — departmentalism in its uncomfortable form, since the only official positioned to decide is the beneficiary.

**Note 5. "Fairchild v. Hughes . . . 'Plaintiff has only the right, possessed by every citizen, to require that the government be administered according to law and that the public moneys be not wasted.' . . . Do you agree?"**

- That sentence does the work in Levitt: a right possessed by every citizen individuates nobody. Agree, and some constitutional commands are enforced only politically; disagree, and any citizen may litigate any appointment.

### Further drilling

- **Vary the plaintiff: Van Devanter.** A judge asserting his retirement was invalid and his seat never vacant has a stake in his own office; standing is satisfied and the result changes.
- **Vary the statute.** A citizen-suit provision would not help: injury, traceability and redressability "must be satisfied in every single federal case" ([[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]]).
- **Formal rule, political enforcement.** As bright a rule as the Constitution contains, enforced only by the branches it restrains. Is it less of a law?
- **Memorable.** One paragraph. "That is insufficient." Thirty-four years; the Court "never ruled on it."
