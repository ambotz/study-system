## Rule

A citizen and member of the Supreme Court bar has no standing to challenge a Justice's appointment: a private individual must show "a direct injury," actual or imminent, and "a general interest common to all members of the public" is not enough. Otherwise nothing new: an application of [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Massachusetts v. Mellon]] and [[Fairchild v. Hughes]] to the most prominent constitutional question of 1937.

## Facts

- **Levitt sought an order requiring Justice Black to show cause why he should be permitted to serve.** *The suit attacked the appointment head-on rather than as a defense to anything Black did to Levitt.*
- **The motion disclosed no interest "other than that of a citizen and a member of the bar of this Court."** *The decisive fact; a litigant whose case Black decided might stand differently (Note 2).*
- **The challenge rested on art. 1, § 6, cl. 2 and on the claim that "there was no vacancy for which the appointment could lawfully be made."** *Both grounds went unexamined; the merits never mattered.*
- **Congress passed the 1937 statute letting Justices retire on full salary while Black was a senator, and the seat opened when Van Devanter retired under it.** *That is why the challenge was not frivolous, and why the injury, if any, fell on everyone equally.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (per curiam)

- **No standing.** Levitt's interest as a citizen and bar member "is insufficient." To "invoke the judicial power to determine the validity of executive or legislative action," a private individual "must show that he has sustained, or is immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury as the result of that action and it is not sufficient that he has merely a general interest common to all members of the public."
- **The constitutional challenge is not reached.** The Court says nothing about whether the appointment violated Article I, § 6.

**Rejected**

- *A member of the Supreme Court bar has a real and distinctive stake in the lawful composition of the Court before which he practises, and if a clear constitutional disqualification cannot be raised by him, it cannot be raised by anyone.* The Court: that interest is shared with "all members of the public," and the judicial power may be invoked only by someone directly injured; [[Fairchild v. Hughes]] and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Mellon]] (262 U.S. at 488) settle it.

## Context

**The showcase of an unenforceable clause.** The Ineligibility Clause (the casebook calls it the "Emoluments Clause") protects the public in general, so its violation injures no one in particular. That is Tocqueville's admitted cost of incidental review: some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]). Note 3: Black sat for 34 years, the question "was only raised a few more times, and the Court never ruled on it."

**The contrast with the appointments cases.** Note 2 points back to [[Buckley v. Valeo]], [[NLRB v. Noel Canning]], [[Morrison v. Olson]] and [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau]]. In each, the challenger was a party whom the challenged officer was regulating, investigating or suing. Structural provisions are enforced when they are raised as a defense by someone the government is acting against; Levitt was not such a person.

**The same result later.** [[Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War]] (1974) denied citizen and taxpayer standing to enforce the Incompatibility Clause in the same sentence of Article I, § 6 against members of Congress holding reserve commissions. When President Obama appointed Senator Hillary Clinton Secretary of State although the office's salary had been increased during her Senate term (Congress rolled it back, a "Saxbe fix"), a Foreign Service officer's challenge, [[Rodearmel v. Clinton]], was dismissed for lack of standing by a three-judge district court (D.D.C. 2009).

**Where it sits.** The first standing case of the unit, after [[The Correspondence of the Justices]] establishes that there must be a case. [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Mellon]] then explains why taxpayers and States are no better placed than Levitt; [[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]] gives the modern test that Levitt's "direct injury" became. The irony that Black, a strict textualist, owed his seat to a textual problem no court would hear is worth remembering alongside his opinion in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Original motion in the Supreme Court by Albert Levitt for leave to file a petition for an order requiring Justice Black to show cause why he should be permitted to serve as a Justice.
- Disposed of in a one-paragraph per curiam opinion (1937) on standing alone.

### Facts

On March 1, 1937, while Hugo Black was a senator for Alabama, Congress passed a statute allowing Supreme Court Justices to "retir[e] from active service on the bench" and keep their full salary. Justice Willis Van Devanter retired under it; he remained an Article III judge. President Roosevelt nominated Black to the seat, and the Senate, after debating the constitutional question at length, confirmed him. Levitt, a lawyer, moved in the Supreme Court for an order against Black. He argued that the appointment and confirmation were "null and void" because Black was ineligible under art. 1, § 6, cl. 2 (the statute had increased the emoluments of the office during his Senate term), and because there was no lawful vacancy: either Van Devanter's retirement was invalid, or the statute had created a new office, to which a sitting senator could not be appointed.

### Issue

- Does a citizen and member of the Supreme Court bar have standing to challenge the validity of a Justice's appointment under Article I, § 6, cl. 2?

### Holding

- No. Levitt showed no interest beyond that of a citizen and bar member, and a "general interest common to all members of the public" does not satisfy the requirement of a direct injury, actual or imminent; the motion therefore failed without reaching the merits.

### Rule

- A private individual invoking the judicial power against executive or legislative action "must show that he has sustained, or is immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury as the result of that action."
- "[I]t is not sufficient that he has merely a general interest common to all members of the public."
- Membership in the bar of the Court adds nothing to a citizen's generalized interest.

### Reasoning

**(Per curiam)**

- The grounds of the motion are ineligibility under art. 1, § 6, cl. 2, and the absence of a vacancy.
- The papers "disclose no interest upon the part of the petitioner other than that of a citizen and a member of the bar of this Court. That is insufficient."
- The rule is "an established principle," resting on [[Fairchild v. Hughes]] (1922) and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon|Massachusetts v. Mellon]] (1923).

### Dissent / concurrence

- None in the casebook's excerpt; the Court spoke per curiam.

### Cold-call notes

- **What is the Ineligibility Clause?** Article I, § 6, cl. 2: "No Senator or Representative shall, during the Time for which he was elected, be appointed to any civil Office under the Authority of the United States, which shall have been created, or the Emoluments whereof shall have been increased during such time." Its purpose is to stop members of Congress from creating or sweetening offices for themselves.
- **Did the appointment violate the Emoluments half?** Webster (1828): an emolument is "The profit arising from office or employment." The 1937 Act did not raise the salary but gave a new option to retire on full pay, which the casebook calls an indirect increase through "the option of a lucrative pension." The argument for Black: the salary of the office was unchanged, and a retirement option is a benefit of leaving the office, not of holding it. The argument against: a pension earned by service is profit arising from the office, and the Clause's anti-self-dealing purpose covers it.
- **Did it violate the "created" half?** Van Devanter stayed an Article III judge without a new commission, so the challengers said either his retirement was illegal (no vacancy) or the statute created a new office (barred to a sitting senator). Note 1 asks whether this is "A stupid technicality? Both?" The best answer is that it proves too much: every retirement under the Act would then create a new office.
- **Why is being a citizen and bar member not enough (Note 2)?** Because the injury is identical for every citizen, the dispute is really about how the government is run, which belongs to the political branches. Standing preserves Tocqueville's point that courts decide constitutional questions only as needed to resolve someone's case.
- **Who would have standing?** The strongest candidate is a litigant who lost a case in which Black cast a deciding vote: injury (the judgment), traceability (Black's participation), redressability (vacatur and rehearing). That mirrors [[NLRB v. Noel Canning|Noel Canning]], where a company bound by an order of improperly appointed Board members could sue. A lawyer threatened with discipline by a Court including Black is similar. The remaining obstacle would be the merits and the de facto officer doctrine, not standing.
- **Vary the plaintiff: Van Devanter.** A judge claiming his retirement was invalid and his seat never vacant would have a personal stake in his office. The result would change on standing.
- **Vary the statute.** If Congress passed a citizen-suit provision authorizing any citizen to enforce the Ineligibility Clause, would Levitt then have standing? Modern doctrine says no: Congress cannot convert a generalized grievance into an Article III injury (see [[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]]).
- **Lawful or merely unchallenged (Note 4)?** The absence of a plaintiff does not make the appointment constitutional; it moves the question to other interpreters. Roosevelt, his Attorney General and the Senate all considered it. Black's own oath arguably required him to decide it himself. This is departmentalism in practice: see [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- **Fairchild (Note 5).** Citizens challenging the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment had "only the right, possessed by every citizen, to require that the government be administered according to law and that the public moneys be not wasted." Levitt applies that sentence to a Justice's seat.
- **Formalism in the clause, politics in the enforcement.** The Ineligibility Clause is as bright a formal rule as the Constitution contains, yet it is enforced only by the political branches. Be ready to say whether that makes it less of a law.
- **Memorable facts.** One paragraph; Black served 34 years; the Court "never ruled on it." Contrast Burwell: the House as an institution could sue over spending without an appropriation ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]), but the Senate, which confirmed Black, had no reason to sue over its own vote.
