## What it is

A short casebook note (pp. 325–326) on how a war ends: the Convention's debate over treaties of peace, [[Ludecke v. Watkins]] (1948) on the termination of a "state of war," and the 1971 repeal of the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution. It closes Assignment 14 before the casebook turns to "Conducting War."

## What to notice

- **The Constitution has no Terminate War Clause.** Congress declares war; the text says nothing about ending one. The Convention voted down, ten states to none, Butler's motion to give the Legislature the power of "peace" along with war (see [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]).
- **The peace-treaty debate.** Madison argued that a treaty of peace should be easier to approve than other treaties: no two-thirds Senate vote, and not even presidential concurrence. The Convention rejected that and subjected treaties of peace "to the same requirements as other treaties." The result is asymmetric: majorities of both Houses can begin a war, but ending it by treaty needs the President and two-thirds of the Senate. Ellsworth's "more easy to get out of war, than into it" did not carry through to the text.
- **Ludecke: three modes, one character.** "'The state of war' may be terminated by treaty or legislation or Presidential proclamation. Whatever the modes, its termination is a political act." And "War does not cease with a cease-fire order": powers that begin "when war is declared" are "not exhausted when the shooting stops."
  - The holding keeps war-based powers (there, under the Act of 1798) alive after fighting ends.
  - "A political act" signals judicial deference on when a war is over, parallel to the Prize Cases' deference on when a war has begun ("a question to be decided by him").
- **Repeal did not end Vietnam.** Congress repealed the Tonkin Resolution in January 1971; Nixon continued the war "based on his authority as commander in chief," and Congress kept funding it "for several more years." The war ended for the United States when Congress cut off funds (see [[Declarations of War]], Note 3).
- **Why repeal failed but defunding worked.** In Youngstown terms, repeal plus continued appropriations sends mixed signals: repeal withdraws category-one authority, but funding looks like implied authorization. A funding cutoff is unambiguous category three, and it acts through the Appropriations Clause, which the President cannot override (see [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] and [[United States v. Lovett]] on the purse).
- **The casebook's questions, with answers.**
  - Is defunding Congress's most effective tool? Yes, because it is self-executing: without an appropriation, money cannot be drawn from the Treasury.
  - Is it politically viable? Rarely; members fear being blamed for abandoning troops in the field, which is the same dynamic Nixon described as Congress "doing nothing" in the WPR veto, here in reverse.
  - Will a determined President always win? Not against an appropriations cutoff, because spending without an appropriation violates Art. I, § 9, cl. 7. He wins against repeal alone if he has an independent Article II claim, and the strength of that claim is the whole war- powers dispute.
- **Variations on the facts.**
  - Congress repeals the authorization and forbids further funding: category three and the purse; the President's only claim is a conclusive and preclusive command power, which cannot include spending unappropriated money.
  - Congress repeals the authorization but keeps funding: the Vietnam pattern; the President argues implied authorization from appropriations, which WPR § 8(a) now forbids Congress's statutes to be read as supplying.
  - The President proclaims the war over but Congress wants to keep war powers in force: Ludecke lists "Presidential proclamation" as a mode, so the President's act likely ends the "state of war" for statutory purposes, though Congress could legislate otherwise.
  - A cease-fire without a treaty (Korea): Ludecke says the war has not ended by the cease-fire alone.
- **The 2002 Iraq AUMF and termination by repeal.** After the casebook, the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2026 (December 2025) repealed the 1991 and 2002 Iraq authorizations. That is termination by legislation, one of Ludecke's three modes, applied to wars whose fighting had long ended.
