## What it is

Alexander Hamilton's essay of April 1, 1788, defending presidential nomination with Senate advice and consent. The casebook (pp. 172–175) prints it under its heading **A. The Appointment Power: "he . . . shall appoint,"** after a short introduction to the three-assignment sequence on appointment, removal and take care, and follows it with a one-paragraph Note. Assigned as the theory behind the Appointments Clause, which [[Buckley v. Valeo]] then applies.

## What to notice

- **The casebook's framing of the unit.** Three "basic elements of executive power": staffing through appointments, supervising and removing appointees, and taking care that the laws are faithfully executed. The editors ask whether "more executive control in one area impl[ies] less, or more, in other areas." Hold on to that question: Senate participation in appointment is the argument for Senate participation in removal (Bland in [[The Decision of 1789]]; the statute in [[Myers v. United States|Myers]]).
- **The Clause's structure.** Art. II, § 2, cl. 2 has a default and an alternative:
  - Default: the President "shall nominate, and by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, shall appoint" ambassadors, ministers and consuls, Supreme Court Justices, and "all other Officers of the United States."
  - Alternative, for "inferior Officers" only: Congress "may by Law vest the Appointment" in "the President alone, in the Courts of Law, or in the Heads of Departments."
  - Congress's role is to create offices "by Law" and to choose among the listed appointing authorities for inferior officers. Congress itself is not on the list.
- **The historical problem.** The King was the "fountain of honors." After 1688, kings "responded by creating lucrative offices and appointing members of Parliament to them, in order to obtain their favor and sway their votes." Revolutionary Americans called this corruption, and the first state constitutions gave appointments to an executive council or the legislature. That produced "weak governors and dominant legislatures," and Americans "saw that making appointments could also corrupt a legislature." The Convention considered Congress, the Senate, and the President alone before the compromise.
- **Three possible holders.** Appointment should be vested "in a single man, or in a select assembly of a moderate number; or in a single man, with the concurrence of such an assembly." Popular election of officers is "impracticable."
- **Why one man chooses better than a body.** "[O]ne man of discernment is better fitted to analyze and estimate the peculiar qualities adapted to particular offices, than a body of men of equal or perhaps even of superior discernment." The reason is responsibility: "The sole and undivided responsibility of one man will naturally beget a livelier sense of duty and a more exact regard to reputation." The same accountability argument as [[The Federalist No. 70]].
- **Why an assembly chooses worse.** An appointing assembly displays "all the private and party likings and dislikes, partialities and antipathies, attachments and animosities." The bargain: "Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that." This is the log-rolling objection and the reason Congress cannot appoint the officers who execute its laws. It is the functional core of [[Buckley v. Valeo]].
- **Nomination is appointment in substance.** "There can, in this view, be no difference between nominating and appointing." Every appointee "would be, in fact, his choice," because no one can be appointed without his nomination. If the Senate rejects, the result is only "another nomination by himself."
- **Why the Senate will rarely reject.** The Senate cannot be sure a second nomination would bring forward the person it prefers, and a rejection may "cast a kind of stigma" on the nominee and look like "a reflection upon the judgment of the chief magistrate." Refusal would come only on "special and strong reasons." Compare modern practice, where Senate refusal to act on nominees is routine; the prediction is the part of the essay that aged worst.
- **What the Senate adds.** Its concurrence "would have a powerful, though, in general, a silent operation": "an excellent check upon a spirit of favoritism in the President," a guard against appointments "from State prejudice, from family connection, from personal attachment, or from a view to popularity," and "an efficacious source of stability in the administration." The President would be "both ashamed and afraid" to nominate candidates whose only merit was "the necessary insignificance and pliancy to render them the obsequious instruments of his pleasure."
- **"Stability in the administration" is a removal argument in disguise.** The phrase supports the view Bland argued in the First Congress, that the Senate should share in removal as it shares in appointment. Madison's side rejected that view in [[The Decision of 1789]]. Be ready to say whether Hamilton's "stability" means stability of personnel (which implies a Senate role in removal) or stability of the quality of appointees (which does not).
- **The Note's questions, with a view.**
  - *Would appointment by the President alone be abused?* Hamilton's answer is the "silent operation" of the Senate: the threat of rejection shapes the nomination even when the Senate never rejects. The Clause itself partly concedes the President could do the job alone, since Congress may give him inferior appointments "alone."
  - *Would a legislative body log-roll?* Hamilton's "Give us the man" bargain is the prediction, and the pre-1787 state experience is the evidence. Buckley treats the point as settled.
  - *Ingenious solution or gridlock?* The design places the initiative with one person and a veto with a body. It produces gridlock only when the Senate is willing to bear the cost of refusing, which is the condition Hamilton assumed would be rare. That condition is also why the recess appointment power matters in [[NLRB v. Noel Canning]].
