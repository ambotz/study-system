## Rule

When a state supreme court decides a case within Article III's list of cases, the U.S. Supreme Court may review that decision.

## Facts

- **The judgment under review came from a state supreme court, in a case falling within Article III's list of cases.** *That is the predicate for Supreme Court review; the casebook reports no other facts.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- A state supreme court's decision in a case within Article III's categories is subject to review by the U.S. Supreme Court.
- The opinion states that Congress "can claim no powers which are not granted to it by the constitution, and the powers actually granted, must be such as are expressly given, or given by necessary implication."
- Dicta in the opinion cut against the view that Congress may give the lower federal courts only part of Article III's list of cases.

**Rejected**

- *(Implicitly) A state supreme court's judgment on a question of federal law is final and beyond federal review.* Supreme Court review extends to state-court decisions in Article III cases.

## Context

Martin is the consequence of the Madisonian Compromise described in [[A Map of Article III]]. Article III leaves it to Congress whether to create lower federal courts, so many federal questions are decided in state courts, and the Supreme Court is usually the only federal forum for reviewing those decisions. *Cohens v. Virginia* (1821) followed Martin. The Supremacy Clause, which binds "the Judges in every State," is the strongest text for that review; see [[Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause]].

Martin's dicta on lower-court jurisdiction did not prevail. *Sheldon v. Sill* (1850) held that lower federal courts' jurisdiction is within Congress's discretion to grant, withhold or limit; see [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]]. Justice Thomas's dissent in *Trump v. Mazars* quotes Martin's "necessary implication" line to argue that implied powers are "very limited," a narrower reading than [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] and [[United States v. Comstock]] give the Necessary and Proper Clause.
