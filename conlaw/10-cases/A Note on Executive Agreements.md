## What it is

Two short casebook notes that end the foreign-affairs section: "A Note on Executive Agreements" and "A Note on the Iranian Nuclear Agreement." They ask what the President can commit the United States to without the Senate, and what such a commitment does to state and federal law.

## What to notice

- **The constitutional question, in the casebook's terms.** Article II lets the President make treaties with two-thirds of the Senate. "But can the president also make foreign agreements outside of the treaty power?" Executive agreements "may have legal status as a matter of international law, but their status as domestic law is trickier. (Look at the text of the Supremacy Clause in Article VI. Do you see why?)" The Supremacy Clause names the Constitution, laws made in pursuance of it, and treaties — not executive agreements.
- **The easy case and the hard case.** An agreement in which the President promises only to exercise powers he already holds is lawful. The question is whether he "may . . . promise, on behalf of the United States, to do things that otherwise lie outside of unilateral presidential authority — such as preempting state law."
- **The Litvinov line.** Recognizing the Soviet Union in 1933 came with a settlement of claims; *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. 324 (1937), and *United States v. Pink*, 315 U.S. 203 (1942), held the agreement lawful and held that it displaced state law.
- **The modern cases pull in both directions.** *Dames & Moore* implicitly narrowed Belmont and Pink: an agreement authorized by Congress displaces state law more easily because it executes a statute, which the Supremacy Clause does cover. *Crosby v. National Foreign Trade Council*, 530 U.S. 363 (2000), and *American Insurance Association v. Garamendi*, 539 U.S. 396 (2003), found state laws preempted; *Medellín v. Texas*, 552 U.S. 491 (2008), refused to let the President use a non-self-executing treaty to override Texas criminal procedure.
- **The JCPOA as the worked example.** The 2015 agreement with Iran was never submitted as a treaty, so it took effect as an executive agreement. Congress answered with the Iran Nuclear Agreement Review Act, requiring submission for approval or disapproval subject to veto — which meant "the Plan could go into effect as an executive agreement with the support of one third of one of the two Houses of Congress." A disapproval motion was filibustered and died.
- **The Cotton letter, and why it matters doctrinally.** Forty-one senators warned Iran that because the Plan had not been ratified by the Senate, "[t]he next president could revoke such an agreement with the stroke of a pen and future Congresses could modify the terms of the agreement at any time." The casebook's verdict: "That turned out to be true."
- **The ping-pong.** Trump withdrew "relying on the same unilateral foreign affairs power as President Obama had"; Biden signalled willingness to return without success, and rejoined the Paris Climate Accord in January 2021. The casebook's closing question is the one to have an answer to: "What does it mean for U.S. foreign policy to keep switching back and forth like this? Is this a constitutional problem of some kind?"
- **The structural point to carry into the war materials.** An instrument that binds only as long as one President wants it to is not law in the Article VI sense, which is why the same reversibility shows up in the war-powers unit: an authorization Congress passes is durable, while a presidential commitment is not.
