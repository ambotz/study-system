## Rule

Congress may regulate intrastate activities that "have such a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce that their control is essential or appropriate to protect that commerce from burdens and obstructions," and the reach of the commerce power "is necessarily one of degree," not a matter of labelling effects "direct" or "indirect."

## Facts

- **The National Labor Relations Act comprehensively regulated labor practices and labor relationships.**
- **The Act was applied to manufacturing facilities.** *Under [[United States v. E.C. Knight Co|E.C. Knight]] and Carter Coal, manufacturing was local; the Court made "no real attempt to distinguish" Carter Coal and [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] (Kennedy, concurring in [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]]).*
- **By its terms the Act reached only what "may be deemed to burden or obstruct commerce."** *The Court construed the Act to "operate within the sphere of constitutional authority"; a statute imposing collective bargaining "upon all industry regardless of effects upon interstate or foreign commerce" would have presented a different question.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (Hughes, C.J., for a 5–4 Court)

- The National Labor Relations Act is within the commerce power.
- Intrastate activities with "such a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce that their control is essential or appropriate to protect that commerce from burdens and obstructions" are within Congress's power.
- The scope of the commerce power "is necessarily one of degree."
- Whether an activity burdens commerce is judged by "practical" considerations, including "actual experience" (as quoted by Ginsburg in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]).
- The commerce power has an outer limit: the power may not embrace effects "so indirect and remote that to embrace them, in view of our complex society, would effectually obliterate the distinction between what is national and what is local and create a completely centralized government."

**Rejected**

- *Labor relations in a manufacturing plant are production, a local activity whose effect on interstate commerce is only indirect, as [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] and Carter Coal held.* The Court departed from the direct/indirect distinction and asked instead whether the activity has a "close and substantial relation" to interstate commerce.
- *The Act imposes collective bargaining on every employer in the country, whatever the effect on commerce.* The Act "purports to reach only what may be deemed to burden or obstruct commerce and, thus qualified, it must be construed as contemplating the exercise of control within constitutional bounds."

## Context

Jones & Laughlin came in 1937, amid the fight over Roosevelt's court-packing plan (see [[Senate Report on Court Packing]]), and the casebook calls the case "a turning point." Justice Roberts's move to the broad-power side produced the quip, "not necessarily justified" in the casebook's view, of a "switch in time that saved nine." Chief Justice Hughes had written the Shreveport Rate Case in 1914. The same year the Court sustained the Social Security taxes in [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis]] and [[Helvering v. Davis]] ([[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]]). The 5–4 decision was narrowly written, but within five years [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] and [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]] reaffirmed and expanded it.

[[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]] uses Jones & Laughlin twice: as the source of the third category, activities having "a substantial relation to interstate commerce," and as proof that even the New Deal cases recognized outer limits. Lopez closes by refusing to hold that there will never be a distinction "between what is truly national and what is truly local, cf. Jones & Laughlin Steel." Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]] cites the case for Congress's power to eliminate potential obstructions to interstate commerce, and Thomas's dissent in [[United States v. Comstock]] cites it as the mark of the Commerce Clause as "the enumerated power this Court has interpreted most expansively." See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].
