## What it is

The founding-era debate over whether Congress could charter the First Bank of the United States (casebook pp. 468–484; syllabus: "Legislative and Executive Debate on the Bank"). It has four parts: the editors' introduction to the First Bank and the doctrine of incidental powers; Representative James Madison's speech against the bill in the House (Feb. 2, 1791); Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson's opinion to President Washington that the bill was unconstitutional (Feb. 15, 1791); and Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton's opinion defending it (Feb. 23, 1791). Washington signed the bill. The closing note follows the Bank to Madison's acceptance of the Second Bank in 1816. It is the first full argument over implied powers and the meaning of "necessary," and it supplies nearly every argument [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] later accepts or rejects.

## What to notice

- **The stakes were economic and cultural, not only legal.** Hamilton wanted to turn "a nation of farmers into a commercial power"; Jefferson "thought land ownership, not speculation in bank stocks, would promote civic virtue." Banks were feared as "a collusion between capital and the capitol." The Bank was to have $10 million in capital, 20% government ownership, and a twenty-year national monopoly.
- **The Convention had rejected an incorporation power.** An express power to charter corporations was left out partly because of opposition from New York and Philadelphia commercial interests, and a narrower canal-corporation power "was voted down eight to three." The editors ask: "Should these facts affect your interpretation?" Madison and Jefferson say yes; Hamilton says no.
- **The background doctrine is incidental powers.** A grant includes powers "incident" to it. Two tests: "proximity or relation" (how close to the principal power) and "size" (is the claimed power "really subordinate," or so significant "that we should expect it to have been stated explicitly?"). In the bookcase illustration, moving books off the bookcase is incidental; repainting is unrelated; knocking out a wall is related but too big: "If you wanted your friend to have permission to knock out your walls, you would have said that expressly." The whole debate is about whether chartering a bank is moving the books or knocking out the wall.
- **Madison (House speech, Feb. 2, 1791): the Bank is a great, substantive power that must be expressly granted.**
  - *Enumeration is the government's defining feature.* "It is not a general grant, out of which particular powers are excepted—it is a grant of particular powers only, leaving the general mass in other hands."
  - *His five rules of interpretation* are a catalogue of modes of argument: structure ("An interpretation that destroys the very characteristic of the government cannot be just"), consequences for doubtful meanings, the parties' intent, "Cotemporary and concurrent expositions," and the size principle: regard "not only the degree of its incidentality to an express authority ... but the degree of its importance also."
  - *General welfare.* The phrase limits the taxing power and does not grant a separate one; reading it otherwise "would give to Congress an unlimited power; would render nugatory the enumeration of particular powers."
  - *Interference with the states is not the test.* "If the power was not given, Congress could not exercise it; if given, they might exercise it, altho it should interfere with the laws, or even the constitution of the States." This is a clean statement that enumeration, not state interests, marks the line.
  - *Borrowing.* "Is this a bill to borrow money? It does not borrow a shilling." A power to borrow does not include a power to create "the ability ... to lend."
  - *Necessary and Proper.* The clause is "limited to means necessary to the end, and incident to the nature of the specified powers," and is "merely declaratory" of what would follow by implication anyway.
  - *The chain of implications.* Borrowing is the end and accumulating capital the means; then capital is the end and a bank the means; then the bank is the end and "a charter of incorporation, a monopoly, capital punishments, &c." the means. "If implications, thus remote and thus multiplied, can be linked together, a chain may be formed that will reach every object of legislation."
  - *Expressio unius.* The Constitution spells out lesser powers it could have left to implication: punishing counterfeiters alongside regulating money, raising armies alongside declaring war. So a far greater power like incorporation cannot be implied.
  - *Incorporation is too important to be incidental.* It creates "an artificial person," is "equivalent, to the naturalization of an alien," delegates by-law-making power, and confers a monopoly. It is "a distinct, an independent and substantive prerogative."
  - *Necessary to what?* He separates powers "necessary and proper for the government or union" from powers necessary and proper "for executing the enumerated powers." Only the second kind are granted. In any case the Bank "could not even be called necessary to the government; at most it could be but convenient."
  - *Ratification-era assurances.* The Federalists' answer to the missing bill of rights assumed that powers not given were retained. Madison also relies on the state conventions and on the proposed 11th and 12th amendments (ratified as the Ninth and Tenth). If the bill passes, "its adoption was brought about by one set of arguments, and ... it is now administered under the influence of another set."
  - *If in doubt, wait.* Respect for successors and constituents requires "that the irrevocable decision should at least be suspended until another session." He treats Congress as bound to decide constitutionality for itself, not to leave it to courts.
- **Jefferson (opinion to Washington, Feb. 15, 1791): "necessary" means indispensable.**
  - *The Tenth Amendment is the foundation.* "To take a single step beyond the boundaries thus specially drawn around the powers of Congress, is to take possession of a boundless f[ie]ld of power, no longer susceptible of any definition."
  - *Taxing and borrowing.* "[N]o debt is paid by this bill, nor any tax laid." The government's lending its $2 million and borrowing it back remains "a payment, and not a loan, call it by what name you please."
  - *Commerce.* "To erect a thing which may be bought and sold, is not to prescribe regulations for buying and selling." And the power does not reach "the internal regulation of the commerce of a state," which he defines as commerce "between citizen and citizen." That reading returns in the commerce cases.
  - *General welfare.* It names the purpose of the taxing power, not a separate grant; otherwise the Constitution collapses into "a single phrase, that of instituting a Congress with power to do whatever would be for the good of the U.S." The framers meant "to lace them up straitly within the enumerated powers."
  - *The strict test of "necessary."* The enumerated powers "can all be carried into execution without a bank. A bank therefore is not necessary." The Constitution "allows only the means which are “necessary” not those which are merely “convenient,”" that is, "those means without which the grant of the power would be nugatory." His reason is a slippery slope: any power can be tortured "into a convenience."
  - *The Convention.* "[T]he very power now proposed as a means, was rejected as an end, by the Convention."
  - *The veto protects the states.* The President's negative is "the shield provided by the constitution" against legislative invasion of the rights of the executive, the judiciary and "the states and state legislatures." But if the arguments "hang so even as to balance his judgment, a just respect for the wisdom of the legislature would naturally decide the balance in favour of their opinion." Jefferson's own rule of deference gave Washington a reason to sign if he found the question close.
- **Hamilton (opinion to Washington, Feb. 23, 1791): "necessary" means useful, and the test is the relation between means and end.**
  - *Sovereignty within its sphere.* "[E]very power vested in a Government is in its nature sovereign, and includes, by force of the term, a right to employ all the means requisite, and fairly applicable to the attainment of the ends of such power," unless forbidden, immoral, or contrary to "the essential ends of political society."
  - *Divided sovereignty is still sovereignty.* The national and state governments each have "sovereign power as to certain things, and not as to other things." To deny this would make the United States "a political society without sovereignty, or of a people governed without government." The Supremacy Clause proves it: "The power which can create the Supreme law of the land, in any case, is doubtless sovereign as to such case."
  - *Incorporation is a means, not an end.* Critics treat a corporation as "some great, independent, substantive thing"; it is really "a quality, capacity, or mean to an end." This is a direct answer to Madison's "substantive prerogative."
  - *The test.* The question is "whether the mean to be employed ... has a natural relation to any of the acknowledged objects or lawful ends of the government." Congress could not create a corporation to police Philadelphia, but could create one to collect taxes or to conduct foreign or interstate trade.
  - *"Necessary."* "[N]ecessary often means no more than needful, requisite, incidental, useful, or conducive to." Jefferson's reading would treat the word "as if the word absolutely or indispensably had been prefixed to it."
  - *Constitutionality cannot vary with circumstances.* Under Jefferson's test the Bank might be necessary one year and not the next. But "the constitutional right of exercising it must be uniform & invariable—the same to day as to morrow." Therefore "The degree in which a measure is necessary, can never be a test of the legal right to adopt it"; the test is "the relation between the measure and the end."
  - *Practice.* The lighthouse act of 1789 was not "strictly necessary" to regulating trade, yet no one doubted it. The act recognizing "the power of the President to remove officers at pleasure" is another implied power. That is [[The Decision of 1789]].
  - *Liberal construction and a third category.* Powers over "finances, trade, defence &c ought to be construed liberally." There will be cases clearly in, cases clearly out, and "a third class" where "a reasonable latitude of judgment must be allowed."
  - *His two-part criterion.* "If the end be clearly comprehended within any of the specified powers, & if the measure have an obvious relation to that end, and is not forbidden by any particular provision of the constitution—it may safely be deemed to come within the compass of the national authority." A tiebreaker is whether the measure abridges "a preexisting right of any State, or of any individual."
  - *Intent is found in the text.* Intent "is to be sought for in the instrument itself"; "arguments drawn from extrinsic circumstances, regarding the intention of the convention, must be rejected." This is an early statement of text-over-intent.
  - *The application.* The Bank relates to taxes, borrowing ("in sudden emergencies an essential instrument"), interstate trade ("Money is the very hinge on which commerce turns"), and defense. As a joint owner, the government also brings the Bank within the Property Clause.
- **The three positions on "necessary."** Jefferson: indispensable, "without which the grant of the power would be nugatory." Madison: means that are both direct and incidental, with an extra requirement that the implied power not be a great one. Hamilton: "needful, requisite, incidental, useful, or conducive to," tested by the means' "natural relation" to the end. [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] adopts Hamilton's definition almost word for word and rejects Jefferson's "absolutely" reading. But Marshall keeps Madison's size limit as well: incorporation qualifies as an implied power because it is not a great, substantive and independent power of the kind one would expect to be granted expressly. [[NFIB v. Sebelius|NFIB]] (2012) revives that same size limit against the individual mandate.
- **Madison's shift: settlement by practice.** Washington signed the bill; the First Bank ran 1791–1811. A recharter failed in 1811. In 1815 President Madison vetoed a Second Bank bill on policy grounds only, "[w]aiving the question of the constitutional authority of the Legislature to establish an incorporated bank as being precluded in my judgment by repeated recognition under varied circumstances of the validity of such an institution in acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial branches of the Government, accompanied by indications, in different modes, of a concurrence of the general will of the nation." He signed the Second Bank in 1816. This is liquidation: an ambiguous text settled by repeated, deliberate practice across branches, with public acceptance. Compare [[The Decision of 1789]]. Madison's fuller defense comes in [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]].
- **Was Madison consistent?** The case for yes: his 1791 rules already made "Cotemporary and concurrent expositions" evidence of meaning, and practice counts only where the text is doubtful. The case for no: in 1791 he said the meaning was clear and the Bank a "usurpation" if the power was absent; if that was right, twenty years of practice cannot cure it. The editors' questions press exactly this: "if the Constitution says one thing but practice under the Constitution is different, which one is controlling?" Stock answer: practice can fix the meaning of a provision that is genuinely ambiguous, but it cannot override a clear text. The hard question is who decides whether the text was clear.
- **Hypotheticals: how the three tests sort cases.**
  - *A federal lighthouse.* Hamilton: valid, obvious relation to regulating trade. Jefferson: hard, since trade can be regulated without lighthouses; Hamilton uses this to show Jefferson's test proves too much. Madison: likely valid, as a small and direct incident.
  - *A federal corporation to police Philadelphia.* Invalid on all three views: the end (city policing) is not within any enumerated power. Hamilton's test polices ends, not means.
  - *A federal canal company.* Jefferson and Madison: invalid, and the Convention rejected it. Hamilton: valid if tied to interstate commerce or defense, and the Convention's intent is irrelevant.
  - *A modern hypothetical: federal civil commitment of sexually dangerous federal prisoners after their sentences end.* Hamilton's "natural relation" test sustains it as a means to running a federal prison system, and [[United States v. Comstock]] (2010) so held. Madison's chain-of-implications objection is the dissent's argument.
- **Types of argument on display.** Text (the meaning of "necessary," "borrow," "regulate"); structure (enumeration as the "characteristic of the government"; the expressio unius pattern); intent (the Convention's rejection of an incorporation power); ratification-era understanding (assurances in the state conventions); practice (lighthouses, removal); consequences (Madison's chain of implications versus Hamilton's "arrest the motions of government"). See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Departmentalism.** All of this constitutional argument happens in Congress and the cabinet, with no court involved. Jefferson treats the veto as a constitutional check, and Madison asks the House to reject the bill on constitutional grounds. The Supreme Court speaks only in 1819. Compare [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]].
