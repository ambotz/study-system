## What it is

Lincoln's first inaugural address, delivered March 4, 1861, seven weeks before Fort Sumter. Seven States had already declared themselves out of the Union; Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina and officials in Missouri and Kentucky followed in the months after the speech. It is at once a legal brief against secession, a conciliatory offer on slavery, and a statement of what the new President would and would not do.

The casebook assigns it as the second of the three positions on secession. [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] said secession was unlawful but coercion was also unlawful; [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]] said secession was a lawful attribute of State sovereignty; Lincoln says secession is unlawful and therefore legally void, so that maintaining the Union is not coercion at all but execution of the laws. [[Texas v. White]] makes Lincoln's theory the holding of the Supreme Court.

The eight Notes that follow (pp. 769–774) carry as much weight as the speech: they raise the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, the location of sovereignty, the "moderate" secessionist argument from [[Dred Scott v. Sandford|Dred Scott]], judicial supremacy, the coercion question, and the Reconstruction problem.

## What to notice

- **The reassurance on slavery in the States.** Lincoln quotes his own record: "I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so." He then reads into the speech the Republican platform plank denouncing "the lawless invasion by armed force of the soil of any State or Territory." Note 1's question — does the Constitution create a general constitutional right to own slaves? — gets a "no" from Lincoln: the right exists only where State law creates it, and federal obligations run only to the specific clauses.
- **The Fugitive Slave Clause is enforced, with conditions attached.** "[T]he intention of the lawgiver is the law," and every member of Congress is under oath to the clause. But Lincoln immediately asks for two things the South did not want: "all the safeguards of liberty known in civilized and humane jurisprudence... so that a free man be not in any case surrendered as a slave," and a statute enforcing the Privileges and Immunities Clause. The second would protect free Black citizens travelling south, and it is the ancestor of the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause.
- **The Corwin Amendment.** Lincoln endorses an amendment barring federal interference with "the domestic institutions of the States, including that of persons held to service": "holding such a provision to now be implied constitutional law, I have no objection to its being made express and irrevocable." Note 1 asks what to make of this. Two answers are available: that he conceded nothing he had not already conceded, since he thought the power did not exist; and that an irrevocable amendment protecting slavery is a very large price for union, and one he was willing to pay in March 1861.
- **Argument one against secession: perpetuity is implied in the nature of government.** "I hold that in contemplation of universal law and of the Constitution the Union of these States is perpetual... It is safe to assert that no government proper ever had a provision in its organic law for its own termination." This is an argument from political theory rather than text; it proves too much if taken alone, since it would equally forbid the Union's dissolution by consent of all the States.
- **Argument two: even on the compact theory, one party cannot rescind.** "One party to a contract may violate it—break it, so to speak—but does it not require all to lawfully rescind it?" This is the strongest argument against [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]], because it concedes the compact premise and still defeats secession. Davis's answer must be that the States are not merely parties but sovereigns who delegated revocable powers "to any agent whomsoever."
- **Argument three: the Union is older than the Constitution.** "The Union is much older than the Constitution. It was formed, in fact, by the Articles of Association in 1774. It was matured and continued by the Declaration of Independence in 1776. It was further matured, and the faith of all the then thirteen States expressly plighted and engaged that it should be perpetual, by the Articles of Confederation in 1778." Then the Preamble: "if destruction of the Union by one or by a part only of the States be lawfully possible, the Union is less perfect than before the Constitution, having lost the vital element of perpetuity." This is James Wilson's 1776 nationalism in [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell's]] scheme, and Chief Justice Chase repeats the Articles-plus-Preamble step almost word for word in [[Texas v. White]].
- **Argument four: secession is anarchy because it destroys majority rule.** "Plainly the central idea of secession is the essence of anarchy." The engine is a regress: "If a minority in such case will secede rather than acquiesce, they make a precedent which in turn will divide and ruin them, for a minority of their own will secede from them whenever a majority refuses to be controlled by such minority." The premise is that constitutional silences are unavoidable — "No foresight can anticipate nor any document of reasonable length contain express provisions for all possible questions" — so somebody must lose, and either the majority governs or "the Government must cease."
- **The three silences Lincoln names are the slavery questions.** "Shall fugitives from labor be surrendered by national or by State authority? The Constitution does not expressly say. May Congress prohibit slavery in the Territories? The Constitution does not expressly say. Must Congress protect slavery in the Territories? The Constitution does not expressly say." The third is the holding of [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], and saying the Constitution does not expressly answer it is already a denial that Dred Scott settled the country's policy.
- **The legal conclusion, and the Take Care duty.** "[N]o State upon its own mere motion can lawfully get out of the Union; that resolves and ordinances to that effect are legally void, and that acts of violence within any State or States against the authority of the United States are insurrectionary or revolutionary, according to circumstances." Therefore "the Union is unbroken, and to the extent of my ability, I shall take care, as the Constitution itself expressly enjoins upon me, that the laws of the Union be faithfully executed in all the States."
- **The duty is qualified twice, and both qualifications matter.** Lincoln will perform it "unless my rightful masters, the American people, shall withhold the requisite means or in some authoritative manner direct the contrary" — the appropriations point from Class 5. And the program is narrow: "The power confided to me will be used to hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government and to collect the duties and imposts; but beyond what may be necessary for these objects, there will be no invasion, no using of force against or among the people anywhere." Where hostility makes local offices unfillable, "there will be no attempt to force obnoxious strangers among the people for that object."
- **Coercion is reframed as law execution against individuals.** Lincoln never answers Buchanan's question whether Congress may "coerce a State"; he denies that the question arises, because the ordinances are void and there is nothing to coerce except persons resisting federal law. That is why the war could be a "war for the suppression of rebellion" rather than a war between nations, and it is the theory the Court adopted in [[The Prize Cases]] and in [[Texas v. White]].
- **Who has to fire first.** "In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war. The Government will not assail you. You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors. You have no oath registered in heaven to destroy the Government, while I shall have the most solemn one to 'preserve, protect, and defend it.'" Holding Sumter while refusing to attack puts the choice of war on the other side, which is exactly what happened.
- **Dred Scott and the limits of judicial authority.** The passage is carefully hedged and every clause does work. Decisions "must be binding in any case upon the parties to a suit as to the object of that suit." They are "entitled to very high respect and consideration in all parallel cases by all other departments of the Government." An erroneous decision can be borne because "the evil effect following it, being limited to that particular case," may be overruled. But "if the policy of the Government upon vital questions affecting the whole people is to be irrevocably fixed by decisions of the Supreme Court, the instant they are made in ordinary litigation between parties in personal actions the people will have ceased to be their own rulers, having to that extent practically resigned their Government into the hands of that eminent tribunal." And it is not an attack on the Court: "it is no fault of theirs if others seek to turn their decisions to political purposes."
- **The distinction is judgment versus precedent, not obedience versus defiance.** Lincoln concedes the judgment in Dred Scott's own case and denies that the opinion binds the political branches' future policy. That is departmentalism, and it is the position [[Cooper v. Aaron]] rejects when it says the federal judiciary is "supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution." Note 5 reports that the softened draft had read that the people would have "turned their government over to the despotism of the few life-officers composing the Court," which links judicial supremacy to minority rule and ties the Dred Scott passage to the anarchy passage: both are arguments that a minority may not fix policy for the majority.
- **This is the position Lincoln argued in 1858.** [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] contain the same move against Douglas's insistence that a Supreme Court decision must simply be obeyed, and the Republican platform he quotes here was built on it.
- **Note 2: the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions.** Lincoln's argument is the mirror image. Madison and Jefferson said the Constitution is a compact among the States, so the States may judge infractions and interpose; Lincoln says the Union preceded the States and a compact cannot be rescinded by one party. Does interposition lead inexorably to secession? Not inexorably: [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate|Davis]] himself insists that "Nullification and secession, so often confounded, are indeed antagonistic principles," because nullification keeps the State in the Union and appeals to the other States. But the premise both share — that each State may judge for itself whether the compact has been broken — has no stopping point once the State concludes that the other parties will not be bound, which is precisely the step Davis takes.
- **Note 3: where sovereignty resides.** Lincoln's July 4, 1861 message is the aggressive version of the inaugural. Secession is a "sophism" resting on "some omnipotent, and sacred supremacy, pertaining to a State." The textual point is small and sharp: "Much is said about the 'sovereignty' of the States; but the word, even, is not in the national Constitution." The historical point is bigger: "no one of them ever having been a State out of the Union," and "The Union is older than any of the States; and, in fact, it created them as States." Texas is the one admitted exception, and "even Texas gave up the character on coming into the Union" — a line [[Texas v. White]] converts into "the incorporation of a new member into the political body."
- **Note 3's Guarantee Clause argument is the weakest of Lincoln's arguments and the most useful.** "[I]f a State may lawfully go out of the Union, having done so, it may also discard the republican form of government; so that to prevent its going out, is an indispensable means, to the end, of maintaining the guarantee mentioned." The structure is [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch's]]: a guarantee implies the means to make it good. The weakness is that it assumes the answer, since a State lawfully out of the Union is not a State "in this Union" to which the guarantee runs. Its value is that it supplies an enumerated textual hook for force, which Article I, § 8, cl. 15 and Article IV, § 4 also supply — and which Buchanan said did not exist.
- **Note 4: the expelled State.** If the Union refused to seat a State's senators, denied it protection against invasion and denied its citizens privileges and immunities in other States, could it secede? Lincoln's arguments do not reach this case cleanly. The anarchy argument fails, because the State is not a minority refusing to acquiesce in a lawful majority but the victim of an admitted constitutional violation. The contract argument cuts the other way: a material breach by the other parties is the classic ground for rescission, and Lincoln's own premise is that "One party to a contract may violate it." What survives is the perpetuity argument, which denies any exit on any ground, and Lincoln's answer that the remedy for unconstitutional action is the amending power, elections, and ultimately "their revolutionary right to dismember or overthrow" the government — not a constitutional right to secede.
- **Note 4's moderate secessionists rest on Dred Scott, which makes judicial supremacy do the work.** Senator Nicholson of Tennessee: the Republicans "repudiate the authoritative adjudication of the Supreme Court" on slaveholders' rights in the Territories. Senator Benjamin of Louisiana builds the analogy: if the Senate seated only one of South Carolina's two senators and refused to repair it, no one "would deny the plain and palpable proposition that it was the exercise of a right inherent in her under the very principles of the Constitution, and necessarily so inherent for self-defense," and territorial rights stand on the same footing because they rest "in accordance with the opinion expressed by the Supreme Court."
- **The answer to Benjamin is that his two hypotheticals are not alike.** Benjamin concedes the difference and asks the reader to overlook it: the two-senator right "is not put down in the Constitution in quite so clear terms." Denying a State its second senator violates an express, self-executing clause and is remediable by no one else; Lincoln's opposition to Dred Scott was a political program to seek the overruling of a decision about a silence, pursued through elections and litigation. On Lincoln's own account the Court's judgment in Dred Scott remained binding on the parties, so nothing had been defied. The deeper problem is that Benjamin's argument makes the lawfulness of secession depend on judicial supremacy, the very doctrine the South rejected when the Court ruled the other way — as it would have after [[The Emancipation Proclamation]].
- **Note 5: the Taney Court hypothetical.** If Virginia sued to stop federal armies crossing its territory and a Taney Court ordered the South released, would Lincoln have had to obey? On his own theory, a judgment binds the parties to the suit, so an injunction in a case properly before the Court would be entitled to compliance in that case; but the Court could not thereby "irrevocably fix" the policy of the Union, and Lincoln would not have treated the opinion as settling his duty under the oath. He behaved that way in fact: [[Ex parte Merryman]] is a judicial order he did not obey, defended by [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Attorney General Bates]] on departmentalist grounds. The casebook's escalating hypotheticals — vacating the White House, returning freed people to slavery — are designed to show that nobody really holds unqualified judicial supremacy.
- **Note 6: was the war a "war"?** On Lincoln's theory it was "a massive domestic insurrection," so no declaration was needed; but he also claimed the law of war under the Commander-in-Chief Clause to justify [[The Emancipation Proclamation]]. The casebook asks whether that is inconsistent. It is not necessarily: [[The Prize Cases]] hold that a civil war may be a war in fact, engaging the law of war, without conferring on the rebels the status of a foreign nation. The awkwardness is real but it is the same awkwardness the Court resolved in 1863 — belligerency for purposes of the conduct of hostilities, insurrection for purposes of sovereignty. [[Lincoln, Order of Retaliation]] runs on the same double aspect.
- **Note 6 also asks whether Lincoln could have negotiated a dissolution.** He answers in the speech: "The Chief Magistrate derives all his authority from the people, and they have referred none upon him to fix terms for the separation of the States. The people themselves can do this if also they choose, but the Executive as such has nothing to do with it." Separation is possible, but only through amendment or revolution, never by executive agreement.
- **Note 7: the Reconstruction stakes.** If the States left, the South was conquered territory governed by Congress under the Territories Clause and re-admitted under Article IV, § 3. If they never left, Reconstruction is restoration of lawful government under the Guarantee Clause. [[Texas v. White]] takes the second view and, as Note 7 says, makes Reconstruction "largely a matter of executive policy, supported by congressional legislation" — although Chase then holds that the Guarantee Clause power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," which is what the Reconstruction Acts and [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] were fought over.
- **Note 7's West Virginia problem is the sharpest test of Lincoln's theory.** The Wheeling convention declared itself the loyal government of Virginia, was recognized as such, and then gave Virginia's consent to its own partition. If secession was void, the Richmond legislature remained Virginia's lawful legislature and did not consent; if the Wheeling government was Virginia, one body consented to itself. Lincoln's theory buys the validity of the Wheeling government at the cost of making Article IV, § 3's consent requirement a formality.
- **Is Buchanan's position coherent?** Buchanan and Lincoln agree that secession is unlawful and disagree only about remedy, and Buchanan's argument is coherent on one assumption: that the only available response is war against a State as such, which the Convention refused to authorize. Lincoln's reply is that the assumption is false. Federal law runs against individuals, so holding forts, collecting duties and arresting those who resist are ordinary executions of the laws, and Article I, § 8, cl. 15 supplies the militia power "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections." What is left of Buchanan is a prudential claim — that the Union "can never be cemented by the blood of its citizens shed in civil war" — which is an argument about whether to use a power, not about whether it exists.
- **Hypothetical: South Carolina secedes and then lets the customs house operate and the mails run.** Lincoln's program would have nothing to execute and no occasion for force, and the Union would be dissolved in fact while remaining whole in law. That is Davis's point about there being "no laws of the United States to be executed within the limits of a seceded State," and it shows that Lincoln's reframing works only because the seceding States seized federal property.
- **Hypothetical: a State secedes by a unanimous vote of its citizens and Congress votes to let it go.** Lincoln's four arguments do not all survive. The anarchy argument fails (no minority is overriding a majority), and the contract argument fails (all the parties have agreed, or at least the political branches of the Union have). Only the perpetuity argument reaches the case, and [[Texas v. White]] preserves exactly this exception: revocation is possible "through revolution, or through consent of the States." Note 2 after Texas v. White asks whether Congress's power to admit implies a power to release.
- **Memorable lines.** "The Union is much older than the Constitution." "[N]o government proper ever had a provision in its organic law for its own termination." "Plainly the central idea of secession is the essence of anarchy." "[T]he people will have ceased to be their own rulers." "Physically speaking, we can not separate." "We are not enemies, but friends." "[T]he better angels of our nature."
