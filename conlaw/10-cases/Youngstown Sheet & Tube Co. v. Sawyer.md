## Rule

Presidential power is **relative to Congress**. Where Congress has addressed the subject and
withheld the power, the President can act only on a power that is his **exclusively** and that
Congress cannot touch. No such power reaches the seizure of private property to settle a
domestic labor dispute: not the Commander in Chief Clause, not the Vesting Clause, not the Take
Care Clause, and not an inherent emergency power.

## Facts

- **No statute authorized the seizure.** The government did not rely on statutory authority at
  all. The two statutes that did permit property seizures had conditions that were not met, and
  the government called the Defense Production Act route "much too cumbersome, involved, and
  time-consuming for the crisis." *A seizure under a statute would have been category one, with
  "the strongest of presumptions" behind it.*
- **Congress had considered seizure and rejected it.** During the Taft-Hartley debates in 1947,
  Congress voted down an amendment authorizing emergency seizures and chose mediation, boards of
  inquiry and cooling-off injunctions instead. *This is the fact that moves the case from Jackson's
  twilight zone to the lowest ebb. Had Congress been silent, the government would have had a
  real argument under category two.*
- **The object of the order was domestic and economic.** Truman ordered the Secretary of Commerce
  to take possession of the steel mills and run them to avert a strike, not to direct troops.
  *Jackson concedes "the widest latitude" to the Commander in Chief when force is "turned against
  the outside world"; the seizure fails because it was turned inward.*
- **The country was fighting in Korea.** Over 108,000 casualties, 84% of certain alloy steel going
  to military use, ammunition "holding the line." *Accepted as real and held irrelevant to the
  source of power: "the emergency did not create power" (Douglas). This is the dissent's whole
  case.*
- **Truman reported to Congress at once and invited it to act.** He sent a message the next
  morning and another twelve days later; Congress did nothing. *Vinson reads this as proof the
  President was only holding the status quo for Congress. The majority treats congressional
  inaction after the seizure as adding nothing.*
- **The seizure was expressly temporary in intent but open-ended in form.** *Frankfurter reserves
  the question of a "short, explicitly temporary" seizure that ended automatically unless Congress
  approved. A hypothetical with that feature is the one the Court did not decide.*

## Court Ruling

**Held** (6–3; Black for the Court, with five separate concurrences)

- The President's power to issue the order "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the
  Constitution itself." There is no act of Congress, so the question is purely constitutional.
- **Commander in Chief Clause.** The theater-of-war cases do not reach this. Seizing property "to
  keep labor disputes from stopping production" is "a job for the Nation's lawmakers, not for its
  military authorities."
- **Vesting and Take Care Clauses.** The power to see that the laws are faithfully executed
  "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker." The order does not execute a congressional
  policy; "it directs that a presidential policy be executed in a manner prescribed by the
  President." That is legislation, and Article I, § 1 vests it in Congress.
- **The injunction stands.** The Court affirms the District Court's preliminary injunction
  against the Secretary of Commerce.

**Rejected**

- *Past Presidents have seized private businesses in labor disputes without congressional
  authority, and that practice establishes the power.* Black: even if so, Congress "has not
  thereby lost its exclusive constitutional authority to make laws." Frankfurter takes the
  argument more seriously and still rejects it: a practice can gloss "executive Power" only if it
  is "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before
  questioned." Three seizures in six months of 1941 are not [[United States v. Midwest Oil|Midwest Oil]]'s 252 withdrawals over 80 years.
- *The aggregate of Article II powers, read together in a national emergency, supports the
  seizure.* Jackson: the Vesting Clause is not "a grant in bulk of all conceivable executive
  power"; if it were, the Framers would not have bothered listing "several specific items,
  including some trifling ones."
- *Sending troops to Korea gives the President affirmative power to secure their supply.*
  Jackson calls this the most "sinister and alarming" doctrine the Court could adopt: a President
  could enlarge his domestic power by his own commitment of forces abroad.
- *The President has inherent emergency power.* Jackson: the Framers "knew what emergencies were"
  and wrote only one emergency provision, the Suspension Clause. Emergency powers "would tend to
  kindle emergencies," and the Weimar experience shows where they lead.
- *Delay is dangerous and the President has better information.* Congress can grant emergency
  powers with "ease, expedition and safety," and has done so. The Court does not defer to the
  President's assessment of the emergency on the question of who holds the power.

## Context

Youngstown is the case every later separation-of-powers dispute reaches for, and it is
**Jackson's concurrence, not Black's opinion for the Court**, that became the law. The three
categories supply the frame for most of the executive-power unit that follows:

- **Category one** (authorized): the War Powers material in Class 9 and the [[Modern Applications of the War Power|AUMF]] test whether a statute
  authorizes.
- **Category two** (twilight): [[The Prize Cases]], where Lincoln acted before Congress met and
  Congress ratified afterwards.
- **Category three** (lowest ebb): [[Zivotofsky v. Kerry]] (2015) is the only case in which the
  Court has upheld presidential action in category three, on the ground that recognition of
  foreign sovereigns is exclusive to the President. It shows what Jackson meant by "conclusive and
  preclusive."

Black's opinion is the formalist pole. It maps each branch onto a function and asks whether the
act is lawmaking. That style returns in [[INS v. Chadha]] and in Gorsuch's dissent in
[[Gundy v. United States]]. Frankfurter's opinion is the source of the **historical gloss**
argument, which returns in the removal cases (the [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]]) and
in [[United States v. Cox]] on prosecutorial discretion.

Douglas's concurrence runs a separate, structural argument through the **Takings Clause**: a
seizure is a taking, only Congress can appropriate compensation, so only Congress can authorize
the taking. That argument links this case to the appropriations material in Class 5:
[[United States v. Lovett]] and [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]].

Justice Clark relied on [[Little v. Barreme]] (1804): where Congress prescribes how a power is to be
exercised, the President must follow that method. Barreme is the earliest statement of the
category-three principle.

[[Trump v. United States]] (2024), on the Class 10 Canvas list, uses the Jackson framework to
identify the President's "conclusive and preclusive" powers for immunity purposes. Read Class 10
back against the lowest-ebb category.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Suit by the steel companies against Secretary of Commerce Sawyer for declaratory relief and an
  injunction. The District Court granted a preliminary injunction on April 30, 1952; the D.C.
  Circuit stayed it the same day.
- The Supreme Court granted certiorari **before judgment** in the court of appeals on May 3, heard
  argument May 12, and decided June 2. The Court first rejected the government's argument that
  there was no irreparable injury, then reached the merits.

### Facts

The collective bargaining agreements in the steel industry expired on December 31, 1951, and
months of conferences between the companies and the United Steelworkers failed. The union gave
notice on April 4, 1952 of a nationwide strike beginning at 12:01 a.m. on April 9. The Wage
Stabilization Board had recommended terms the companies would not accept without a price
increase that the administration would not allow.

A few hours before the strike, Truman issued Executive Order 10340, directing Secretary Sawyer to
take possession of most of the steel mills and keep them running. Sawyer named the presidents of
the seized companies as operating managers for the United States, subject to his regulations.
The companies obeyed under protest and sued.

The United States argued in the District Court that the President had "inherent power"
"supported by the Constitution, by historical precedent, and by court decisions," and that no
injunction should issue because damages would later be available. The government relied on no
statute. Vinson's dissent adds the record: Secretary of Defense Lovett's affidavit, the draft
bringing the armed forces to 3.5 million, and the UN Charter commitments behind Korea. The
casebook omits Justice Burton's concurrence.

### Issue

- Did the President have power, without statutory authorization, to order the seizure and
  operation of the nation's steel mills to avert a strike during the Korean War?

### Holding

- No. No statute authorized the seizure, and no provision of Article II supplies the power: the
  Commander in Chief Clause does not reach domestic property seizures to settle labor disputes,
  and the executive power and Take Care Clause do not include lawmaking, which is what the order
  was.

### Rule

- The President's power to act "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution
  itself" (Black).
- Presidential power "fluctuate[s], depending upon [its] disjunction or conjunction with those of
  Congress" (Jackson):
  - **Category one.** Acting on express or implied authorization, his power is "at its maximum."
  - **Category two.** Acting in the absence of a grant or denial, he is in a "zone of twilight"
    where authority may be concurrent and the result turns on "the imperatives of events."
  - **Category three.** Acting against the express or implied will of Congress, his power is "at
    its lowest ebb," and the Court can sustain him only by holding the power "conclusive and
    preclusive," disabling Congress from acting.
- Long, systematic executive practice known to Congress and never questioned may be "a gloss on
  'executive Power'" (Frankfurter).

### Reasoning

**(Black, J., for the Court)**

- Strict function analysis: the order has the form of a statute. It recites policy reasons,
  proclaims rules of conduct and authorizes subordinate rulemaking, "like a statute."
- The President's role in lawmaking is limited to recommending and vetoing.
- Practice by earlier Presidents cannot divest Congress of its Article I power.

**(Frankfurter, J., concurring)**

- Decide narrowly. He reserves what the President could do if Congress had never legislated, or
  if the seizure were short and self-terminating.
- Congress has provided for seizures at least 16 times since 1916, always with limits. In 1947 it
  deliberately withheld the power, as clearly as if it had said "You may not seize."
- "It is an inadmissibly narrow conception of American constitutional law to confine it to the
  words of the Constitution and to disregard the gloss which life has written upon them." The
  practice here does not qualify.
- Brandeis: separation of powers was adopted "not to promote efficiency but to preclude the
  exercise of arbitrary power."

**(Douglas, J., concurring)**

- "[T]he emergency did not create power; it merely marked an occasion when power should be
  exercised."
- A seizure is a taking that requires just compensation. The branch that can pay is the only
  branch that can authorize.
- "Today a kindly President uses the seizure power to effect a wage increase"; tomorrow another
  may use it against labor.

**(Jackson, J., concurring)**

- Written from experience as Attorney General under FDR, and he says so.
- Sets out the three categories and places the seizure in category three by process of
  elimination: no authorization, and Congress has "covered" the field with three inconsistent
  statutory policies.
- Reads each Article II clause and refuses each one. The Vesting Clause is an allocation of the
  powers "thereafter stated." The Commander in Chief is not "Commander in Chief of the country,
  its industries and its inhabitants." The Take Care Clause is matched by the Due Process Clause.
- Rejects inherent emergency power, citing Weimar's Article 48 and its more than 250 invocations.
- "With all its defects, delays and inconveniences, men have discovered no technique for long
  preserving free government except that the Executive be under the law, and that the law be made
  by parliamentary deliberations."

**(Clark, J., concurring in the judgment)**

- The President has "extensive authority in times of grave and imperative national emergency."
- Where Congress has laid down procedures for the type of crisis, the President must follow them.
  [[Little v. Barreme]] controls.

### Dissent / concurrence

**(Vinson, C.J., with Reed and Minton, JJ., dissenting)**

- "[T]hese are extraordinary times." Korea, the UN commitments, the defense buildup and the
  mobilization statutes are all congressional programs, and steel production is essential to
  every one of them.
- The President was executing those programs under the Take Care Clause, holding the status quo
  "until Congress could act," like a judicial stay preserving jurisdiction.
- Presidential practice from Washington's Neutrality Proclamation through FDR's 1941–43 seizures
  shows the power "has been accepted throughout our history."
- Rejects the "messenger-boy concept of the Office," under which the President can only send a
  message and wait.
- There is no fear of dictatorship when the President acts only to save the situation until
  Congress can decide, and invites Congress to reject his course.

### Cold-call notes

- **Who wrote the controlling opinion?** Black wrote for the Court. Jackson's concurrence is what
  courts apply. Be ready to explain why a concurrence became the law: it is the only opinion that
  offers a framework for cases other than this one.
- **Where does this case fall in Jackson's scheme, and why?** Category three. The step to know is
  why it is not category two: Congress rejected seizure during Taft-Hartley. Congressional silence
  and a congressional refusal are not the same thing.
- **What is the difference between Black and Jackson?** Black asks whether the act is legislative
  in character; if so, only Congress can do it, full stop. Jackson asks what Congress has said.
  Under Black, a congressional authorization of the seizure would still raise a question about
  lawmaking by the executive. Under Jackson, it would put the President at his maximum.
- **Types of argument.** Note 1 asks directly. Black: text and structure. Frankfurter: practice
  and precedent. Douglas: structure (the Takings Clause and appropriations). Jackson: all five,
  and consequences above all (Weimar). Vinson: practice and consequences. See
  [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Frankfurter and Vinson both rely on practice. Why do they disagree?** Frankfurter requires
  practice that is long, systematic, known to Congress and unchallenged, and finds three
  instances in 1941. Vinson counts every vigorous presidential response to crisis back to
  Washington. They disagree about what counts as the relevant practice, not about whether
  practice matters.
- **Would a "short, explicitly temporary" seizure be constitutional?** Frankfurter reserved the
  question. Be ready for a hypothetical in which Truman seizes the mills for ten days pending a
  congressional vote.
- **The emergency question (Note 4).** Would an express emergency clause make the country safer or
  more dangerous? Jackson's answer is Weimar. The Constitution's one emergency provision is the
  Suspension Clause, which appears in Article I, not Article II. That placement returns in 
  [[Ex parte Merryman]] (Class 13).
- **Don't overstate the holding.** The Court did not hold that the President lacks emergency
  power in general, or that the Commander in Chief Clause has no domestic application. It held
  that this seizure, against Congress's expressed will, was unlawful.
- **Memorable lines.** Jackson: "zone of twilight"; "lowest ebb"; "conclusive and preclusive";
  "Such institutions may be destined to pass away. But it is the duty of the Court to be last, not
  first, to give them up." Frankfurter: "the gloss which life has written upon them." Vinson:
  "messenger-boy concept of the Office."
