## What it is

The opening of the casebook's Part C, "Adjudication of Genuine Disputes: 'Cases [and] Controversies,'" followed by an exchange of letters from the Neutrality Controversy of 1793. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, writing for President Washington on July 18, 1793, asked the Justices whether they would answer twenty-nine legal questions about the treaties with France and the law of nations (the casebook prints the first four). Chief Justice Jay and the Justices replied to Washington on August 8, 1793, declining to answer any of them.

The casebook assigns the exchange as "a paradigm case of what is forbidden by Article III" (Note 1). The precedent is the refusal, not the answers: the Justices treated the judicial power as limited to actual disputes between adverse parties, which is the root of both the ban on advisory opinions and the modern law of standing.

## What to notice

- **The casebook's frame.** The Court has answered "What is a case or a controversy?" with "two related rules: First, a prohibition on the issuance of 'advisory opinions,' and later, the doctrine of 'standing' to sue." At "a bare minimum standing requires an actual dispute, between adverse litigants, and a substantial likelihood that a federal court decision will have an effect in the real world."
- **The Convention had already said no.** Madison's notes show proposals to give courts power to issue advisory opinions "and they were rejected," along with a Council of Revision that would have vested the veto "jointly in the president and the justices." The Justices' letter enforces a design choice the Framers made, which makes the refusal an argument from history as well as structure.
- **Jefferson concedes there is no case.** The questions "are often presented under circumstances which do not give cognisance of them to the tribunals of the country." The administration was asking the Justices to decide questions no lawsuit had brought them.
- **Jefferson wanted the Justices' authority, not only their learning.** Their "knowledge of the subject would secure us against errors," and "their authority insure the respect of all parties." That second clause is the danger: a judicial answer, given outside a case, would lend the Court's prestige to executive policy.
- **The questions were purely legal.** The treaty questions (may France "fit out originally in & from the ports of the U.S., vessels armed for war") are the sort courts decide every day. The refusal therefore cannot rest on lack of competence; it rests on the form in which the questions arrived.
- **First reason: separation of powers.** The Justices invoke "the lines of separation drawn by the Constitution between the three departments of the government," which are "in certain respects checks upon each other." A court that advises the executive in advance is no longer a check on it.
- **Second reason: "judges of a court in the last resort."** The same questions could reach them later in a real case. Answering now would prejudge that case without parties, facts or argument, and would bind the Court's later judgment to advice given "extra-judicially."
- **Third reason: the Opinion Clause.** "[T]he power given by the Constitution to the president, of calling on the heads of the departments for opinions, seems to have been purposely as well as expressly united to the executive departments." The argument is textual inference (expressio unius): the Constitution tells the President whom to ask for written opinions, and the Justices are not on the list. The President's legal adviser became the Attorney General; [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] is that system at work.
- **The courtesy is part of the message.** "We exceedingly regret every event that may cause embarrassment to your administration," but the Justices trust the President's "usual prudence, decision, and firmness." The decision whether to fight belongs to the President, not to the Court.
- **Note 1: types of argument.** Structure (separation and checks), text (the Opinion Clause), and institutional consequences (the "last resort" point). Were they right? Yes on the strongest ground: a judge who has already announced an answer cannot sit impartially when the question comes back with real parties. See [[Types of Constitutional Argument]].
- **Note 2: what "Cases" and "Controversies" mean.** The exchange reads "judicial Power" as the power to decide cases, so that "Cases" and "Controversies" are limits and not merely a list of subjects. On that reading, the nine heads of jurisdiction describe what kinds of disputes courts may hear, and the word "Cases" requires that there be a dispute.
- **Note 3: are all opinions advisory?** Dicta and broad reasoning go beyond "A wins and B loses." The difference is that dicta arise while deciding a real dispute between adverse parties, so the reasoning is tested by the facts and binds only through the judgment. This is the same line the Class 13 Paulsen-Baude exchange drew between the binding force of a judgment and the persuasive force of an opinion; see [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- **Note 4: is the letter itself an advisory opinion?** Formally, yes: the Justices told the President what the Constitution forbids. The better view is that every court may decide whether it has jurisdiction, and declining to act is the one opinion a court can always give without a case. The letter decides nothing about anyone's rights.
- **Vary the requester.** If the Senate had asked, the Opinion Clause argument would weaken (it speaks only of the President and department heads), but the separation-of-powers and "last resort" arguments would be unchanged, so the answer would be the same. If Congress passed a statute directing the Court to answer, the statute would fail for the same reason: Congress cannot enlarge "the judicial Power" beyond cases.
- **Vary the form.** A real suit between a French privateer's owner and a British merchant over a captured ship would have presented the same treaty question and the Court would have decided it. The legal question is identical; only the presence of adverse parties with something at stake changes.
- **Formalism with a functional justification.** The rule is formal (no case, no opinion), but every reason the Justices gave is functional: preserving the Court as a check, protecting later adjudication, and keeping legal advice within the executive branch.
