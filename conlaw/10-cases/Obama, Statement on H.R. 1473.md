## What it is

President Obama's signing statement of April 15, 2011 on H.R. 1473, the "Department of Defense and
Full-Year Continuing Appropriations Act, 2011," the bill that funded the government for the rest
of fiscal year 2011. It objects to three spending restrictions: § 1112 (no funds to transfer
Guantanamo detainees into the United States), § 1113 (no funds to transfer detainees to foreign
countries unless conditions are met), and § 2262 (no funds for several positions advising the
President directly).

The casebook pairs it with [[United States v. Lovett]] on the section's first question: how far
Congress can use the power of the purse to limit the executive, and what the President may do when
he thinks a limit is unconstitutional. The footnote marked at the end of the § 2262 paragraph is
not reproduced in the extract.

## What to notice

- **Two different responses in one statement.** On §§ 1112 and 1113 Obama objects on policy and
  constitutional grounds but announces only that his Administration "will work with the Congress
  to seek repeal of these restrictions, will seek to mitigate their effects, and will oppose any
  attempt to extend or expand them." He does not say he will disregard them. On § 2262 he says
  "the executive branch will construe section 2262 not to abrogate these Presidential
  prerogatives." Only the second is a refusal to be bound.
- **Why he signed.** "I have signed this Act because of the importance of avoiding a lapse in
  appropriations for the Federal Government, including our military activities." The same hostage
  dynamic as Roosevelt in [[United States v. Lovett|Lovett]]: "The Senate yielded, as I have been
  forced to yield, to avoid delaying our conduct of the war."
- **The constitutional claim on § 1112.** A "dangerous and unprecedented challenge to critical
  executive branch authority to determine when and where to prosecute Guantanamo detainees." This
  is a claim about prosecutorial discretion, the subject of [[United States v. Cox]] in Class 8.
  Note that Congress is not directing a prosecution; it is refusing to pay for moving detainees to
  the place where one could happen.
- **The constitutional claim on § 1113.** The conditions "interfere with the authority of the
  executive branch to make important and consequential foreign policy and national security
  determinations" about transfers "in the context of an ongoing armed conflict." The argument
  borrows the Commander in Chief and foreign-affairs powers of Class 9.
- **The constitutional claim on § 2262.** The President "has well-established authority to
  supervise and oversee the executive branch, and to obtain advice," including "from advisers
  within" the White House. Laws that "significantly impede" that ability "violate the separation
  of powers by undermining the President's ability to exercise his constitutional responsibilities
  and take care that the laws be faithfully executed." The textual anchors he would need are the
  Vesting Clause, the Opinions Clause and the Take Care Clause.
- **Put § 2262 in Youngstown's scheme.** Congress has spoken expressly: no money for these
  positions. Paying the advisers anyway is category three in
  [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]], where the President must show a power
  "conclusive and preclusive" that disables Congress from acting. A power to receive advice may be
  preclusive; a power to have Congress pay for particular advisers is much harder to defend,
  because the Appropriations Clause is itself an express textual grant to Congress.
- **"construe . . . not to abrogate" is doing a lot of work.** A construction that saves the
  statute by reading it not to bar what it plainly bars is nonenforcement under another name.
  Compare Frankfurter's saving construction in Lovett, which was a court's reading, not the
  affected branch's.
- **Note 1: may Congress refuse to fund operations it disagrees with?** Generally yes: the
  Appropriations Clause means no money leaves the Treasury without a law. The limits are the
  Constitution's other provisions. Congress cannot use funding to punish named people
  ([[United States v. Lovett|Lovett]]), and it arguably cannot use conditions to accomplish what
  it could not command directly in a field of exclusive presidential power, such as recognition in
  [[Zivotofsky v. Kerry]]. It cannot reduce the President's or judges' salaries.
- **Note 2: what should the President do with an unconstitutional rider?** The options are to veto
  the whole bill, sign and comply while objecting (Roosevelt; Obama on §§ 1112–13), sign and
  refuse to comply (Obama on § 2262), or spend without an appropriation. The last runs straight
  into Art. I, § 9, cl. 7: an unconstitutional restriction may be void, but voiding it does not
  create an appropriation. For a pure funding denial the President's remedy is limited to not
  spending.
- **Note 3: is a signing statement a line-item veto?** In [[Clinton v. City of New York]] the
  cancellation changed the law's legal effect after presentment. A signing statement changes
  nothing in the law; it states how the executive will read it, and a court remains free to
  disagree. But a statement announcing nonenforcement of a valid provision would achieve in
  practice what Clinton forbade in form, and it would be worse: the Line Item Veto Act at least
  had Congress's authorization. The answer turns on whether the provision is in fact
  unconstitutional. If it is, the President is refusing to enforce a nullity, not cancelling a
  law.
- **Suspending and dispensing.** The objection to nonenforcement by signing statement is the
  seventeenth-century objection to the royal suspending and dispensing powers, which the Take Care
  Clause is often read to deny. That is the Class 8 question in [[United States v. Cox]],
  [[Adams v. Richardson]] and [[The Thompson Memo]].
- **Who decides?** The statement presupposes that the President may interpret the Constitution for
  himself in executing the law. That is the departmentalist position; the counterweight is
  [[Marbury v. Madison]]. Lovett shows the alternative path: sign, comply, and let an injured
  party take the question to court.
