# Part VI — Federalism II: Taxing, Spending, and NFIB

## 1. The taxing power

**Clause.** "The Congress shall have Power To lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises . . . but all Duties, Imposts and Excises shall be uniform throughout the United States" — Art. I § 8 cl. 1 (Art.I-8.1). Point to four more places on the page:

- **Art. I § 2 cl. 3 and § 9 cl. 4 (Art.I-2.3, Art.I-9.4)** — the apportionment rule, stated twice, for "Capitation, or other direct, Tax."
- **Art. I § 9 cl. 5 (Art.I-9.5)** — "No Tax or Duty shall be laid on Articles exported from any State."
- **Sixteenth Amendment** — income taxes "from whatever source derived, without apportionment."
- **Grammar of cl. 1** — "Taxes" is the general word and includes direct taxes; the uniformity proviso reaches only "Duties, Imposts and Excises."

**Bottom line.** The exaction should be upheld as a tax if it functions as one — revenue-raising, modest, scienter-free, IRS-collected, and leaving payment a lawful choice ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]], Roberts). If it functions as a penalty, it needs another enumerated power ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture]], Taft). If it is a tax, it must still take the right form: apportioned if direct, geographically uniform if a duty, impost or excise.

### The test

1. **Tax or penalty?** Label is irrelevant to constitutional power (NFIB). Run the [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]] factors, which NFIB turned over:
   - **Prohibitory?** Bailey's 10 percent of a year's net profits for one child for one day was; a payment "far less than the price of insurance" is not.
   - **Scienter?** "[T]ypical of punitive statutes" (NFIB, Roberts describing Bailey).
   - **Which agency enforces?** IRS points to tax; the Labor Department points to penalty.
   - **Prescribed code of conduct?** A "heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business" — the factor Taft treats as indispensable.
   - **Revenue?** "[T]he essential feature of any tax" ([[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]]).
   - **Unlawfulness?** Congress "did not think it was creating four million outlaws" (NFIB).
2. **If the natural reading is a command, is a tax reading "fairly possible"?** Constitutional avoidance, after the most natural reading fails (NFIB, Part III-C).
3. **If a penalty, what other power supports it?** Route to [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]] or [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
4. **If a tax, is it direct?** Settled direct taxes are capitations and land taxes ([[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton]]); *Pollock* added taxes on personal property and its income. Iredell's test: "the Constitution contemplated none as direct but such as could be apportioned."
5. **Does it satisfy the form rule for its class?** Direct → apportioned. Duty, impost or excise → uniform. Income → Sixteenth Amendment. Export → barred outright.
6. **Is it a lever on the States?** A tax-and-credit scheme inducing state legislation routes to spending ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]).

### Uniformity versus apportionment — the arithmetic

- **Uniform** = same rate in every State. **Apportioned** = same amount per person in every State, so the rate must differ wherever wealth differs. Apportionment protects against uniform taxes with disparate impact; uniformity protects against disparately targeted statutes (Baude, Class 21).
- **Why apportionment is fatal rather than annoying.** Two States, one million people each. State A's per-capita income is $100k; State B's is $25k. Congress wants $200M, so each State owes $100M. State A's rate: $100M ÷ $100B = 0.1%. State B's rate: $100M ÷ $25B = 0.4%. The poorer State pays four times the rate. "Must be apportioned" effectively means "cannot exist."
- **Iredell's carriage point.** "If any state had no carriages, there could be no apportionment at all." Apportionment assumed heads and wealth tracked each other, which held only for land; *Hylton*, *Pollock*, the Sixteenth Amendment and *Moore v. United States* are the law coping with that assumption failing.
- **Capitation** is a per-head tax ($10 per person), paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance." The NFIB payment escapes because it is "triggered by specific circumstances" and avoidable: buy insurance and you owe nothing. The feature that makes the mandate feel coercive is the same feature that makes it indirect.
- **Geographic uniformity.** A duty, impost or excise laid only in some States, or exempting one, fails uniformity unless the classification is genuinely non-geographic. *United States v. Ptasynski* (1983) allowed a geographically defined exemption absent actual regional favoritism; an exemption written to buy one Senator's vote is the strongest case against it.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]] (1796, seriatim; Chase, Paterson, Iredell) → a carriage tax is not direct because apportionment cannot "reasonably apply"; Paterson's third class, "[a]ll other classes of an indirect kind." Collusive record (stipulated 125 carriages; Hamilton for the government).
- *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.* (1895) → income from property is direct; reversed by the Sixteenth Amendment. Baude: hard to reconcile with Hylton.
- *License Tax Cases*; *Veazie Bank v. Fenno*; [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] (1904) → Congress may tax what it cannot regulate; courts review neither size ("not to the courts, but to the people") nor motive.
- [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture]] (1922, 8–1, Taft) → the Child Labor Tax is a penalty; "a court must be blind not to see" its object. The leading case holding a tax went too far (see also *Hill v. Wallace* (1922) and *United States v. Constantine* (1935)).
- [[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]] (1953) → a flat occupational tax on bookmakers survives though revenue is negligible.
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] (2012, 5–4 on tax, Roberts with Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan) → the shared responsibility payment is a tax and not direct (NFIB, joint dissent: "we have never held—never—that a penalty . . . was so trivial as to be in effect a tax").
- **Distinguishing line (NFIB leads; Bailey counters).** A statute writing its own code of conduct and punishing departures heavily, with scienter and non-revenue enforcement, imposes a penalty; a modest, IRS-collected, revenue-raising payment leaving a lawful choice is a tax.

### Baude in class

- He ran Hylton, Pollock, License Tax Cases, Veazie, McCray and Bailey in a row: inconsistent case law with no real limits but also a technical limit.
- Taxpayer standing to challenge a tax is "very orthodox" (Hylton). See [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- "Bailey is very ambiguous" (Class 23) — the factors are arguable in both directions, and they work as factors, not a checklist.
- His hypo (Class 21): a ban on brewing whiskey at home to help collect taxes works as the tax power plus Necessary and Proper.
- His hypo (Class 23): a tax to create a gun-free school zone probably works, because a tax is not punitive the way jail is.
- His aside: Congress could have called the mandate an income tax and avoided the direct-tax question entirely.
- Office hours: Hylton and Pollock start from the same premise — apportionment is "hard and weird" — and reach opposite conclusions: Hylton, so do not call things direct taxes and take the indirect route; Pollock, so the tax cannot be laid.
- Past-exam pattern (not a class statement): a tax question appeared in 2018 Q1, 2021 Q1 and 2023 Q2. Tax-versus-penalty drives 2021 and 2023; 2018 Q1's wildfire tax is really a direct-tax and uniformity question.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Doctrine.** The only enforced limit is Bailey's penalty line, rarely applied since (*Constantine*, 1935); once [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]] overruled [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer]], Bailey's second step lost its premise.
- **Should.** The best understanding should keep Bailey's line but anchor it in text: "Taxes" are exactions for revenue, so a measure whose face (scienter, code, non-revenue enforcer, prohibitory rate) shows revenue is not its object is not a tax — Taft's workaround of McCray, reading the statute rather than motives.
- **Strongest counter.** Kahriger and McCray: there is no administrable line, and judicial weighing of purpose corrects one department's abuse "by the abuse of its powers by another department."

### Fact triggers

- Tax/penalty: "tax" plus "knowingly," a large flat sum, or a non-IRS enforcer.
- Direct tax: an exaction per person or on ownership (wealth, head, land).
- Uniformity: the statute names States or exempts one.

### Oscillation points

- **The 2023 Q2 Gun Free Sensitive Places Tax.** $25,000, "knowingly," enforced by four non-revenue agencies: all three Bailey signals, so a penalty. Each location then needs its own power — federal property and postal power for post offices and federal courthouses; channels or instrumentalities for train stations ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]]), with [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]] weaker; schools hardest, because Lopez struck the school-zone statute.
- **The 2021 Q1 carbon tax.** $51 per ton is revenue-raising and non-prohibitory; EPA collection is the Bailey factor against; the West Virginia exemption is the uniformity problem, since an emissions levy is an excise.
- **The 2018 Q1 wildfire tax.** Per person, only in five named States: a capitation would be unapportioned; an excise non-uniform. Best defense: triggered by a circumstance and income-exempt, so not a capitation (NFIB) — and if neither direct nor a duty, impost or excise, the uniformity proviso arguably does not reach it.
- **Drafting cures.** Make it a payable credit or modest surcharge so there is no "heavy exaction for a departure" ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]); move collection to the IRS; drop scienter. Pushing the code into agency rules invites a nondelegation objection ([[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]).

### Traps

- **"The label decides."** It decides the Anti-Injunction Act, not constitutional power: Bailey turns a "tax" into a penalty; NFIB turns a "penalty" into a tax.
- **"Apportionment and uniformity are the same rule."** Same rate everywhere versus same amount per head everywhere; only a capitation satisfies both.
- **"The Sixteenth Amendment ended direct-tax questions."** It reaches only taxes "on incomes"; a wealth tax still faces Hylton and *Pollock*, and *Moore* left realization open.

### Canned hook

- "The exaction should be upheld as a tax: it raises revenue, is collected by the IRS, carries no scienter requirement, and leaves paying 'a reasonable financial decision' (NFIB, Roberts). Bailey's penalty line survives, but none of its signals is present."
- "Even if this is a tax, it must take the right form. Because it is laid only in [named States], it fails uniformity if it is an excise and fails apportionment if it is a capitation (Art.I-8.1; Art.I-9.4)."

## 2. The spending power

**Clause.** "To . . . pay the Debts and provide for the common Defence and general Welfare of the United States" — Art. I § 8 cl. 1 (Art.I-8.1). Read with Art. I § 9 cl. 7 (Art.I-9.7, appropriations) and the Necessary and Proper Clause (Art.I-8.18).

**Bottom line.** Congress may spend for the general welfare beyond its other enumerated powers ([[United States v. Butler|Butler]]) and may buy from the States what it cannot command, so long as the condition is unambiguous, germane, does not induce unconstitutional state action, and does not cross from "pressure" into "compulsion" ([[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]]). The live arguments are ambiguity and coercion; general welfare is nominal.

### The test — Dole's four limits plus the fifth

1. **General welfare.** "[C]ourts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress"; the object must be "General and not local" ([[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]]), a limit never enforced ([[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering]]: discretion belongs to Congress "unless the choice is clearly wrong").
2. **Unambiguous.** The State must "exercise [its] choice knowingly," because spending legislation is "much in the nature of a contract."
3. **Germane.** Related "to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs" — shared purpose (Dole, Rehnquist) against marginal effect (Dole, O'Connor).
4. **No independent constitutional bar.** The condition may not induce the State to act unconstitutionally; the limit is "not . . . a prohibition on the indirect achievement of objectives which Congress is not empowered to achieve directly."
5. **No coercion.** Pressure may not turn into compulsion ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]; Dole; first enforced in NFIB). NFIB's two facts: sheer size (over 10 percent of a State's budget) and old funds threatened to force a new program.

### Cases and who said what

- [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]] (1817) → "general Welfare" is limited by the enumeration; [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]] (1791) → its own end, but "General and not local"; [[The Savannah Fire Debate]] (1796, defeated 55–34) → relief to one city fails even Hamilton's limit.
- [[United States v. Butler|Butler]] (1936) → adopts Hamilton's view while striking the AAA; its "purchase compliance" half is what Dole repudiates.
- [[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]] (1937, 5–4) → "temptation" is not coercion; [[Helvering v. Davis|Helvering]] (1937) → welfare is "shaped by Congress."
- [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] (1987, 7–2, Rehnquist) → 5 percent of highway funds for a 21 drinking age is "relatively mild encouragement" (Dole, O'Connor: a "regulation determining who shall be able to drink"; Dole, Brennan: the Twenty-first Amendment is an independent bar).
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] (2012, 7–2 on coercion) → "a gun to the head" (NFIB, Roberts); Medicaid is "a single program with a constant aim" (NFIB, Ginsburg).
- [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon]] → taxpayer and declining-State suits over spending lack standing.
- **Distinguishing line (Dole leads; NFIB counters).** Threatening a State's large, existing, independent grants to force a new program is coercion; conditions on new money or modest amendments are not.

### Baude in class

- He teaches spending as the Hamiltonian view the Court came around to, against Madison's reading that "general welfare" limits only the laying of taxes and is fixed by the enumeration.
- Run Dole as four prongs plus an overall fifth limit: general welfare, unambiguous, germaneness, not unconstitutional, no coercion.
- The fourth prong is close to unusable.
- Between Dole and NFIB the unambiguous prong became the leading prong, operating almost as a nondelegation doctrine ([[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]).
- O'Connor supplies the two arguments against a condition: not germane, and her categorical limit — Congress may say where money goes but may not attach separate regulations as a quid pro quo.
- His hypo: could Congress set a national drinking age by commerce? Only via substantial effects plus a comprehensive scheme ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]], [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Raich]]) — and even then it is only a federal crime, since under [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] the State may keep the conduct legal. Spending reaches what commerce cannot: state law itself.
- Office hours: Blackmun's [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] political-process view against O'Connor's categorical limit.
- Office hours: the Dole factors decide whether an exercise of the spending power is valid or invalid — not whether it is anti-commandeering; do not use commandeering vocabulary. After NFIB the four factors remain current, with coercion as a fifth.
- Office hours: O'Connor has two separate objections — she does not buy the safety connection, and the test has no categorical limit. Baude's answer to the categorical limit is drafting: spend only on highways where teenage accidents occur, which bypasses the limit and forces her back to the weaker relatedness objection.
- Office hours: Garcia's political-process logic can justify conditional spending as a solution to a collective-action problem — States agree a rule is good but none will adopt it alone, so a national conditional program is the political process working.

### The 2018 Q1 wildfire conditions, run through the test

- **General welfare.** Mitigation conditions apply in all States; passes.
- **Unambiguous — strongest challenge.** "[P]asses regulation to substantially mitigate the risks of wildfires" gives no content in advance; whether a State complied is decided after a fire by the Secretary of the Interior. A State cannot accept "knowingly" a condition it cannot read, and judicial review of the Secretary does not cure the ex ante problem. This is the nondelegation-flavored prong Baude flagged.
- **Germane.** Fire-mitigation regulation conditioning fire-suppression money passes even O'Connor's marginal-effect test: mitigation makes suppression cheaper.
- **Independent bar.** None; the condition asks for legislation the State may lawfully enact.
- **Coercion.** The FMAG grant (about $600M against a $200B budget, 0.3 percent) is Dole-sized. But both programs are pre-existing (NFIB's reliance factor), and Forest Service "watch it burn" withholds protection of life and property, not just dollars — coercion "not merely in theory but in fact." Counter: Forest Service operations are federal services, not a grant to the State, so they may not be a "condition" at all, and the question becomes whether Congress may direct executive non-protection ([[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]]).
- **Anti-commandeering overlay.** The condition buys state legislation, which Dole permits and [[New York v. United States|New York]] forbids by command; NFIB roots the coercion limit in that same principle.
- **Ranking.** Ambiguity first, coercion via "watch it burn" second, germaneness last.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Off track.** Madison's reading has the better structural argument — the broad reading "would render nugatory the enumeration of particular powers" ([[The Bank Debate]]) — and conditional grants are the hole in Hamilton's concession that appropriation carries no power "to do any other thing."
- **Should.** A departmentalist should adopt O'Connor's categorical line: Congress may specify how its money is spent, but a requirement beyond that is "not a condition, but a regulation, which is valid only if it falls within one of Congress' delegated regulatory powers." It is administrable, tracks the text, and preserves the Hamiltonian spending power itself.
- **Strongest counter.** The First Congress funded lighthouses "from Maine to Georgia," and practice since has arguably liquidated the broad reading; O'Connor's line also invites endless fights over what counts as the funded activity.

### Fact triggers

- This is a coercion problem when the threatened money is old, large relative to the State's budget, and from a separate program.
- This is an ambiguity problem when compliance is determined after the fact by an officer.

### Oscillation point — repeal and re-enact

- **The hypo.** Congress repeals Medicaid outright and re-enacts it tomorrow with the expansion built in — one program, take it or leave it, no pre-existing funds to threaten. On Roberts's reasoning that should be fine — the hypo Baude posed in Class 23. Is the holding a structural protection or a drafting rule?
- **Ranked responses:**
  1. **Reliance, read functionally.** Coercion asks whether the State accepted "voluntarily and knowingly," and Roberts measures choice "not merely in theory but in fact." A State with fifty years of Medicaid infrastructure is not choosing on a blank slate; "existing funds" is a proxy for reliance, not a formal category.
  2. **Look through the sequencing.** Repeal-then-re-offer is substantively identical to threaten-to-withdraw; the gun is reloaded, not holstered. The burden is explaining why two lawful steps combine into an unlawful one — which is what unconstitutional-conditions doctrine does.
  3. **Limit the bundling.** Expansion is germane to Medicaid, so this bundle survives, but re-enact Medicaid conditioned on something unrelated and the bundle itself is the violation. Concedes the case, saves the doctrine.
  4. **Political impossibility.** Repeal would defund every State, including willing ones. True, and why the holding bites in practice — but an admission that the constraint is formal, not legal.

### Traps

- **"Dole's fourth limit means Congress may not do indirectly what it cannot do directly."** Dole rejects exactly that; Congress may buy what it may not command.
- **"There are four limits."** Coercion is the fifth, and the only one that has ever invalidated a spending statute.
- **"NFIB set a percentage."** Roberts declines to "fix the outermost line"; size and old-versus-new money work together.

### Canned hook

- "The condition should fail Dole's clear-notice requirement before it ever reaches coercion: a State cannot 'exercise [its] choice knowingly' when compliance is defined only after the fact by the Secretary."
- "Degree of success is not coercion (Dole); threatening money a State already relies on, at a scale that leaves 'no real option,' is (NFIB, Roberts)."

## 3. NFIB v. Sebelius as the synthesis case

**Clause.** Art. I § 8 cl. 1, cl. 3 and cl. 18 (Art.I-8.1, Art.I-8.3, Art.I-8.18), with the Tenth Amendment as the coercion backdrop.

**Bottom line.** [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] runs every federalism power on one statute and answers each differently, so it is the template for any multi-power question: commerce first, then Necessary and Proper, then tax as the avoidance backstop, then spending. Baude "REALLY" likes the opinion, so its sequence is the sequence to write in.

### The holdings, the votes, the authors

| Issue | Result | Vote | Opinion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-Injunction Act | No bar — the label controls this statute | Unanimous | Roberts |
| Commerce Clause | Mandate invalid | 5 Justices, two opinions, no opinion of the Court | Roberts (Part III-A, alone) + joint dissent |
| Necessary and Proper | Mandate not "proper" | Same five | Roberts alone + joint dissent |
| Taxing power | Mandate valid as a tax; not direct | 5–4, opinion of the Court (Part III-C) | Roberts + Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan |
| Medicaid coercion | Threat to existing funds invalid | 7–2 | Roberts + Breyer, Kagan; joint dissent agrees |
| Remedy | Bar the threat only; expansion stands as an offer | 5–4 | Roberts plurality + Ginsburg, Sotomayor in the judgment |

- Only Roberts agreed with the whole of the reasoning and the judgment.
- **Vote-counting move.** It is unclear whether the commerce holding had five votes to make it binding: the joint dissenters agreed with Roberts's reasoning but joined no part of his opinion. Ginsburg calls it dictum because the tax holding carried the judgment; Roberts answers that the saving construction becomes available only after the straightforward commerce reading fails, so the commerce step was necessary. Class note (marked uncertain, "[?]"): the Marks rule — the narrowest ground becomes the holding.

### Cases and who said what

- **Commerce.** "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" (NFIB, Roberts); "If all inactivity affecting commerce is commerce, commerce is everything" (NFIB, joint dissent); forgoing insurance "is hardly inconsequential or equivalent to 'doing nothing'" (NFIB, Ginsburg). [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] limits by the kind of activity; NFIB by its existence. See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].
- **Necessary and Proper.** The mandate is a "great substantive and independent power," not an incident ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]), and creating "the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not proper (NFIB, Roberts); "You will know it when you see it" (NFIB, Ginsburg). Baude's own scholarship argues some powers are too great to be implied — the theory Roberts used. See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
- **Tax.** Functional, "fairly possible" reading; [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]] inverted on the same factors. See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].
- **Spending.** Coercion descends from anti-commandeering ([[New York v. United States|New York]]; [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]). See [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]] and [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].

### Baude in class

- Not buying insurance could be economic, but cannot be activity; his answer to the broccoli parable: maybe Congress could force people to buy broccoli, but not to eat it.
- N&P: the mandate is too affirmative to be incidental (see Printz) — people cannot be commandeered; Ginsburg attacks the test as unworkable.
- Tax "must be a back up," reached by avoidance — "Why would we bail out Congress?" Open: is an inescapable tax on inactivity a new kind of capitation?
- Medicaid passes Dole's four factors but flunks coercion: (1) the amount of money, (2) old funds threatened, in reliance.
- Consequences: pay and move on; a zeroed tax might not be a tax; States may refuse the new money.
- Office hours: call the Medicaid problem an invalid use of the spending power, not commandeering. Medicaid is a quasi-state program run through state agencies, far beyond the ministerial duties Printz left open; a Printz-type program funded by conditions goes straight to Dole plus coercion.
- EXAM SIGNAL: he "REALLY" likes this opinion.

### Doctrine vs. best understanding

- **Off track.** Avoidance deployed after an adverse constitutional ruling lets the Court announce new limits costlessly; the tax holding "save[s] a statute Congress did not write" (NFIB, joint dissent).
- **Should.** The best understanding should keep Roberts's framework: Article I "enumerates not federally soluble problems, but federally available powers" (NFIB, joint dissent), and the "great power" screen is the only limit on the Necessary and Proper Clause that tracks McCulloch's own text. The tax holding is right too, because a power Congress has does not disappear for being mislabeled.
- **Strongest counter.** Ginsburg: the activity line is formalism with no economic content, and "[t]his Court's former endeavors to impose categorical limits on the commerce power have not fared well."

### Fact triggers

- This is an activity/inactivity problem when the statute compels a purchase or a status.
- This is a "great power" problem when the means is an independent, freestanding power dressed as an incident (compelled purchase, eminent domain, conscription).

### Traps

- **"NFIB held the mandate invalid under the Commerce Clause, for the Court."** No opinion of the Court on commerce; count the votes.
- **"The Medicaid expansion was struck down."** Only the threat to existing funds fell; the expansion survives as an offer.
- **"The tax question is closed."** Congress zeroed the payment in 2017; *California v. Texas* (2021) dismissed on standing, leaving open whether a $0 tax is a tax.

### Canned hook

- "Following NFIB's sequence, the provision should first be tested under the commerce power, then the Necessary and Proper Clause, and only then saved, if it can be, as a tax — which requires only a 'fairly possible' reading (NFIB, Roberts)."
- "The commerce holding had no opinion of the Court: five Justices agreed in two opinions, so whether it binds is itself contestable (NFIB, Ginsburg)."
