# Part 0 — The Exam Engine

## 1. Format and clock

- **Date:** December 9, 2026. In-class, closed-book.
- **Materials (office hours, 11/1):** one sheet of paper, front and back — confirmed. A full copy of the Constitution — *might* be supplied. Know the core clause cites cold either way; if the text is supplied, cite from it rather than from memory.
- **Unknowns:** length of the sitting, number of questions, word limits. Every past Baude Con Law exam had three questions totalling 100 points, with a word cap on each. Plan for that shape and adjust at minute zero.
- **The ratio:** 35 words per point in 2018; 30 words per point in 2021 and 2023, on every question. If caps appear on the cover page, read them off; if none appear, budget by points and keep the same proportions.
- **Weights move every year:** 30/50/20 (2018), 50/20/30 (2021), 45/30/25 (2023). Allocate time by points before reading Question One.
- **The standing instructions** (printed on the 2018, 2021 and 2023 exams, and therefore the rubric):
  - "If there are additional facts you need to know, please say what they are and how they would affect your analysis."
  - "Don't assume that I'll know you know the basics; show me!" (2018 and 2021; dropped from the 2023 cover page, but still the grading habit)
  - "Grades will be based on your understanding, analysis, and clarity of exposition."
  - "Sketch an outline to each of the questions before you actually begin to write."
  - 2023 added: "save a significant amount of time at the end for proofreading."
- **What is tested** (syllabus, verbatim): "what the Supreme Court has said the Constitution means — i.e., the doctrine," and "what the Constitution actually requires — i.e., places the doctrine may be off track. Both of these things will be tested on the exam."

### Clock template (scale to the real sitting)

| Step | Share of time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Minute zero | 2% | Points × ratio for each question; write the budgets in the margin |
| Per question: read + outline | 15% of that question's time | Gates in order, a holding on each, word budget per gate |
| Per question: write | 75% | Gate openings first, then bodies; spend the whole budget |
| Per question: buffer | 10% | Missing-fact sentences, verdict restated, proofread |
| End | Reserve 8–10 minutes | Word counts; one pass for dropped "not"s and wrong case names |

For a three-hour sitting at 100 points: 1.8 minutes per point, so a 45-point question gets about 80 minutes.

## 2. What Baude rewards — the ten rules

1. **Bottom line in sentence one.** Zero of fourteen circulated Con Law openings restate the question. "Bicameralism and presentment requirements are met (Art.I-7)."
2. **Mirror the prompt's labels as headings,** then convert the prompt's verbs into sub-headings: *Could / Should*, *Current doctrine / Best understanding*, the prompt's locations in the prompt's order (2023 Q2: post office, courthouses, school, train station — the exemplars split courthouses into federal and state).
3. **Cite clauses, not statutes,** in his house shorthand: Art.I-7, Art.II-2.2, Art.I-9.7. Seventeen clause cites in one 3,500-word exemplar. If the Constitution is supplied, use it; if not, the cites you need are the ones in Part X's clause map.
4. **Case plus Justice** is the citation currency: (Youngstown, Jackson) · (Raich, Scalia) · (Gundy, Kagan) · (NFIB, joint dissent). Six to thirteen named Justices per strong script.
5. **Count votes.** "Unclear whether that holding had five votes" is a rewarded move. Know which opinions are pluralities (Gundy), which holdings are one-Justice controlling (NFIB commerce), which decisions were stay orders (Cook).
6. **Rank the arguments.** "California's strongest challenge is…"; "a slightly weaker theory is…"
7. **Attribute and answer every counterargument,** with a weight judgment: "This is a fairly weak argument, however."
8. **Name the missing fact, say how it flips the result, then answer anyway.** Never "we cannot say."
9. **Commit with "should."** Hedge the argument, never the writer's own verb. Arguing the Court is wrong is graded content.
10. **Element-march.** "Show me" means every element of every test appears on the page, even the easy ones, disposed of in a clause.

**Class signals that bear on the whole exam**

- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 1): make constitutional arguments to a fact set.
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 16): keep three kinds of argument separate — **policy**, **constitutional**, and **constitutional that a court will agree with** — and do not let the answer become inconsistent across them. Understand [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] and the separation of powers fully.
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 17): enumerate each person's view and how Marshall responded; Madisonian liquidation.
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 18): Baude's liquidation theory — dig deep. See Part I § 2.
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 20): always level-set which commerce prong is in play and which case supplies it.
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 7): can the unitary-executive and congressional-control arguments reach a middle ground? See Part III § 2.
- EXAM SIGNAL (Class 15): what are the limits on congressional jurisdiction stripping? See Part IV § 4.
- Office hours (11/1): structure arguments cannot rank Article I above the Bill of Rights because it comes first, or discount amendments for being amendments; the available flip is that a later amendment clarifies original meaning.
- Office hours (11/8): on spending conditions, do not use the commandeering label — the Dole factors (plus coercion as the fifth) decide whether the spending is a valid exercise of the spending power.

## 3. The two-track structure

Every fact pattern can be answered twice, and Baude has asked for both explicitly (2021 Q1: 20 points doctrine, 30 points "proceeding as a departmentalist"). Even when the prompt asks only one, a short second track is where the 186 lives.

| Track | Question | Authority currency | Verdict form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Current doctrine** | What would a court bound by these precedents do? | Holdings, votes, controlling opinions | "A district court should hold…" |
| **B. Best understanding** | What does the Constitution actually require, and what should a departmentalist official do? | Text, structure, original meaning, liquidated practice, named Justices' positions, Baude's scholarship | "The President should conclude…, because…" |

**Track B in four moves** (the 2021 Q1 instruction: "tell the President how the Constitution should be interpreted, and how that interpretation applies"):

1. **State the method in one sentence.** Default: original public meaning, with indeterminacies settled by liquidated practice, and precedent given weight according to its quality and reliance. See Part I §§ 1–2.
2. **Defend it in two or three sentences** against the strongest rival (living constitutionalism / common-law constitutionalism; Frankfurter's gloss; the dead-hand objection, Part I § 3).
3. **Apply it gate by gate,** in the same order as Track A, so the parallel structure carries the argument.
4. **Mark where the tracks diverge** and say which is right: "Here the doctrine is off track, and the President should not adopt it."

**The departmentalist's legal duties, in order** (Part IV § 2): a judgment binds and must be enforced unless the court lacked jurisdiction (Baude, *The Judgment Power*); an opinion's reasoning binds other branches only as persuasive authority; the President may sign a bill with an unconstitutional part when other duties justify signing and he stands ready not to enforce that part (Baude, *Signing Unconstitutional Laws*); the veto and the pardon are the President's own constitutional judgments ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).

## 4. The universal attack sequence

Run every fact through these gates before writing. Not every gate fires; every gate gets asked.

**G0 — Role and ask.** Whose lawyer am I (White House, Senate staff, clerk, governor's adviser)? What exactly is asked — could, should, strongest, which parts? The role sets the verdict form: a clerk predicts, an adviser recommends, a President decides.

**G1 — Forum and threshold.**
- Is anyone in court? If so, who has standing — a State (sovereign or proprietary injury; *parens patriae* against the United States barred by [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon]]), a citizen or taxpayer (generalized grievance barred by [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] and *Frothingham*), a House of Congress (institutional injury, [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)|House v. Burwell]])? Part IV § 3.
- Is the question committed elsewhere — impeachment, the Guarantee Clause ([[Luther v. Borden]]), Article V procedure ([[Coleman v. Miller]])?
- If no court will hear it, the question does not disappear: it moves to the political branches, and the answer becomes a Track B answer (2023 Q1B: "imagine instead that nobody has standing").

**G2 — Who is acting?**
- Congress by statute → G3 source of power.
- One House, a committee, or Congress without the President → bicameralism and presentment (Part II § 4).
- The President with Congress, against Congress, or in silence → Youngstown category (Part II § 2).
- An agency or officer → is the actor an officer (Part III § 1), removable by whom (Part III § 2), acting on what delegation (Part II § 3)?
- A court or a court-located body → Article III (Part IV).
- A State → Article IV, dormant commerce, supremacy (Part VII), or the State as the regulated object (Part V § 4).

**G3 — Source of power.** For Congress: name the clause. Commerce (which of the three [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|Lopez]] categories — Class 20 signal), tax (Part VI § 1), spending (Part VI § 2), Necessary and Proper (Part V § 2), war (Part III § 4), Art. IV § 3 property and territory (Part VII § 4). For the President: Article II's text (vesting, Commander in Chief, Take Care, pardon, appointment) plus statutory authorization.

**G4 — Process constraints.** Bicameralism and presentment (Art.I-7); appointments (Art.II-2.2); appropriations (Art.I-9.7); the intelligible principle (Art.I-1).

**G5 — External limits.** Anti-commandeering (Part V § 4); coercion and the [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] conditions (Part VI § 2); tax-or-penalty, direct taxes, uniformity, export taxes (Part VI § 1); bills of attainder (Part II § 7); suspension (Part II § 6); removal limits (Part III § 2); Article III independence (Part IV § 4); privileges and immunities and dormant commerce for States (Part VII §§ 1–2).

**G6 — Political-branch remedies and counter-moves.** Impeachment (Part III § 5); defunding (Part II § 5); jurisdiction stripping and court packing (Part IV § 4); veto, signing statement, non-enforcement (Part III § 3); a successor President's reversal (2023 Q1C); elections.

**G7 — Best understanding.** Section 3 above.

**G8 — Missing facts and remedy.** For each gate, the one fact that would flip it. Then severability: which parts survive ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley]] severed the appointment mechanism; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] limited the Medicaid remedy to the new funds).

## 5. Paragraph templates

**Gate opening (25–40 words).** Disposition + authority + the reason in a clause.
> "The $25,000 'tax' is a penalty, not a tax (Art.I-8.1; Bailey; NFIB, Roberts): it is prohibitory in size, applies only to knowing possession, and is collected by the Postal Service and three other agencies rather than the IRS."

**Pre-scored factors.** Name the factors, score each, then analyse.
> "Four things decide whether the exaction is a penalty (Bailey): a prohibitory rate, a scienter requirement, enforcement by a regulating agency rather than the revenue collector, and a prescribed code of conduct, which Taft called indispensable. The first three point toward penalty; the government's best reply is that the fourth is missing."

**The whipsaw.** Trap the government between two forms.
> "Even if the wildfire exaction is a tax, it takes no valid form: as a per-person charge it is a capitation that is not apportioned (Art.I-9.4); as an indirect tax laid on five named States it is not uniform (Art.I-8.1)."

**Ranked arguments.**
> "On the spending condition, California's strongest argument is that 'substantially mitigate the risks' is too ambiguous to be knowingly accepted (Dole; Pennhurst); its next strongest is that cutting off pre-existing programs it relies on is coercive (NFIB); the germaneness argument is weak."

**The named counter, answered and weighed.**
> "Justice Scalia would argue the intelligible-principle test is unmanageable, but he would likely uphold this delegation because it is one of carrying out the law (Mistretta, Scalia dissenting). That argument is weaker here, because…"

**The missing fact.**
> "If the 'fire-prone' designation tracks a neutral criterion, the uniformity objection fails; if Congress chose the five States by name to burden them, the tax is geographically non-uniform. I assume the former, so…"

**The Track B pivot.**
> "That is what current doctrine requires. The best understanding of the Constitution differs at one point: [gate]. A departmentalist President should conclude…, because the text… and the practice from 1789 to 1791 liquidated…"

**The close.** One sentence restating the verdict, plus word count if caps exist: "The President should sign the bill, state in a signing statement that the West Virginia exemption raises a uniformity problem, and ask Congress to cure it. (WC: 1,498)"

## 6. Canned hooks — portable moves that push the ball

Each hook is a sentence Ashwin can deploy in any answer where its trigger appears. Hooks drawn from Baude's own work are marked **[Baude]** — he is the reader.

- **Liquidation [Baude, *Constitutional Liquidation*, 71 Stan. L. Rev. 1 (2019)].** Trigger: an argument from historical practice. "Practice can settle the meaning of an indeterminate provision, but only if three conditions hold: a genuine textual indeterminacy, a course of deliberate practice reflecting constitutional reasoning, and a settlement marked by the losing side's acquiescence and public sanction. Practice can expound the Constitution; it cannot alter it." Part I § 2.
- **Liquidation versus gloss.** Trigger: [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]], removal, war powers, long-standing agency practice. "Frankfurter's 'systematic, unbroken executive practice' asks only how long; liquidation asks whether the practice was deliberate, whether the other side acquiesced, and whether the text was open to begin with. On that test, [[The Decision of 1789]] counts and ninety years of independent agencies do not — 'no branch may rely on adverse possession' ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]], Roberts)."
- **Judgment versus opinion [Baude, *The Judgment Power*, 96 Geo. L.J. 1807 (2008)].** Trigger: defiance, departmentalism, [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]], [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Merryman]], [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]]. "The President must enforce a federal court's judgment unless the court lacked jurisdiction; he need not adopt its reasoning as his own rule for other cases. Lincoln's position on Dred Scott is the model: obey the judgment as to Dred Scott, resist the opinion as a political rule." Paulsen's "Merryman power" is the foil — and Paulsen co-edits the casebook.
- **Signing unconstitutional laws [Baude, 86 Ind. L.J. 303 (2011)].** Trigger: a bill presented to the President (2021 Q1). "There is no categorical duty to veto a bill with an unconstitutional provision. The President may sign it when his other duties justify enacting the rest, provided he stands ready not to enforce the unconstitutional part, because nothing unconstitutional happens until enforcement." Cross-ref [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]].
- **Great powers [Baude, *Rethinking the Federal Eminent Domain Power*, 122 Yale L.J. 1738 (2013)].** Trigger: Necessary and Proper. "Some powers are too great to be implied as incidental — McCulloch's 'great substantive and independent power.' Chief Justice Roberts used that idea against the mandate in NFIB." Baude's own application is federal eminent domain within the States; he calls Roberts's use questionable and suggests commandeering, sovereign immunity and conscription as stronger candidates. Part V § 2.
- **Kind of power, not kind of procedure [Baude, *Adjudication Outside Article III*, 133 Harv. L. Rev. 1511 (2020)].** Trigger: a tribunal or adjudicator outside Article III; Slaughter's reserved category. "The question is what kind of governmental power the body exercises, not whether it adjudicates. An executive adjudicator exercises executive power, bounded by due process — which after Slaughter suggests the President can control it."
- **How to save the Fed [Baude, "How To Save The Federal Reserve," *Divided Argument* blog, May 2025 — his writing, not a class statement].** Trigger: [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Cook]]. Before the decisions, Baude suggested the Court use its stare decisis discretion to preserve [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's]] for the Fed alone. The Court instead rested on First-and-Second-Bank history. Ask which ground is more honest: a precedent-based carve-out admits the line is pragmatic; a history-based carve-out claims it is principled and invites Justice Thomas's reply that the Banks "possessed no sovereign power."
- **"It is a constitution we are expounding."** Trigger: any expressio unius or "the text doesn't mention it" argument against a federal power. [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]] (Marshall): a constitution marks "only its great outlines," so incidental powers need not be listed.
- **The lowest ebb.** Trigger: the President acting against a statute. "Presidential power is at its lowest ebb, and the President can rely only on his own constitutional powers minus any constitutional powers of Congress over the matter" (Youngstown, Jackson). Then ask whether the power is "conclusive and preclusive" ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]).
- **"Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."** Trigger: any multimember independent body ([[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Slaughter]], Roberts).
- **Formalism / functionalism pivot.** Trigger: any separation-of-powers question. "Under the formalist approach of Chadha and Slaughter, the question is which branch's power this is; under Morrison's functionalism, whether the arrangement impermissibly undermines a branch's core function. The Court has used both, and the answer here turns on which governs."
- **The dead hand answered.** Trigger: a normative "why follow the 1787 text" argument. "The dead-hand objection is strongest against a Constitution that cannot be changed; Article V, liquidation, and the people's ongoing acquiescence are the three answers, and the hardest of the three is Article V's difficulty." Part I § 3; Part VII § 5.
- **Political safeguards and their limits.** Trigger: [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]], spending, commandeering. "Garcia trusts the political process to protect the States; Printz and NFIB conclude that some structural protections cannot be left to it. The question is which protections the political process actually supplies."

## 7. Writing it — the Transactions method, adapted

- **Write it pissed off; stay calm.** Every fact in a Baude prompt is load-bearing: the $200B budget, the 75% reimbursement, "watch it burn," the five named States, the $25,000 "knowingly," the agency that collects. Before writing, underline each number, actor, and verb and assign it to a gate. A fact with no gate is a gate not yet found.
- **Peel and ram.** For each gate: rule → each element → apply facts → strongest counter, named → weight → verdict. Then the next gate.
- **Hooks that push forward.** Every canned hook must end by applying to the facts. A hook that only shows knowledge is wasted words against a cap.
- **Regurgitate his research — accurately.** Liquidation, judgment power, signing unconstitutional laws, great powers, adjudication outside Article III. One precise use beats three name-drops.
- **Spill every tie-in, then come out on a side.** Weave in the adjacent doctrine (tax ↔ commerce ↔ N&P; removal ↔ immunity ↔ impeachment), but every paragraph ends in a verdict.
- **Structure before writing.** Headings first, mirroring the prompt; gate list with word budgets; then write.
- **Control the tempo.** If a gate runs 50% over budget, cut to its verdict and move on. Return only if words remain.
- **End-of-question checklist:** bottom line in sentence one? every heading mirrors the prompt? every element shown? strongest argument ranked? counter named and weighed? missing fact named? Track B present? verdict restated? word count?
