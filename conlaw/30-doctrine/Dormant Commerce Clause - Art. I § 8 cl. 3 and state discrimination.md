## Rule statement

A State may not favor its own economic interests by burdening out-of-state competitors, but outside that core the dormant Commerce Clause now does very little. The negative implication of Art. I, § 8, cl. 3 forbids "regulatory measures designed to benefit in-state economic interests by burdening out-of-state competitors," a principle that "lies at the 'very core'" of the doctrine ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]). There is no freestanding "almost per se" rule against laws with extraterritorial effects, so absent purposeful discrimination a State may condition in-state sales on how a good was produced elsewhere. Pike survives, but its substantial-burden threshold does most of the work, and its balance has no majority behind it. The contest is over footing: several Justices think the antidiscrimination principle "may be more appropriately housed elsewhere in the Constitution," and the Court now sends disputes about one State's reach to structure, "sovereignty and comity," Due Process and Full Faith and Credit.

## Elements

1. **Has Congress acted?** A federal statute displacing or authorizing the state law ends the dormant inquiry; in [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] Congress "has yet to adopt any statute that might displace Proposition 12."
   - The rule is old: in *Willson v. Black-Bird Creek Marsh Co* Marshall upheld a state dam across a navigable creek because "congress has passed no such act," and the Taney Court's police-power cases, which the casebook says would now be called dormant Commerce Clause cases, include *Cooley v. Board of Wardens*, distinguishing subjects "imperatively demanding a single uniform rule" from those demanding local "diversity."
2. **Does the law discriminate on its face by origin?** A law that bars or burdens goods because they come from another State operates "like 'a tariff or customs duty'" and fails at the core of the doctrine, with no Pike balancing.
   - Founding-era models: Rhode Island's tax on imported "New-England Rum"; Connecticut's duties on goods "brought into th[e] State . . . from any of the United States of America."
3. **Does the law discriminate in purpose or in practical operation?** "[T]he Pike line serves as an important reminder that a law's practical effects may also disclose the presence of a discriminatory purpose," and in that line "the presence or absence of discrimination in practice proved decisive."
   - Arizona's order in *Pike v. Bruce Church* required "business operations to be performed in [state] that could more efficiently be performed elsewhere."
4. **Is the law a price-control or price-affirmation statute?** *Baldwin v. G. A. F. Seelig*, *Brown-Forman* and *Healy* condemned laws tying in-state prices to out-of-state prices that deliberately "prevent[ed out-of-state firms] from undertaking competitive pricing"; [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] files them under discrimination, not under a separate extraterritoriality rule.
5. **If the law is nondiscriminatory, does the complaint plausibly plead a substantial burden on the interstate market?**
   - A "speculative possibility" of harm is not enough.
   - The Clause protects "the interstate market . . . from prohibitive or burdensome regulations," not "particular . . . firms" or "particular structure[s] or methods of operation" (*Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland*); "That goes for pigs no less than gas stations."
   - Compliance costs falling mostly on out-of-state firms are not, without more, a burden on the market (Part IV-C, four Justices).
6. **Only then: is the burden "clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits"?** That is Pike as Roberts, C.J., restates it in his partial dissent; three Justices say the balance cannot be struck where the benefits are noneconomic, because the task "is like being asked to decide 'whether a particular line is longer than a particular rock is heavy.'"
7. **Would another clause do the work?**
   - Art. IV, § 2 for discrimination against out-of-state citizens in fundamental rights ([[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]]; [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]]).
   - Due Process and Full Faith and Credit for regulation of wholly out-of-state conduct.
   - The "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause — that is, Congress — for nationwide disruption; see [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption:** the democratically adopted state law stands. "'[E]xtreme caution' is warranted before a court deploys this implied authority," and displacing a state law under the Clause is "a matter of 'extreme delicacy,'" done only "where the infraction is clear" ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]], Part V, for the Court).
- **Standard — discrimination:** a law discriminating by origin is condemned at the doctrine's core without Pike balancing.
- **Burden — nondiscrimination:** the plaintiff must plead facts plausibly showing a substantial burden on interstate commerce, "a threshold requirement that plaintiffs must satisfy before courts need even engage in Pike's balancing and tailoring analyses" (Sotomayor, J., concurring in part). The case was decided on the pleadings, with the allegations taken as true, and the plaintiffs still lost.
- **Standard — beyond the threshold:** there is no controlling account. Parts I, II, III, IV-A and V are the opinion of the Court; Part IV-B (incommensurability) has three votes; Part IV-C (no substantial burden pleaded) has four; Barrett supplies the third vote for IV-B and withholds the fifth for IV-C, Sotomayor and Kagan do the reverse, and four Justices would have vacated and remanded.
- **Standard — the sibling clause:** Art. IV, § 2 is stricter on the State. If the right is fundamental to the promotion of interstate harmony, the State must show a substantial reason for the discrimination and a close relation between the discrimination and that reason (the modern test described with [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]]).
- **What fills the gap:** Congress. The Court points petitioners to the "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause and wonders whether they sued because "winning a majority of a handful of judges may seem easier than marshaling a majority of elected representatives across the street" — the same hand-back to politics, for want of a judicial standard, as in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]].

## Exceptions

- **Congressional authorization or displacement.** The remedy the Court points to is a federal statute, because the "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause gives Congress both the power and the institutional competence.
- **Inspection, quarantine and health laws.** [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] recognized reserved state powers "sure to have 'a remote and considerable influence on commerce' in other States," and an "immense mass" of such laws has always been valid.
- **Bans on goods a State deems immoral or unsafe.** "[A] State may exclude from its territory, or prohibit the sale therein of any articles which, in its judgment, fairly exercised, are prejudicial to" its citizens' interests. Petitioners conceded that States may ban goods made with child labor, and on a burden-only test "one complaint from an unhappy out-of-state producer" would force States to allow horsemeat, fireworks and single-use plastic bags (Parts IV-B and IV-D, three Justices).
- **Harm to particular firms or their methods of operation.** Under *Exxon* the Clause does not protect "particular structure[s] or methods of operation," so a complaint that today's producers must rebuild their barns is not a burden on the market.
- **Costs borne by in-state consumers.** Costs "ultimately borne by in-state consumers thanks to a law they adopted" are not a cognizable harm (Part IV-C, four Justices).
- **Noneconomic out-of-state harms.** The cases authorize claims alleging "burdens on commerce," not "a roving license" to reassess state legislation "in light of any conceivable out-of-state interest, economic or otherwise" (Part IV-D, three Justices).
- **A State's common property (Art. IV, § 2).** [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]] treats an unappropriated oyster fishery as owned by the State's citizens as "tenants in common"; *Toomer v. Witsell* later rejected ownership as a ground for hoarding fisheries, so state "ownership" is no longer a trump.
- **Corporations under Art. IV, § 2.** The Privileges and Immunities Clause protects citizens, and corporations are not citizens for its purposes (*Paul v. Virginia*), which is why a trade association's claim must be brought under the Commerce Clause.
- **Not decided: regulation of wholly out-of-state sales.** A law requiring that pork sold anywhere by a firm doing business in the State be compliant is not decided; Gorsuch's own examples suggest it would fail, but under Due Process and sovereignty principles rather than an extraterritoriality rule.
- **Not decided: market-size leverage.** Gorsuch's answer to the claim that a large State can set national policy is that otherwise "voters in States with smaller markets are constitutionally entitled to greater authority to regulate in-state sales than voters in States with larger markets. So much for the Constitution's 'fundamental principle of equal sovereignty among the States'" — but that is Part IV-D, three Justices.
- **Not decided: where the antidiscrimination principle belongs.** Members of the Court have suggested the Import-Export Clause, the Privileges and Immunities Clause or preemption, and the Court says "we have no need to engage with any of them to resolve this case."

## Leading case

[[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]

- **Holding:** there is no "almost per se" rule against laws with the "practical effect of controlling" out-of-state commerce, and a Pike claim pleading no discrimination "falls well outside Pike's heartland" (Gorsuch, J., for the Court in Parts I, II, III, IV-A and V; judgment affirmed 5–4 on the pleadings).
- **Driving fact — the concession:** petitioners disavowed any discrimination claim, conceding "that Proposition 12 imposes the same burdens on in-state pork producers that it imposes on out-of-state ones."
- **Driving fact — what the law regulates:** Proposition 12 forbids the in-state **sale** of whole pork from breeding pigs "confined in a cruel manner," so it regulates a practice rather than an origin.
- **Reasoning — the trilogy reclassified:** Baldwin's minimum-price law "plainly discriminate[d]"; Brown-Forman and Healy "differed from Baldwin only in that they involved price-affirmation, rather than price-fixing, statutes"; and "[t]he language of an opinion is not always to be parsed as though we were dealing with language of a statute."
- **Reasoning — consequences:** "many (maybe most) state laws have the 'practical effect of controlling' extraterritorial behavior," including income tax, environmental, libel, securities, franchise and tort laws. "Can anyone really suppose Baldwin, Brown-Forman, and Healy meant to do so much?"
- **Reasoning — the pleadings (Part IV-C, four Justices):** 28% of the industry had already converted to group housing and complying producers could pass on some costs, so substantial harm to interstate commerce "remains nothing more than a speculative possibility."
- **Separate opinions:** Sotomayor (with Kagan) joins all but Parts IV-B and IV-D and would keep Pike balancing; Barrett agrees the goods are incommensurable but finds the burden adequately pleaded; Roberts, C.J. (with Alito, Kavanaugh and Jackson) would vacate and remand under Pike as traditionally stated; Kavanaugh also wrote separately.
- **Quote:** "While the Constitution addresses many weighty issues, the type of pork chops California merchants may sell is not on that list." On balancing, for three Justices: "Your guess is as good as ours. . . . More accurately, your guess is better than ours."

## Best counter-case

[[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Art. IV, § 2 protects a citizen against discrimination in fundamental rights and makes the State justify it, while the dormant Commerce Clause protects the interstate market, corporations included, against protectionism and defers to the State absent discrimination.
- **Holding:** the clause secures only privileges and immunities "which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments," as a rule of equal treatment rather than a transfer of one State's citizenship to visitors.
- **Driving fact:** New Jersey reserved an unappropriated oyster fishery, which Justice Washington treats as held by its citizens as "tenants in common"; reading the clause to open it would be "a grant of a cotenancy in the common property of the state, to the citizens of all the other states."
- **Argue for the Article IV route:** Washington's catalogue includes "[t]he right of a citizen of one state to pass through, or to reside in any other state, for purposes of trade" and "an exemption from higher taxes or impositions than are paid by the other citizens of the state," and the modern test puts a real burden of justification on the State, unlike the Pike threshold.
- **Argue for the commerce route:** the [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|Pork Producers]] petitioners would have lost under Article IV as well — they are corporations and associations rather than citizens, no citizen was treated worse than a Californian, and Proposition 12 restricts a sale rather than denying a fundamental privilege.
- **Shared history:** Corfield itself rejected a dormant commerce claim, citing [[Gibbons v. Ogden]], in a passage the casebook does not print; the Pork Producers Syllabus cites "Art. I, §8, cl. 3; Art. IV, §2" together.
- **Holding versus dictum:** the famous catalogue is dictum, and the operative rule is the narrow common-property exception; the Fourteenth Amendment's drafters nonetheless borrowed the dictum from a case that upheld a discriminatory state law.
- **Method:** Washington reasons from natural law and the law of nations (Grotius, Rutherforth) and from consequences; Gorsuch reasons from judicial competence and consequences. Neither doctrine has a strong textual anchor, which is why both keep retreating to caution.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The law controls conduct in other States, so it violates the dormant Commerce Clause."** After [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] there is no "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule; the interests it protected were redistributed to the antidiscrimination principle, to Due Process and Full Faith and Credit, and to "original and historical understandings of the Constitution's structure."
- **"The Court killed Pike."** It did not. The Court preserves the "courtroom door open" language, concedes that "a small number of our cases have invalidated state laws . . . that appear to have been genuinely nondiscriminatory," and four Justices would have let this very claim proceed.
- **"Part IV is the opinion of the Court."** Only Part IV-A is. Part IV-B (incommensurability) has three votes, Part IV-C (pleading) has four, and Part IV-D has three; attributing the incommensurability holding to the Court is the cleanest way to be wrong in a cold call.
- **"Costs to out-of-state producers are a burden on commerce."** The complaint alleged that "the majority" of compliance costs "will be initially borne by out-of-state firms," and that was accepted as pleaded and held insufficient. What might have worked is an allegation that no producer could profitably comply, so supply would fail and the national market would contract.
- **"Art. IV, § 2 and the dormant Commerce Clause are interchangeable."** Art. IV, § 2 protects citizens in fundamental rights and does not reach corporations; the dormant Commerce Clause protects the market and does. The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause is a third thing, borrowing Washington's phrase for rights against a citizen's own State — and [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]]'s list is dictum in a case that upheld a residency requirement.
- **Missing the fact that flips the result.** Rewrite Proposition 12 to bar the in-state sale of pork raised **in Iowa** and it is facial discrimination by origin, failing at the core with none of this analysis. Or suppose California produced all its own pork and banned only imported crated pork: the practical effects would expose a protectionist purpose, and Pike would do its real job.
