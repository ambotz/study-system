## Rule statement

The grant of commerce power to Congress in Art. I, § 8, cl. 3 carries a negative implication that forbids state economic protectionism — "regulatory measures designed to benefit in-state economic interests by burdening out-of-state competitors" — and that antidiscrimination principle "lies at the 'very core'" of the doctrine ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]). Outside discrimination the clause does very little: there is no freestanding "almost per se" rule against state laws with the practical effect of controlling out-of-state conduct, so absent purposeful discrimination a state may condition the in-state sale of an ordinary consumer good on how it was produced elsewhere. Pike survives, but its threshold does the work — a plaintiff must plausibly plead a substantial burden on the interstate market, and harm to particular firms' preferred "structure[s] or methods of operation" is not one — and no opinion commands a majority on whether courts may balance burdens against noneconomic benefits at all. The contest Baude will press is that the negative implication has thin textual support: several Justices think the antidiscrimination principle "may be more appropriately housed elsewhere in the Constitution," and the Court itself now says that disputes about one state's reach are better resolved through "original and historical understandings of the Constitution's structure," "principles of 'sovereignty and comity,'" and the Due Process and Full Faith and Credit Clauses. The sibling instrument is Art. IV, § 2, which protects citizens rather than markets ([[Corfield v. Coryell]], and see [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]]).

## Elements

1. **Confirm the power is dormant.** Ask whether Congress has displaced the state law; in [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] Congress "has yet to adopt any statute that might displace Proposition 12," which is what leaves the negative implication as the only route.
2. **Look for facial discrimination by origin.** A law that bars or burdens goods because they come from another state operates "like 'a tariff or customs duty'" and fails at the core; the founding-era vice the clause answers is Rhode Island's tax on imported "New-England Rum" and Connecticut's duties on goods "brought into th[e] State . . . from any of the United States of America."
3. **Look for discriminatory purpose or discriminatory operation.** Practical effects are evidence: "the Pike line serves as an important reminder that a law's practical effects may also disclose the presence of a discriminatory purpose," and in the Pike line itself "the presence or absence of discrimination in practice proved decisive."
4. **Check for a price-control or price-affirmation statute.** Baldwin, Brown-Forman and Healy are reclassified as cases about laws tying "the price of . . . in-state products to out-of-state prices" and deliberately "prevent[ing out-of-state firms] from undertaking competitive pricing"; that species is still condemned, but it is an application of the antidiscrimination principle, not a separate extraterritoriality rule.
5. **If the law is genuinely nondiscriminatory, apply Pike's threshold first.** Plead facts plausibly showing a substantial burden on the interstate market; a "speculative possibility" is not enough, and the clause protects "the interstate market . . . from prohibitive or burdensome regulations," not "particular . . . firms." "That goes for pigs no less than gas stations."
6. **Only then reach the balance.** Nondiscriminatory regulation is valid "unless the burden imposed on [interstate] commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits" (Roberts, C.J., stating Pike as it has always been stated); three Justices say the balance cannot be struck where the benefits are noneconomic, because the task "is like being asked to decide 'whether a particular line is longer than a particular rock is heavy.'"
7. **Apply the caution rule to the whole analysis.** "'[E]xtreme caution' is warranted before a court deploys this implied authority," and displacing a democratically adopted state law under the clause is "a matter of 'extreme delicacy,'" done only "where the infraction is clear."
8. **Run the other clauses in parallel, because they may do the work the commerce clause will not.** Art. IV, § 2 for discrimination against out-of-state citizens in fundamental rights ([[Corfield v. Coryell]]); Due Process and Full Faith and Credit for genuinely extraterritorial regulation; and the "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause — that is, Congress — for nationwide disruption.

## Standard of review / burden

Discrimination claims and nondiscrimination claims are reviewed on entirely different footings, and the difference is the module. A law that discriminates by origin is at the core and the state bears the justification; a nondiscriminatory law is reviewed with "extreme caution" and invalidated only "where the infraction is clear," with the plaintiff bearing a pleading burden that [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] treats as substantial — everything in the case is decided on the pleadings, with the plaintiffs' allegations taken as true, and they still lost. Justice Sotomayor's separate opinion states the threshold cleanly: a substantial burden on interstate commerce is "a threshold requirement that plaintiffs must satisfy before courts need even engage in Pike's balancing and tailoring analyses." Beyond the threshold there is no controlling account of the standard at all, because the five-Justice judgment fractures: Parts I, II, III, IV-A and V are the opinion of the Court; Part IV-B (courts cannot weigh incommensurables) has three votes; Part IV-C (no substantial burden pleaded) has four; Barrett supplies the third vote for incommensurability and withholds the fifth for the pleading holding, while Sotomayor and Kagan do the reverse; and four Justices would have vacated and remanded. The safe exam formulation is that Pike is intact, its threshold is doing most of the work, and its balance has no majority behind it. Under the sibling clause the burden is stiffer for the state: the modern Art. IV, § 2 test asks whether the right is fundamental to the promotion of interstate harmony and, if so, requires the state to show a "substantial reason" for the discrimination and a "close relation" between the discrimination and that reason — a real standard of review, where the dormant Commerce Clause outside the discrimination core now supplies almost none.

## Exceptions

- **Congressional authorization or displacement.** The remedy the Court points petitioners to is a federal statute, because the "(wakeful)" Commerce Clause gives Congress both the power and the institutional competence.
- **Inspection, quarantine and health laws.** Marshall's list in *Gibbons v. Ogden* of reserved state powers "sure to have 'a remote and considerable influence on commerce' in other States" is the baseline; an "immense mass" of such laws has always been valid.
- **Bans on goods a state deems immoral or unsafe.** "[A] State may exclude from its territory, or prohibit the sale therein of any articles which, in its judgment, fairly exercised, are prejudicial to" its citizens' interests; petitioners themselves conceded that states may ban goods made with child labor and may address "imperfectly understood" health risks.
- **Harm to particular firms or their methods of operation.** Exxon's rule is generalized: the clause does not protect "particular structure[s] or methods of operation," so a complaint that today's producers must rebuild their barns is not a burden on the market.
- **Costs borne by in-state consumers.** Costs "ultimately borne by in-state consumers thanks to a law they adopted" are not a cognizable harm.
- **A state's common property.** [[Corfield v. Coryell]] treats an unappropriated oyster fishery as owned by the state's citizens as "tenants in common," so that outsiders cannot use it "without the tacit consent, or the express permission of the sovereign"; the modern law has narrowed this sharply, and a state's "ownership" of a fishery is no longer a trump.
- **Corporations under Art. IV, § 2.** The Privileges and Immunities Clause speaks of citizens and does not reach corporations, which is the doctrinal reason a trade association's claim must be brought under the Commerce Clause at all ([[Corfield v. Coryell]]).
- **Not decided: regulation of wholly out-of-state sales.** A law requiring that pork sold anywhere by a firm doing business in the state be compliant would raise the problem under Due Process and sovereignty principles rather than under an extraterritoriality rule, and the Court reserves the question.
- **Not decided: market-size leverage.** Gorsuch's answer to the argument that a large state can set national policy is the "fundamental principle of equal sovereignty among the States," but the point was pressed separately and no majority resolved it.
- **Not decided: where the antidiscrimination principle belongs.** Members of the Court have suggested the Import-Export Clause, the Privileges and Immunities Clause or preemption, and the Court "ha[s] no need to engage with any of them to resolve this case."

## Leading case

[[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]

- Holds, for the Court, that there is no "almost per se" rule against state laws with the "practical effect of controlling" out-of-state commerce, and that a Pike claim pleading no discrimination "falls well outside Pike's heartland." Judgment affirmed 5–4 on the pleadings.
- The fact that drove it is a concession: petitioners disavowed any discrimination claim and conceded "that Proposition 12 imposes the same burdens on in-state pork producers that it imposes on out-of-state ones." Everything else follows from that.
- The second driving fact is what the law regulates. Proposition 12 forbids the in-state **sale** of whole pork from breeding pigs "confined in a cruel manner," which lets the Court treat it as territorial regulation of a practice rather than of an origin.
- Reclassifies the extraterritoriality trilogy by looking at what each statute did: Baldwin's minimum-price law "plainly discriminate[d]" and "operated like 'a tariff or customs duty,'" and Brown-Forman and Healy "differed from Baldwin only in that they involved price-affirmation, rather than price-fixing, statutes." "[T]he language of an opinion is not always to be parsed as though we were dealing with language of a statute."
- Argues from consequences rather than text: "[i]n our interconnected national marketplace, many (maybe most) state laws have the 'practical effect of controlling' extraterritorial behavior," including income tax, environmental, libel, securities, franchise and tort laws. "Can anyone really suppose Baldwin, Brown-Forman, and Healy meant to do so much?"
- Turns the complaint's own concessions against it in Part IV-C: 28% of the industry had already converted to group housing, and complying producers could pass along at least some costs, so substantial harm to interstate commerce "remains nothing more than a speculative possibility."
- The horsemeat hypothetical is the argument to have ready against a burden-only test: many states ban the sale of horsemeat, and on the dissent's rule "all it would take is one complaint from an unhappy out-of-state producer and — presto — the Constitution would protect the sale of horsemeat," and likewise fireworks and single-use plastic bags.
- The sentence worth quoting: "While the Constitution addresses many weighty issues, the type of pork chops California merchants may sell is not on that list." And on balancing: "Your guess is as good as ours. . . . More accurately, your guess is better than ours."

## Best counter-case

[[Corfield v. Coryell]]

- The other instrument for policing a state that favours its own, and it cuts the other way in two directions at once — its test is stricter than the "extreme caution" of [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]], and its subject matter is narrower.
- Holds that Art. IV, § 2 secures only privileges and immunities "which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments," and that it is a rule of equal treatment in those rights rather than a transfer of one state's citizenship to visitors.
- The distinguishing fact is what New Jersey reserved: an unappropriated oyster fishery, which Washington treats as property the citizens hold as "tenants in common," so that reading the clause otherwise "would be going quite too far" and amount to "a grant of a cotenancy in the common property of the state, to the citizens of all the other states."
- Argue the Article IV side against a Proposition 12-style law: the catalogue includes "[t]he right of a citizen of one state to pass through, or to reside in any other state, for purposes of trade," and "an exemption from higher taxes or impositions than are paid by the other citizens of the state," and the modern test makes the state show a substantial reason closely related to the discrimination — a real burden the state must carry, unlike the Pike threshold.
- Argue the commerce side against an Article IV claim, and be precise about why these petitioners would have lost under it as well: they are corporations and associations rather than citizens, there is no discrimination between citizens to complain of, and Proposition 12 restricts a sale rather than denying anyone a fundamental privilege.
- Note the historical symmetry: [[Corfield v. Coryell]] itself carried a dormant commerce claim, which Justice Washington rejected in a single unreported paragraph citing *Gibbons v. Ogden*, so the two doctrines have been travelling together since 1823 and the Syllabus in [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] cites both provisions together.
- Know what is holding and what is dictum. The famous catalogue is dictum, offered in answer to a question the court did not have to reach; the operative rule is the narrow common-property exception, and Washington lists nothing resembling oyster-raking. The Fourteenth Amendment's drafters nonetheless borrowed that dictum from a case that upheld a discriminatory state law.
- The methodological point for a Baude exam: Washington reasons from natural law and the law of nations (Grotius, Rutherforth) and from consequences, barely from text; Gorsuch reasons from judicial competence and consequences, not from text. Neither doctrine has a strong textual anchor, which is exactly why both keep retreating to caution.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Arguing extraterritoriality as a freestanding theory.** After [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] there is no "almost per se" rule under this clause; the interests it protected were redistributed to the antidiscrimination principle, to Due Process and Full Faith and Credit, and to structural "sovereignty and comity." Naming that redistribution is the transferable move.
- **Saying the Court killed Pike.** It did not. Pike survives, the Court preserves the "courtroom door open" language and concedes that "a small number of our cases have invalidated state laws . . . that appear to have been genuinely nondiscriminatory," and four Justices would have let this very claim proceed.
- **Citing Part IV as the opinion of the Court.** Only Part IV-A is. Part IV-B (incommensurability) is three votes, Part IV-C (pleading) is four, and Part IV-D is three. Attributing the incommensurability holding to the Court is the cleanest way to be wrong in a cold call.
- **Confusing burden on producers with burden on the market.** The complaint alleged that most compliance costs "will be initially borne by out-of-state firms," and that was accepted as pleaded and held insufficient. What would have worked is an allegation that no producer could profitably comply, so supply would fail and the national market would contract.
- **Confusing the two clauses.** Art. IV, § 2 protects citizens in fundamental rights and does not reach corporations; the dormant Commerce Clause protects the interstate market and does. And the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause is a third thing again, borrowing Washington's phrase for the different job of securing rights against a citizen's own state.
- **Reading [[Corfield v. Coryell]] for its list.** The list is dictum and the holding is the common-property exception; the modern answer to the same facts would not use state "ownership" as a trump, and the case's actual result was to uphold a residency requirement.
- **Assuming a state's size changes its constitutional powers.** If it did, "voters in States with smaller markets are constitutionally entitled to greater authority to regulate in-state sales than voters in States with larger markets. So much for the Constitution's 'fundamental principle of equal sovereignty among the States.'"
- **Missing the fact pattern that flips the result.** Rewrite Proposition 12 to bar the in-state sale of pork raised **in Iowa** and it is facial discrimination by origin and fails at the core with none of this analysis. Or suppose California produced all its own pork and banned imported crated pork: the practical effects would expose a protectionist purpose and Pike would do its real job.
