## Rule statement

Congress may choose any reasonable means of carrying out its enumerated powers, but a means must serve those powers rather than amount to a new power of its own. The canonical test is: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]). The degree of necessity belongs to Congress; the Court reserves only pretext and the requirement that a means be "proper," which excludes any "great substantive and independent power" and any means at odds with the Constitution's structure ([[Printz v. United States]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]). The contest is the 1791 one in [[The Bank Debate]]: McCulloch adopts Hamilton's "needful, requisite, incidental, useful, or conducive to" over Jefferson's "indispensable," but keeps Madison's limit on size.

## Elements

1. **Is the end within an enumerated power?**
   - Marshall never tied the Bank to one clause; he listed taxing, borrowing, commerce, war and armies together and called the Bank "a convenient, a useful, and essential instrument in the prosecution of its fiscal operations." The critique worth making is that a means serving many powers at once begins to look like a freestanding power.
   - Outside: an end no enumerated power covers. Hamilton's own example is a federal corporation to police Philadelphia, invalid on every 1791 view ([[The Bank Debate]]); the design refers "the great and aggregate interests" to the nation and "the local and particular" to the States ([[The Federalist No. 10]]).
   - Outside: coercing a seceding State, which Buchanan argued was neither enumerated nor "necessary and proper" to any enumerated power ([[Buchanan, Address to Congress]]); see [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].
2. **Is the thing claimed a means or an end?** Incorporation "is never the end for which other powers are exercised, but a means by which other objects are accomplished" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]); Madison's contrary view in [[The Bank Debate]] was that a charter is "a distinct, an independent and substantive prerogative."
3. **Is the means appropriate and plainly adapted to the end?** Hamilton asked whether the means has "a natural relation" to a lawful end; Thomas in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] would require "an 'obvious, simple, and direct relation'" and would test the fit as applied.
4. **Is the means prohibited?** Art. I, § 9, the Bill of Rights and any other express prohibition defeat the law regardless of fit.
5. **Does the means "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution," that is, is it "proper"?**
   - It must not be a "great substantive and independent power" of the kind one would expect to be enumerated — Madison's size principle, revived in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]].
   - It must not violate state sovereignty: a law commandeering state executives is not a "La[w] . . . proper for carrying into Execution the Commerce Clause" ([[Printz v. United States]]). O'Connor's [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority|Garcia]] dissent and Scalia's [[Gonzales v. Raich|Raich]] concurrence locate the same limit in McCulloch's "letter and spirit" phrase.
   - It must execute a granted power rather than manufacture its predicate: a law giving Congress "the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not proper even if necessary ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]).
6. **Does the objection really go to the degree of necessity?** If so, it fails in court: inquiring "into the degree of its necessity" "would be to pass the line which circumscribes the judicial department, and to tread on legislative ground." The reserved check is a law passed "under the pretext of executing its powers" for "objects not entrusted to the government" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
7. **Does the Tenth Amendment add a separate limit?** No. It omits the Articles' word "expressly" (McCulloch) and "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered" ([[United States v. Darby]]); any real limit runs through the enumeration or through "proper." See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Burden:** the challenger bears it, and it is heavy.
- **Standard — "necessary":** once the end is legitimate and the means "really calculated" to effect it, courts do not weigh necessity ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]). Roberts in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] calls the Court "very deferential" on necessity, citing [[United States v. Comstock]], and [[Gonzales v. Raich]] asks only whether "a 'rational basis' exists."
- **Standard — "proper":** there is no tier of scrutiny. [[Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] apply "proper" categorically, and Ginsburg's objection is that Roberts's test tells lower courts: "You will know it when you see it."
- **Not reviewed / political question — motive:** the pretext inquiry Marshall reserved is formally available but has been dormant since [[United States v. Darby]] held that motive and purpose "are matters for the legislative judgment upon the exercise of which the Constitution places no restriction, and over which the courts are given no control."
- **What fills the gap — the political branches:** Jackson read Marshall's deference as making the degree of necessity "a question exclusively for legislative consideration," so the President, acting legislatively when he vetoes, judges it himself ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).
- **What fills the gap — the States' representation:** [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] holds that the States' protection "is one of process rather than one of result," borrowing the political-question factors (no manageable standards, commitment to the political branches) without the jurisdictional consequence.
- **What fills the gap — practice:** [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] treats settled practice as "a construction put on the Constitution by the Nation," and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] shows that such a settlement can be undone: the Bank won in court in 1819 and died politically in 1832–36.

## Exceptions

- **Great substantive and independent powers.** Powers too important to be incidental must be enumerated; the individual mandate failed on this ground in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]], which reads [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s own language as a limit.
- **Laws that are necessary but not proper.** Commandeering state legislatures or executives ([[Printz v. United States]]) and creating the predicate for federal regulation ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]) are outside the Clause however well adapted.
- **Express prohibitions.** A means "prohibited" by the Constitution fails at step 4 whatever its fit.
- **Pretext.** A law using an enumerated power as cover for objects not entrusted to the national government is void ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]); [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] is the high-water mark of that inquiry for the commerce power, and [[United States v. Darby]] abandoned it.
- **The mirror-image limit on the States.** "[T]he States have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress"; nondiscriminatory state taxes on a federal instrumentality's real property, and on residents' shares in it, survive ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
- **The conditional-spending workaround.** What Congress may not command it may buy, subject to germaneness and coercion ([[South Dakota v. Dole]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]); [[Printz v. United States]] expressly reserves funding conditions and pure information-reporting requirements. Dole's germaneness test is a Necessary and Proper question in different clothes, and the majority's shared-purpose version is looser than McCulloch's. See [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
- **Not decided: whether the Clause enlarges or merely declares.** Marshall says only that "if it does not enlarge, it cannot be construed to restrain the powers of Congress," and writing as "A Friend of the Constitution" he agreed the Clause adds nothing beyond background incidental powers; Madison in 1791 called it "merely declaratory."
- **Not decided: the source of the spending power.** If spending is an incident of the other enumerated powers rather than a free-standing grant, conditional-spending doctrine collapses into this Clause — the result Madison's reading in [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]] implies, against Hamilton's free-standing power in [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]].
- **Not decided: whether the third commerce category is really this Clause.** Stone's production holding in [[United States v. Darby]] is McCulloch's test almost word for word, and Scalia in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] relocates "substantially affects" to Art. I, § 8, cl. 18, which imports the "proper" limit into every commerce case; the Court has never adopted or rejected the move. See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].

## Leading case

[[McCulloch v. Maryland]]

- **Holding — implied powers:** Congress may charter a bank (unanimous, Marshall, C.J.); the Clause is "placed among the powers of Congress, not among the limitations on those powers," and "[i]ts terms purport to enlarge, not to diminish."
- **Premise:** the Constitution "derives its whole authority" from the people, and the government, "though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action." On [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell]]'s account, that nationalist premise, set against Maryland's compact theory, is what drives liberal rather than strict construction.
- **Driving textual fact:** Art. I, § 10 says "absolutely necessary" when it means that, so the unmodified "necessary" in cl. 18 cannot carry Jefferson's reading; adding "proper," "the only possible effect of which is to qualify that strict and rigorous meaning," confirms that some choice of means was intended.
- **Driving structural fact:** a constitution "intended to endure for ages to come" cannot "partake of the prolixity of a legal code"; a strict reading would forbid oaths of office beyond the constitutional oath, most of the federal criminal code and the punishment of mail robbery.
- **Holding — the tax:** Maryland's tax is void because "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and the plea that "all inconsistencies are to be reconciled by the magic of the word Confidence" fails: Congress taxing state banks is "the action of the whole on a part," Maryland taxing the Bank "the action of a part on the whole."
- **Practice:** the Bank's history gets "a considerable impression" on "a doubtful question," but "a bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long acquiesced in, and Marshall says the result would be the same "were the question entirely new."
- **Quote:** "[W]e must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding" is an argument about the kind of document, not a licence to update, and it is paired with the "endure for ages to come" passage.

## Best counter-case

[[NFIB v. Sebelius]]

- **Distinguishing line:** a means that operates on persons or activity already within a federal power is incidental, while a means that draws people into federal regulation to create the predicate for that power is not "proper."
- **Holding:** Roberts, C.J., for himself, holds that even if the individual mandate is "necessary" to the Act's guaranteed-issue and community-rating reforms, "such an expansion of federal power is not a 'proper' means"; the four joint dissenters agree in their own opinion, so five Justices reach the result without an opinion of the Court.
- **Driving fact:** who supplied the predicate. Civil commitment in [[United States v. Comstock]] operated on people already in federal custody, and [[Gonzales v. Raich]] concerned "individual applications of a concededly valid statutory scheme"; the mandate instead let Congress "draw within its regulatory scope those who otherwise would be outside of it."
- **Argue for the government:** Ginsburg reads "proper" as [[Printz v. United States]] read it, a rule against commandeering *States*, and the mandate "acts directly upon individuals"; without it the reforms would produce "an adverse-selection death-spiral," so the mandate is an "essential par[t] of a larger regulation of economic activity" under Scalia's own Raich concurrence.
- **Argue for the challengers — size:** run [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s own limit, that a purchase mandate is a "great substantive and independent power," and add that "the most telling indication of [a] severe constitutional problem . . . is the lack of historical precedent" (Roberts, borrowing from [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]]). The casebook cites Baude, *Rethinking the Federal Eminent Domain Power* (2013), for this reading of the Clause.
- **Argue for the challengers — structure:** the Clause "cannot be read to justify most of what the national government does, or there would be no point in enumerating its powers," the one structural argument in the Bank review note that pushes toward limits ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- **Pressure point:** Congress's own insurance reforms created the adverse-selection problem the mandate fixed, so a rule against manufacturing the predicate either bites hard or proves too much. Ginsburg lists criminal law, civil imprisonment and a national bank as implied powers; ask why those are less "great" than a purchase mandate, and note Harrison's rejoinder that "filling in the substance is famously difficult."
- **Founding-era counterpart:** Madison's chain-of-implications objection in [[The Bank Debate]] ("If implications, thus remote and thus multiplied, can be linked together, a chain may be formed that will reach every object of legislation") is the argument NFIB revives, and Jefferson's warning against taking "possession of a boundless f[ie]ld of power" is its strongest form.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"McCulloch means deference, full stop."** The opinion also contains the two limits that decide modern cases — "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution" and the pretext reservation — and [[Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] both quarry [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] for a limit rather than for deference.
- **"'Necessary and proper' is a single idea."** Marshall read "proper" as softening "necessary," but the cases that strike laws down give "proper" independent force; an answer that never separates the two words cannot reach the holdings of Printz or NFIB.
- **"The Tenth Amendment is the limit."** It "states but a truism" ([[United States v. Darby]]), and [[Printz v. United States]] says that "[w]hat destroys the dissent's Necessary and Proper Clause argument . . . is not the Tenth Amendment but the Necessary and Proper Clause itself."
- **"McCulloch settled the Bank question."** It settled that Congress *may* charter a bank, not that it would: [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] killed the Second Bank on constitutional grounds, Tyler vetoed two more charters, and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] records that nothing like it existed until 1913.
- **"Madison changed his mind about the Bank."** "My construction of the Constitution on this point is not changed" ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]); what changed was the weight of settled practice, and liquidation on his terms fixes only text that is genuinely doubtful.
- **"The Lopez and Wickard tests decide whether a means is proper."** The economic/noneconomic line of [[United States v. Lopez]] and the aggregation principle of [[Wickard v. Filburn]] govern the commerce power's third category; they say nothing about whether a means is "proper," and [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]]'s production/commerce line was overruled by [[United States v. Darby]].
