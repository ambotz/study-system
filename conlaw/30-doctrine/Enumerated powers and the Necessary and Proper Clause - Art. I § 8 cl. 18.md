## Rule statement

The federal government is one of enumerated powers, but the enumeration carries with it the means of execution: under Art. I § 8 cl. 18, "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]). "Necessary" does not mean indispensable; how necessary a means is belongs to Congress, and the judicial check Marshall reserved is pretext, not degree. The Clause carries incidental means only: a "great substantive and independent power" must be granted expressly, and "proper" bars a means that is inconsistent with the Constitution's structure even when it is useful. The contest is the 1791 one in [[The Bank Debate]] — Jefferson's "necessary" as indispensable, Madison's incidental-and-not-great, Hamilton's "needful, requisite, incidental, useful, or conducive to" — and McCulloch resolves it by adopting Hamilton's definition of "necessary" while keeping Madison's limit on size.

## Elements

1. **Identify a legitimate end within an enumerated power.** The end must be one the Constitution entrusts to the national government; Marshall never tied the Bank to a single clause, resting it on taxing, borrowing, commerce, war and the support of armies together, as "an instrument of fiscal operations."
2. **Ask whether the thing claimed is a means or an end.** Incorporation "is never the end for which other powers are exercised, but a means by which other objects are accomplished" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]); Madison's contrary view in [[The Bank Debate]] was that a charter is "a distinct, an independent and substantive prerogative."
3. **Test the fit between means and end.** The means must be "appropriate" and "plainly adapted," or in Hamilton's phrase have "a natural relation" to the end; Thomas in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] would require instead "an 'obvious, simple, and direct relation'" and would test it as applied.
4. **Confirm the means is not prohibited.** Art. I § 9, the Bill of Rights and any other express prohibition defeat the law regardless of fit.
5. **Ask whether the means is "proper," that is, whether it "consist[s] with the letter and spirit of the constitution."** Three sub-tests have emerged.
   - It must not be a "great substantive and independent power" of the kind one would expect to be enumerated — Madison's size principle, revived in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]].
   - It must not violate state sovereignty: a law commandeering state executives is not a law "proper for carrying into Execution the Commerce Clause" ([[Printz v. United States]]).
   - It must execute a granted power rather than manufacture its predicate: a law giving Congress "the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not proper even if necessary ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]).
6. **Stop short of weighing degree.** Inquiring "into the degree of its necessity" "would be to pass the line which circumscribes the judicial department, and to tread on legislative ground"; the reserved check is a law passed "under the pretext of executing its powers ... for the accomplishment of objects not entrusted to the government" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
7. **Do not add the Tenth Amendment as a separate step.** It omits the Articles' word "expressly" and "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered" ([[United States v. Darby]]); any real limit runs through the enumeration or through "proper." See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].

## Standard of review / burden

The challenger bears the burden, and it is heavy: once the end is legitimate and the means "really calculated" to effect it, the Court does not weigh necessity, and [[Gonzales v. Raich]] asks only whether "a 'rational basis' exists" for Congress's judgment. There is no tier of scrutiny for "proper": [[Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] apply it categorically rather than by balancing, and the joint dissenters' complaint in NFIB — that Roberts's test tells lower courts "You will know it when you see it" — is the standing objection. The pretext inquiry Marshall reserved is formally available but has been dormant since [[United States v. Darby]] held that motive and purpose "are matters for the legislative judgment ... over which the courts are given no control." Where no judicial standard is available, the gap is filled by politics rather than by the political-question doctrine: [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] holds that the States' protection "is one of process rather than one of result," borrowing the political-question factors (no manageable standards, commitment to the political branches) without the jurisdictional consequence, and [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] makes settled practice the operative answer where courts supply none.

## Exceptions

- **Great substantive and independent powers.** Powers too important to be incidental must be enumerated; the individual mandate failed on this ground in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]], which reads [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s own language as a limit.
- **Laws that are necessary but not proper.** Commandeering state legislatures or executives ([[Printz v. United States]]) and creating the predicate for federal regulation ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]) are outside the Clause however well adapted.
- **Express prohibitions.** A means "prohibited" by the Constitution fails at step 4 whatever its fit.
- **Pretext.** A law using an enumerated power as a cover for objects not entrusted to the national government is void ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]); [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] is the high-water mark of that inquiry for the commerce power, and [[United States v. Darby]] abandoned it.
- **The mirror-image limit on the States.** "[T]he States have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress"; but nondiscriminatory state taxes on a federal instrumentality's real property, and on residents' shares in it, survive ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
- **The conditional-spending workaround.** What Congress may not command it may buy, subject to germaneness and coercion ([[South Dakota v. Dole]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]); [[Printz v. United States]] expressly reserves funding conditions and pure information-reporting requirements. See [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
- **Not decided: whether the Clause enlarges or merely declares.** Marshall says only that "if it does not enlarge, it cannot be construed to restrain," and as "A Friend of the Constitution" he agreed the Clause adds nothing to background incidental powers.
- **Not decided: the source of the spending power.** If spending is an incident of the other enumerated powers rather than a free-standing grant, conditional-spending doctrine collapses into this Clause — Madison's position in [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]], against Hamilton's in [[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]].
- **Not decided: whether the third commerce category is really this Clause.** Scalia in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] relocates "substantially affects" to Art. I § 8 cl. 18, which imports the "proper" limit into every commerce case; the Court has never adopted or rejected the move. See [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]].

## Leading case

[[McCulloch v. Maryland]]

- Holds that Congress may charter a bank: the Constitution "derives its whole authority" from the people, the government is "supreme within its sphere of action," and the Clause is "placed among the powers of Congress, not among the limitations on those powers" and "purport[s] to enlarge, not to diminish."
- The driving textual fact is comparative: Art. I § 10 says "absolutely necessary" when it means that, so the unmodified "necessary" in cl. 18 cannot carry Jefferson's reading, and adding "proper" — "the only possible effect of which is to qualify that strict and rigorous meaning" — confirms that some choice of means was intended.
- The driving structural fact is that a constitution "intended to endure for ages to come" cannot "partake of the prolixity of a legal code"; a strict reading would forbid oaths of office beyond the constitutional oath, most of the federal criminal code and the punishment of mail robbery.
- The second holding voids Maryland's tax: "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and "all inconsistencies are to be reconciled by the magic of the word Confidence" will not do, because Congress taxing state banks is "the action of the whole on a part" while Maryland taxing the Bank is "the action of a part on the whole."
- Practice gets "a considerable impression" on "a doubtful question" but no more: "a bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long acquiesced in, and Marshall disclaims reliance by saying the result would be the same "were the question entirely new."
- The sentence worth quoting: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."
- The line to have ready against it: "we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding" is an argument about the kind of document, not a licence to update, and it is paired with the "endure for ages" passage.

## Best counter-case

[[NFIB v. Sebelius]]

- Roberts holds that even if the individual mandate is "necessary" to the Act's guaranteed-issue and community-rating reforms, "such an expansion of federal power is not a 'proper' means," because it "vests Congress with the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power."
- The distinguishing fact is who supplied the predicate. Civil commitment operated on people already in federal custody and [[Gonzales v. Raich]] concerned "individual applications of a concededly valid statutory scheme"; the mandate instead lets Congress "draw within its regulatory scope those who otherwise would be outside of it."
- Arguing the government's side: Ginsburg reads "proper" as [[Printz v. United States]] read it, a rule against commandeering *States*, and the mandate "acts directly upon individuals"; the reforms would produce an adverse-selection death spiral without it, so the mandate is an "essential par[t] of a larger regulation of economic activity" and "reasonably adapted" under Scalia's own [[Gonzales v. Raich]] test.
- Arguing the challengers' side: run [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s own size limit — a purchase mandate is a "great substantive and independent power," not one "incidental to the [enumerated] power, and conducive to its beneficial exercise" — and add that "the most telling indication of [a] severe constitutional problem . . . is the lack of historical precedent."
- The pressure point either way: Congress's own insurance reforms created the adverse-selection problem the mandate fixed, so a rule against manufacturing the predicate either bites hard or proves too much. Conscription, legal tender, eminent domain and the federal criminal code have long rested on implied power; ask why those are less "great" than a purchase mandate.
- Keep [[The Bank Debate]] as the founding-era counter-case for the same proposition: Madison's chain-of-implications objection ("If implications, thus remote and thus multiplied, can be linked together, a chain may be formed that will reach every object of legislation") is the argument NFIB revives, and Jefferson's "To take a single step beyond the boundaries thus specially drawn around the powers of Congress, is to take possession of a boundless f[ie]ld of power" is its strongest form.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Citing [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] for deference and stopping there. The opinion contains the two limits that decide modern cases — "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution" and the pretext reservation — and [[Printz v. United States]] and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] both quarry it for a limit rather than for deference.
- Treating "necessary and proper" as a single idea. Marshall read "proper" as softening "necessary," but the cases that strike laws down give "proper" independent force; an answer that never separates the two cannot reach the holdings of Printz or NFIB.
- Using the Tenth Amendment as the limit. It "states but a truism" ([[United States v. Darby]]), and [[Printz v. United States]] says explicitly that "[w]hat destroys the dissent's Necessary and Proper Clause argument . . . is not the Tenth Amendment but the Necessary and Proper Clause itself."
- Saying that [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] settled the Bank question. It settled that Congress *may* charter a bank and nothing about whether it would: [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] killed the Second Bank on the oath argument, Tyler vetoed two more charters, and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] records that nothing resembling it existed until 1913.
- Reading [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] as a change of mind. "My construction of the Constitution on this point is not changed"; what changed was the weight of settled practice, and liquidation on his own terms fixes only text that is genuinely doubtful, so it cannot cure a clear usurpation.
- Demanding that the statute be tied to one enumerated power. Marshall lists several at once and never chooses; the criticism worth making is the opposite one, that a means serving many powers begins to look like a freestanding power.
- Importing commerce-doctrine categories into the Clause. The economic/noneconomic line of [[United States v. Lopez]] and the aggregation principle of [[Wickard v. Filburn]] limit the commerce power's third category; they say nothing about whether a means is "proper," and [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]]'s production/commerce line was overruled by [[United States v. Darby]].
- Forgetting that the Clause runs both ways on the States. [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] refuses to police "traditional governmental functions," but O'Connor's dissent there, Scalia's [[Gonzales v. Raich]] concurrence and [[Printz v. United States]] all locate the state-sovereignty limit in the same McCulloch sentence.
