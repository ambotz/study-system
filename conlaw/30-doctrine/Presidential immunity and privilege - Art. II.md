## Rule statement

The Constitution gives the President no express immunity or privilege, but the Court infers both from the structure of Article II. Presidential communications are "presumptively privileged," but "[t]he generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial" ([[United States v. Nixon]]). A former President has "absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for actions within his conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority," "at least presumptive immunity from prosecution for all his official acts," and "no immunity for unofficial acts" ([[Trump v. United States]]). The contest is over method and burden. Nixon balances and leaves the last word to the courts; Trump turns Jackson's "conclusive and preclusive" category in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] into a zone the criminal law cannot reach, over a dissent that finds no textual immunity and reads the Impeachment Judgment Clause to assume liability.

## Elements

1. **Is the demand for evidence, or for the President's own liability?**
   - A subpoena for evidence in someone else's criminal trial runs through [[United States v. Nixon]].
   - A prosecution of the President himself runs through [[Trump v. United States]].
   - The burdens run in opposite directions, so the classification often decides the case.
2. **For a subpoena, are the threshold requirements met?**
   - Appealability: the order must be reviewable; in Nixon it was, and the Court took the case on certiorari before judgment.
   - Justiciability: "[t]he mere assertion of a claim of an 'intra-branch dispute,' without more, has never operated to defeat federal jurisdiction," and an Executive regulation binds the Executive "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force."
   - Rule 17(c): the subpoena must clear three "hurdles": "(1) relevancy; (2) admissibility; (3) specificity."
3. **For a subpoena, does the demonstrated need outweigh the privilege?**
   - Weigh "the importance of the general privilege of confidentiality of Presidential communications in performance of the President's responsibilities against the inroads of such a privilege on the fair administration of criminal justice."
   - Ask whether the claim is generalized or asserts "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets"; the second kind draws far more deference.
   - Protect whatever survives the balance by in camera inspection, not by refusal to produce.
4. **For a prosecution, which tier does the charged act fall in?**
   - Conclusive and preclusive (absolute immunity): the decision whom to investigate and prosecute, removal of executive officers, and the pardon. "Trump's threatened removal of the Acting Attorney General likewise implicates 'conclusive and preclusive' Presidential authority."
   - Official but not exclusive (presumptive immunity): acts within the "outer perimeter" of official responsibility that touch a power the Constitution gives someone else, such as pressing the Vice President, who acts as President of the Senate in counting electoral votes.
   - Unofficial (no immunity): "not everything the President does is official." Classification "may depend on the content and context of each," and the district court makes it first.
5. **Was the classification made without the two forbidden shortcuts?**
   - "[C]ourts may not inquire into the President's motives."
   - "Nor may courts deem an action unofficial merely because it allegedly violates a generally applicable law."
6. **Is evidence of immune conduct kept from the jury?**
   - Part III-C (five Justices): evidence of immune official conduct is inadmissible, even to prove intent in a prosecution for unofficial acts, because a contrary rule "threatens to eviscerate the immunity we have recognized."
   - Barrett's dissent from Part III-C (Barrett, J.): "[t]he Constitution does not require blinding juries to the circumstances surrounding conduct for which Presidents can be held liable."
7. **What check remains for immune conduct?**
   - Impeachment, which is available but is not a precondition to prosecution; see [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].
   - The dissent reads the Impeachment Judgment Clause as presupposing criminal liability for official acts "such as 'Bribery,' Art. II, §4, which implicates official acts almost by definition."

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption (privilege):** presidential communications are "presumptively privileged," and the confidentiality interest is "weighty indeed and entitled to great respect."
- **Who decides (privilege):** the courts, on [[Marbury v. Madison]]'s "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is"; the judicial power "can no more be shared with the Executive Branch" than the President can share the veto with the courts.
- **Burden (privilege):** the party seeking the evidence must satisfy Rule 17(c) and show a demonstrated, specific need; a generalized claim then yields, with in camera review as the safeguard.
- **Presumption (immunity):** for official acts outside the core, the presumption favours the President, and "[i]t is ultimately the Government's burden to rebut the presumption of immunity."
- **Standard (immunity):** the Government rebuts the presumption only by showing that "applying a criminal prohibition to that act would pose no 'dangers of intrusion on the authority and functions of the Executive Branch.'"
- **Not reviewed:** "Congress cannot act on, and courts cannot examine, the President's actions on subjects within his 'conclusive and preclusive' constitutional authority."
- **Timing:** immunity is decided before trial and the ruling may be appealed before trial, so the immunity protects against the prosecution itself and not only against conviction.
- **What fills the gap:** impeachment, which [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] shows is hard to use (35–19, one vote short). Bates's claim that the President answers "before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal" is the nineteenth-century ancestor of the absolute tier ([[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]]).

## Exceptions

- **National-security secrets:** a claim to protect "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets" draws far more deference; [[United States v. Nixon]] does not say that such a claim would be absolute, and no such claim was made there.
- **Settings Nixon reserved:** the balance against "the need for relevant evidence in civil litigation," against "congressional demands for information," and the President's interest in state secrets. *Clinton v. Jones* later let a civil suit for unofficial conduct proceed, and *Trump v. Mazars* set a separate test for congressional subpoenas.
- **Revocable self-binding:** an Executive regulation protecting a prosecutor binds the Executive only while it stands; the Attorney General could revoke it and direct the subpoena dropped, and in October 1973 the first Special Prosecutor was fired and his office abolished when he refused to drop his pursuit of the tapes. After [[Trump v. Slaughter]] and [[Trump v. United States]] treat removal and prosecution as core powers, how far such a regulation can bind the President himself is a live question.
- **No impeachment precondition:** "By its own terms, the Clause does not address whether and on what conduct a President may be prosecuted if he was never impeached and convicted."
- **Unofficial conduct:** no immunity at all. On Barrett's view (Barrett, J.), "[a] President has no legal authority—and thus no official capacity—to influence how the States appoint their electors."
- **Not decided:** whether a sitting President may be indicted. [[United States v. Nixon]] assumed a criminal process in which the President was an unindicted coconspirator, and [[Trump v. United States]] concerned a former President.
- **Not decided:** how the electors, state-official and public-statement allegations come out. The Court remanded that "necessarily factbound analysis," and Barrett and the majority do not agree about the electors.
- **Not decided:** how a bribery prosecution survives the evidence rule when the official act is the thing sold. Barrett's dissent from Part III-C raises the problem, and the Impeachment Judgment Clause's "nevertheless" liable language is the dissent's text for it.

## Leading case

[[Trump v. United States]]

- **Holding:** three tiers — absolute immunity for conduct within conclusive and preclusive authority, "at least presumptive immunity" for other official acts, and none for unofficial acts (6–3; Roberts, C.J.; Barrett, J., joining except Part III-C).
- **Driving fact (absolute tier):** the charged conduct included discussions with Justice Department officials and a threatened replacement of the Acting Attorney General; investigation, prosecution and removal are core executive powers, so "Trump is absolutely immune from prosecution for the alleged conduct involving his discussions with Justice Department officials."
- **Driving fact (presumptive tier):** the Vice President's certification role is legislative, so the President has no conclusive power over it; but President and Vice President routinely confer, so the conversations are official, and the Government bears the burden on remand.
- **Reasoning — structure:** the President's authority "stem[s] either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself" ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]), and constitutional authority is "sometimes 'conclusive and preclusive.'" The Framers "deemed an energetic executive essential" ([[The Federalist No. 70]]), and the fear is a President "chilled from taking the 'bold and unhesitating action' required of an independent Executive."
- **Reasoning — precedent:** *Nixon v. Fitzgerald* gave former Presidents absolute immunity from civil damages for official acts and rejected as "unpersuasive" the premise that immunity requires text; the Court reads *United States v. Burr* and [[United States v. Nixon]] as rejecting only an absolute privilege.
- **Separate opinions:** Sotomayor, J. (joined by Kagan and Jackson, JJ.): "In every use of official power, the President is now a king above the law" — "Takes a bribe in exchange for a pardon? Immune." Barrett, J., frames the question as an as-applied challenge to the statute rather than "immunity." Thomas, J., questions the Special Counsel's appointment; see [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].
- **Quote:** "The President is not above the law. But under our system of separated powers, the President may not be prosecuted for exercising his core constitutional powers, and he is entitled to at least presumptive immunity from prosecution for his official acts."
- **Removal feedback loop:** because removal is the leading example of the absolute tier, [[Trump v. Slaughter]] widens the zone of absolute criminal immunity by widening the removal power; see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

## Best counter-case

[[United States v. Nixon]]

- **Distinguishing line:** when the President is asked to produce evidence for someone else's criminal trial, a generalized confidentiality claim yields to demonstrated need and the courts decide; when the President himself is prosecuted for an official act, the Government must overcome the immunity, and for a core power it cannot.
- **Holding:** 8–0 (Burger, C.J.; Rehnquist, J., not participating): "Neither the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances."
- **Reasoning:** Nixon accepts the candour premise and rejects the inference — "we cannot conclude that advisers will be moved to temper the candor of their remarks by the infrequent occasions of disclosure." The competing function is Article III's: "The very integrity of the judicial system and public confidence in the system depend on full disclosure of all the facts," backed by the Fifth and Sixth Amendments' due process and compulsory process guarantees.
- **Argue for the President:** [[Trump v. United States]] reads Burr and Nixon as rejecting only an absolute privilege, not immunity for official acts; a prosecution burdens the office far more than producing tapes; and Nixon's own reservations show how little it decided.
- **Argue for the prosecution:** Nixon's method is Jackson's "separateness but interdependence, autonomy but reciprocity," and Trump inverts Nixon's burden. Nixon's judicial-power holding rests on [[Marbury v. Madison]], while Trump's absolute tier removes the question from the courts entirely.
- **Argue from original meaning:** the Constitution gives members of Congress Speech or Debate immunity and gives the President nothing comparable; the Framers made the President, unlike a king who could do no wrong, removable by impeachment and "nevertheless" liable to indictment. Trump answers from structure, energy and precedent, not text.
- **Verdict:** Nixon's presumptive-privilege balance and Trump's presumptive-immunity tier use the same vocabulary and run in opposite directions, and only the Trump tier is the operative rule for prosecuting a President.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Nixon holds that the President can be prosecuted."** [[United States v. Nixon]] decided a subpoena for evidence in seven other defendants' trial; the President was an unindicted coconspirator, and whether he could be prosecuted stayed open until 2024.
- **"Trump gives absolute immunity for all official acts."** That was Trump's argument, and the Court rejected it; absolute immunity attaches only to conclusive and preclusive powers, and other official acts get presumptive immunity with the burden on the Government.
- **"The act was unofficial because it was illegal or corruptly motivated."** Both moves are foreclosed: courts "may not inquire into the President's motives," and an alleged violation of a generally applicable law does not make an act unofficial.
- **"Immune conduct can still come in to prove intent."** Part III-C (five Justices) makes immune official conduct inadmissible even for that purpose, which is where many prosecutions will die; Barrett's dissent from Part III-C and the principal dissent's pardon-for-bribe hypothetical both press the bribery problem.
- **"The Impeachment Judgment Clause requires conviction before prosecution."** Trump argued it and lost: the Clause "does not address whether and on what conduct a President may be prosecuted if he was never impeached and convicted." The dissent uses the same Clause for the opposite point, that it presupposes liability for official acts.
- **"Nixon and Trump apply the same presumption."** The burdens sit on opposite parties. In Nixon the President's generalized claim must yield to a demonstrated, specific need; in Trump the Government must show no danger of intrusion, so the same facts can come out differently depending on which frame the question invites.
