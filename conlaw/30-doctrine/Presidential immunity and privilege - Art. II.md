## Rule statement

The President has no textual immunity or privilege, and both are inferred from the structure of Article II. On evidence, presidential communications are "presumptively privileged" and the privilege is "constitutionally based," but it is qualified and its scope is for the courts: "[t]he generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial" ([[United States v. Nixon]]). On liability, [[Trump v. United States]] sorts a former President's conduct into three tiers — "absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for actions within his conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority," "at least presumptive immunity from prosecution for all his official acts," and "no immunity for unofficial acts." The two cases pull in opposite directions and the exam lives in the gap: Nixon balances and gives the courts the last word, while Trump takes Jackson's [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]] category of exclusive power and converts "Congress cannot regulate this" into "Congress cannot punish this," putting the burden on the Government for everything else.

## Elements

1. **Classify the demand before anything else.** A subpoena for evidence to be used in someone else's criminal trial is [[United States v. Nixon]]; a prosecution of the President himself is [[Trump v. United States]]. The distinguishing fact is whose liability is at stake, and the burdens run opposite ways.
2. **For a subpoena, clear the threshold questions.** Is the order appealable; is the dispute justiciable ("[t]he mere assertion of a claim of an 'intra-branch dispute,' without more, has never operated to defeat federal jurisdiction," and a regulation binds the Executive "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force"); and does the subpoena satisfy Rule 17(c)'s three "hurdles": "(1) relevancy; (2) admissibility; (3) specificity."
3. **Then run the privilege balance.** Weigh "the importance of the general privilege of confidentiality of Presidential communications in performance of the President's responsibilities against the inroads of such a privilege on the fair administration of criminal justice," ask whether the claim is generalized or asserts "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets," and protect what survives by in camera inspection rather than by refusal.
4. **For a prosecution, classify the act.** Official or unofficial; if official, within a conclusive and preclusive power or merely within the "outer perimeter."
   - Conclusive and preclusive (absolute): the decision whom to investigate and prosecute, removal of executive officers, the pardon. "Trump's threatened removal of the Acting Attorney General likewise implicates 'conclusive and preclusive' Presidential authority."
   - Official but not exclusive (presumptive): conduct touching a power the Constitution gives someone else, such as pressing the Vice President over the certification of electoral votes, where the Vice President acts as President of the Senate.
   - Unofficial (no immunity): "not everything the President does is official"; classification "may depend on the content and context of each," and is for the district court in the first instance.
5. **Apply the two classification rules, which are prophylactic and do most of the work.** "[C]ourts may not inquire into the President's motives," and "[n]or may courts deem an action unofficial merely because it allegedly violates a generally applicable law."
6. **Apply the evidence rule.** Five Justices hold that a jury may not hear evidence of immune official conduct, because a contrary rule "threatens to eviscerate the immunity we have recognized"; Barrett dissents from this part — "[t]he Constitution does not require blinding juries to the circumstances surrounding conduct for which Presidents can be held liable."
7. **Name the remaining check.** Impeachment and, for a convicted party, the Impeachment Judgment Clause — which the Court holds is not a precondition to prosecution but which the dissent reads as presupposing criminal liability for official acts "such as 'Bribery' . . . which implicates official acts almost by definition." See [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].

## Standard of review / burden

Privilege: the presumption favours the President, but the courts decide its scope, and the Court rests that on [[Marbury v. Madison]] — "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is" — because the judicial power "can no more be shared with the Executive Branch" than the President can share the veto with the courts. The moving party must satisfy Rule 17(c) first; then the generalized confidentiality interest, "weighty indeed and entitled to great respect," yields to a demonstrated, specific need in a pending criminal trial, with in camera review as the protective device. Immunity: for core powers there is no review at all, because "Congress cannot act on, and courts cannot examine, the President's actions on subjects within his 'conclusive and preclusive' constitutional authority." For other official acts the presumption favours the President and "[i]t is ultimately the Government's burden to rebut the presumption of immunity," and it does so only by showing that "applying a criminal prohibition to that act would pose no 'dangers of intrusion on the authority and functions of the Executive Branch.'" For unofficial acts there is no immunity and the ordinary criminal law applies. Procedurally, the immunity question is decided before trial and the ruling is immediately appealable, which makes the immunity a protection against the process and not merely against the judgment. Where no court may review — the absolute tier — what fills the gap is impeachment, which [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] shows is very hard to use.

## Exceptions

- A claim to protect "military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets" draws far more deference; [[United States v. Nixon]] does not say it would be absolute, and no such claim was made there.
- Nixon expressly reserves three settings: the balance against "the need for relevant evidence in civil litigation," against "congressional demands for information," and the President's interest in state secrets. (*Clinton v. Jones* later let a civil suit for unofficial conduct proceed; *Trump v. Mazars* set a separate test for congressional subpoenas.)
- An Executive Branch regulation binds the Executive while it stands, but only while it stands — the Attorney General could have revoked it and directed the subpoena dropped, which is what happened to the first Special Prosecutor in October 1973.
- Impeachment and conviction are not a precondition to prosecution: "By its own terms, the Clause does not address whether and on what conduct a President may be prosecuted if he was never impeached and convicted."
- Unofficial conduct carries no immunity at all, and on Barrett's view "[a] President has no legal authority—and thus no official capacity—to influence how the States appoint their electors."
- Not decided: whether a sitting President may be indicted. [[United States v. Nixon]] assumed a criminal process in which the President was named an unindicted coconspirator and decided nothing about prosecuting him.
- Not decided: which way the electors, state-official and public-statement allegations come out. The Court remanded that "necessarily factbound analysis," and Barrett and the majority do not agree about it.
- Not decided: how a bribery prosecution survives the evidence rule when the official act is the thing sold. That is Barrett's objection, and the Impeachment Judgment Clause's "nevertheless . . . liable" language is the dissent's text for it.

## Leading case

[[Trump v. United States]]

- Holds three tiers: absolute immunity for conduct within conclusive and preclusive authority, "at least presumptive immunity" for other official acts, and none for unofficial acts.
- The fact that drove the absolute holding: some of the charged conduct was discussion with Justice Department officials and a threatened replacement of the Acting Attorney General, and investigation, prosecution and removal are core executive powers — so "Trump is absolutely immune from prosecution for the alleged conduct involving his discussions with Justice Department officials."
- The fact that drove the presumptive holding: the Vice President's certification role is legislative (President of the Senate), so the President has no conclusive power over it, but President and Vice President routinely confer, which makes the conversations official.
- Built on [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]: "the President's authority to act necessarily 'stem[s] either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself,'" and where it comes from the Constitution alone it "is sometimes 'conclusive and preclusive.'"
- Built on energy: the Framers "deemed an energetic executive essential" ([[The Federalist No. 70]]), and the fear is a President "chilled from taking the 'bold and unhesitating action' required of an independent Executive."
- The sentence worth quoting is the concluding one, because it concedes and holds in the same breath: "The President is not above the law. But under our system of separated powers, the President may not be prosecuted for exercising his core constitutional powers, and he is entitled to at least presumptive immunity from prosecution for his official acts."
- The dissent's sentence worth quoting back: "In every use of official power, the President is now a king above the law," with the hypotheticals — "Orders the Navy's Seal Team 6 to assassinate a political rival? Immune. . . . Takes a bribe in exchange for a pardon? Immune."
- Note the feedback loop with removal: because the absolute tier's leading example is removal, [[Trump v. Slaughter]] widens the zone of absolute criminal immunity by widening the removal power — see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

## Best counter-case

[[United States v. Nixon]]

- Cuts the other way on both of Trump's moves: it holds that the courts and not the President fix the scope of the protection, and that a structural interest in the Presidency yields to the criminal process rather than displacing it. "Neither the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances."
- The distinguishing facts are two: whose liability is at stake (evidence for seven other defendants' trial, not the President's own prosecution) and what was claimed (a generalized confidentiality interest, with no assertion of military, diplomatic or national-security secrets).
- Nixon accepts the President's premise and rejects the inference: the candour interest is "weighty indeed and entitled to great respect," but "we cannot conclude that advisers will be moved to temper the candor of their remarks by the infrequent occasions of disclosure."
- The competing constitutional function is Article III's, not Congress's: "The very integrity of the judicial system and public confidence in the system depend on full disclosure of all the facts," and the Fifth and Sixth Amendments guarantee due process and compulsory process.
- Arguing for the President: [[Trump v. United States]] reads Burr and Nixon as merely rejecting an *absolute* privilege, not as denying immunity for official acts; Nixon is about a subpoena, and a prosecution burdens the office far more than producing tapes; and its own reservations show it decided little.
- Arguing for the prosecution: Nixon's method is the method — Jackson's "separateness but interdependence, autonomy but reciprocity" — and Trump inverts its balance by putting the burden on the Government; and [[United States v. Nixon]]'s judicial-power holding is formalist ([[Marbury v. Madison]]) while Trump's absolute tier removes the question from courts entirely.
- The honest verdict to give: Nixon's presumptive-privilege balance and Trump's presumptive-immunity tier use the same vocabulary and run in opposite directions, and only the second one is the operative rule for prosecuting a President.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Treating [[United States v. Nixon]] as a holding that the President is not immune from criminal prosecution. It decided a subpoena for evidence in other defendants' trial; the President was an unindicted coconspirator, and the question of prosecuting him was left open until 2024.
- Reading [[Trump v. United States]] as giving absolute immunity for all official acts. That was Trump's argument and the Court rejected it; absolute immunity attaches only to conclusive and preclusive powers, and everything else is presumptive with the burden on the Government.
- Arguing that conduct is unofficial because it broke the law or because the motive was corrupt. Both moves are expressly foreclosed: courts "may not inquire into the President's motives," and an alleged violation of a generally applicable law does not make an act unofficial.
- Forgetting the evidence rule, which is where most prosecutions actually die. Immune official conduct is inadmissible even to prove intent in a prosecution for unofficial acts (five Justices); Barrett's separate opinion is the only place in the case where a bribery prosecution is taken seriously.
- Saying the Impeachment Judgment Clause requires conviction before prosecution. Trump argued it and lost; the Court held the Clause "does not address whether and on what conduct a President may be prosecuted if he was never impeached and convicted." The dissent uses the same Clause for the opposite point, that it presupposes liability for official acts.
- Missing that the two cases put the burden on opposite parties. In Nixon the President's generalized claim must yield to a demonstrated, specific need; in Trump the Government must show no danger of intrusion. The same facts can come out differently depending only on which frame the question invites.
