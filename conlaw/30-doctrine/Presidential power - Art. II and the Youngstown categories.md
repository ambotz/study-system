## Rule statement

The President needs authority from a statute or from the Constitution itself, and when he relies on the Constitution alone, the strength of his claim depends on what Congress has said. Under Justice Jackson's concurrence in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]], which supplies the operative framework, authorization puts him at his "maximum," silence leaves him in a "zone of twilight," and action against Congress's express or implied will puts him at his "lowest ebb." At the lowest ebb a court can sustain him only by holding the power "conclusive and preclusive," which disables Congress from acting on the subject. The contest is textual, because Article II vests "the executive Power" without Article I's "herein granted." Jackson denied that the clause is "a grant in bulk of all conceivable executive power"; Vinson, Miller in [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]], and the Office of Legal Counsel in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] read it closer to Locke's prerogative to act "without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it" ([[Introduction to Article II]]).

## Elements

1. **What source does the President claim: a statute, or the Constitution alone?**
   - If a statute authorizes the act, the inherent-power question never arises. The Youngstown government disclaimed its statutory route as "much too cumbersome, involved, and time-consuming for the crisis," which forced the constitutional question.
   - Authorization cannot give the President a share in lawmaking itself ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]); see [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
2. **Into which of Jackson's three categories does Congress's action put the President?**
   - *Category one (express or implied authorization):* power "at its maximum," comprising "all that he possesses in his own right plus all that Congress can delegate." The 2001 AUMF is the model ([[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).
   - *Category two (neither grant nor denial):* a "zone of twilight" where authority may be concurrent and the result turns on "the imperatives of events and contemporary imponderables." Examples: [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]], [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]], and [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] before Congress ratified.
   - *Category three (against Congress's express or implied will):* power "at its lowest ebb." Examples: Youngstown itself; [[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]]; the § 2262 non-enforcement in [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]].
   - The War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock is designed to move unauthorized hostilities from category two into category three once it runs ([[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]]).
3. **Has Congress refused, or merely stayed silent?**
   - A considered refusal is category three: Congress weighed emergency seizure in the 1947 Taft-Hartley debates and chose other remedies, which Frankfurter reads as Congress saying "You may not seize."
   - Occupying the field is category three: Jackson placed the seizure there because Congress had "covered" seizure "by three statutory policies inconsistent with this seizure."
   - Silence is category two: "congressional inertia, indifference or quiescence" may "enable, if not invite," independent presidential action.
4. **In category three, is the asserted power exclusive to the President?**
   - Test each clause, as Jackson did, by asking whether Congress is disabled from acting on the subject at all.
   - Commander in Chief: the President is not "Commander in Chief of the country, its industries and its inhabitants."
   - Take Care: the clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker" (Black) and is matched by the Due Process Clause (Jackson).
   - Vesting Clause: an allocation of the powers "thereafter stated," not a grant in bulk (Jackson).
   - Powers treated as exclusive: the pardon, removal of executive officers, and decisions whom to investigate and prosecute ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]); recognition of foreign sovereigns is the one power the Court has upheld in category three (*Zivotofsky v. Kerry*).
5. **In category two, does executive practice supply a "gloss" on executive power?**
   - Frankfurter's test: the practice must be "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned."
   - Three seizures in six months of 1941 fail; the 252 land withdrawals over 80 years in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.* passed.
   - A practice Congress has contested fails, as unilateral war-making did once Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over a veto.
   - Clark's alternative: absent congressional procedures, the President's independent power "depends upon the gravity of the situation confronting the nation."
6. **Is the act lawmaking in character (Black's independent route)?**
   - An order that sets out policy reasons, proclaims rules of conduct and, "like a statute," authorizes subordinate rulemaking is legislation, which Article I, § 1 vests in Congress.
   - The editors' ideal of executive power "includes the action, strength, or force of the government but not its lawmaking power" ([[Introduction to Article II]]).
   - Under Black, a lawmaking order may remain suspect even with a statute behind it; under Jackson, a statute moves the act to category one.
7. **Is force turned outward against an enemy, or inward against Americans?**
   - Jackson would give the "widest latitude" to the President's command of "the instruments of national force, at least when turned against the outside world," but none when it is turned inward "not because of rebellion but because of a lawful economic struggle between industry and labor."
   - [[The Emancipation Proclamation]] (enemy resources in enemy territory) and the blockade in [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] (rebellion) stand; Truman's seizure of American mills falls.
   - Jackson calls the claim that committing troops abroad enlarges domestic power "sinister and alarming"; the Court never decided whether the Korean deployment itself was lawful ([[Declarations of War]]).
8. **Is an emergency doing work the Constitution denies it?**
   - "[T]he emergency did not create power; it merely marked an occasion when power should be exercised" (Douglas).
   - The only express emergency provision is the Suspension Clause, and it sits in Article I; see [[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption:** In category one, the act is "supported by the strongest of presumptions and the widest latitude of judicial interpretation, and the burden of persuasion would rest heavily upon any who might attack it"; a loss there "usually means that the Federal Government as an undivided whole lacks power" (Jackson).
- **Burden:** In category three, the President must show the power is exclusive, not merely important, because the act can be sustained only by "disabling the Congress from acting upon the subject," and such a claim "must be scrutinized with caution."
- **Burden:** In category two, the party invoking executive practice must show one that meets Frankfurter's four requirements.
- **Standard:** No deference on who holds the power, even in wartime: the Court rejected the arguments that delay is dangerous and that the President has better information, because Congress can grant emergency powers with "ease, expedition and safety."
- **Standard:** Deference on facts within a power the President holds: whether a rebellion has reached the level of war "is a question to be decided by him," and his proclamation is "official and conclusive evidence" of the state of war ([[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]]).
- **Standard:** For criminal liability rather than validity, [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] gives absolute immunity within "conclusive and preclusive" authority and presumptive immunity for other official acts, and "[i]t is ultimately the Government's burden to rebut the presumption"; see [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]].
- **Not reviewed / political question:** War-powers suits rarely reach the merits. *Dellums v. Bush* held a Gulf War challenge unripe but not a political question, because the Constitution supplies a clear standard; *Ange v. Bush* held the same dispute a political question; the Supreme Court never decided Vietnam on the merits ([[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]], [[Declarations of War]]).
- **What fills the gap:** Political checks: appropriations, the Senate's refusal to confirm or ratify, impeachment, and disclosure to "an informed electorate" (Brown in [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]]).

## Exceptions

- **Conclusive and preclusive powers.** Congress cannot regulate the pardon, removal of executive officers, or the decision whom to investigate and prosecute ([[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]]); see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].
- **Defensive force.** When war is made on the United States by invasion or organized rebellion, the President is "bound to resist force by force" without waiting for Congress, though "[h]e has no power to initiate or declare a war" ([[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]]); see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
- **Protection of federal officers and instrumentalities.** The Take Care duty reaches "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself," so the President may protect a federal judge, or the mails, without a statute ([[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]]).
- **Enforcement discretion in particular cases.** Declining to prosecute is executive and unreviewable ([[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]]); the limit is a general policy that amounts to "an abdication of its statutory duty" ([[Adams v. Richardson (1973)|Adams v. Richardson]]). See [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
- **Executive agreements.** The President may bind the United States internationally without the Senate. An agreement promising only what he can already do alone is easy; its domestic-law force is contested because the Supremacy Clause names treaties, not executive agreements ([[A Note on Executive Agreements]]). See [[Supremacy and the oath - Art. VI]].
- **Not decided: a "short, explicitly temporary" seizure.** Frankfurter reserved a self-terminating seizure pending congressional approval, and what the President could do if Congress had never legislated on the subject.
- **Not decided: non-enforcement of a statute the President believes unconstitutional.** [[The Thompson Memo]] qualifies its abdication limit with the 1994 OLC view that the President must follow "the Constitution, which takes precedence over other forms of law," and [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] acts on that view as to § 2262.
- **Not decided: whether the President must obey a specific judgment he thinks unconstitutional.** [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] poses the question; the President did not comply and nothing further was done. See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Leading case

[[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]

- **Holding:** 6–3, Black for the Court. Truman's seizure of the steel mills was unlawful: no statute authorized it; the Commander in Chief Clause does not reach seizing property "to keep labor disputes from stopping production," which is "a job for the Nation's lawmakers, not for its military authorities"; and the Take Care Clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker."
- **Driving fact:** Congress had rejected emergency seizure in 1947. Silence would have left a category-two argument; a refusal put the case at the lowest ebb.
- **Driving fact:** The order was domestic and economic, aimed at American mill owners rather than at an enemy.
- **Reasoning (Jackson):** Jackson's framework, not Black's opinion, became the law because it alone gives a rule for other cases: maximum, twilight, lowest ebb, and "conclusive and preclusive" as the only exit from the third.
- **Reasoning (Jackson, emergencies):** The Framers "knew what emergencies were" and wrote only the Suspension Clause; emergency powers "would tend to kindle emergencies." The casebook's note adds that Weimar's emergency clause "helped bring Adolf Hitler to power."
- **Separate opinions:** Frankfurter supplies the competing route, "the gloss which life has written upon" the Constitution, and finds this practice insufficient. Clark, relying on *Little v. Barreme*, holds that the President must follow procedures Congress has prescribed for the crisis.
- **Dissent:** Vinson, with Reed and Minton, reads the seizure as executing Congress's defense programs and holding the status quo "until Congress could act," and rejects the "messenger-boy concept of the Office."
- **Quote:** "With all its defects, delays and inconveniences, men have discovered no technique for long preserving free government except that the Executive be under the law, and that the law be made by parliamentary deliberations."

## Best counter-case

[[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Where Congress has said nothing and the President acts to protect the government's own officers or functions, Neagle sustains him; where Congress has addressed the subject and the President regulates private property or private economic relations, Youngstown condemns him.
- **Holding:** 6–2, Miller for the Court. A deputy marshal who killed Terry while guarding Justice Field acted "in pursuance of a law of the United States," though no statute authorized bodyguards for judges, because "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law.'"
- **Driving fact:** An empty field. Congress had legislated in detail about marshals and said nothing about protecting judges, so the case is category two at worst; Miller never relies on congressional authorization.
- **Driving fact:** The subject was personal protection of a federal officer, not the regulation of anyone's property or business.
- **Argue for the President:** Black's own formula, power from "the Constitution itself," is satisfied because Miller grounds the power there. A government unable to protect the persons who carry on its work is "without the means of self-protection." Clark's Youngstown concurrence lists Neagle among the decisions upholding presidential power, as "protection of federal officials from personal violence while performing official duties."
- **Argue for Congress:** Lamar, dissenting with Fuller, reads "law" as enacted law, the reading Article VI supports by making supreme only laws "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution. The Take Care Clause is phrased as a duty on the President, not a grant, and Miller offers a rhetorical question rather than an argument for the broader reading.
- **Fact that flips it:** A statute providing that marshals shall not accompany judges moves Neagle to category three, where Black's opinion controls; an appropriation for judicial security moves it to category one.
- **Runner-up counter-case:** [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] upheld unilateral presidential force, but Grier concedes "[h]e has no power to initiate or declare a war," so the case supports response, not initiation.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Youngstown holds that the President was at his lowest ebb."** Black's test is whether the act is legislative in character; Jackson's test is what Congress has said. Name the opinion you are applying.
- **"Congress did not authorize it, so the President is at his lowest ebb."** Silence is category two. Category three requires an express or implied denial: a rejected proposal, an occupied field of inconsistent schemes, or a prohibition. [[In re Neagle (1890)|In re Neagle]], [[United States v. Cox (1965)|United States v. Cox]] and [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] are silence cases, and the executive won all three.
- **"Youngstown denies the President emergency power."** The Court held only that this seizure, against Congress's expressed will, was unlawful. It did not deny emergency power in general or all domestic reach of the Commander in Chief Clause, and Frankfurter reserved the short, self-terminating seizure.
- **"Category three means automatic defeat."** It means defeat unless the power is conclusive and preclusive. [[Trump v. United States (2024)|Trump v. United States]] turns that same category into a zone of absolute criminal immunity, so the move from "Congress cannot forbid this act" to "Congress cannot punish this act" is the point to argue.
- **"Congress kept funding it, so it authorized it."** Funding is the weakest category-one evidence, and War Powers Resolution § 8(a)(1) bars inferring authority from an appropriation that does not say so. Kosovo is the fact pattern: the House rejected a declaration, refused to order withdrawal, and Congress then passed a supplemental appropriation. See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **"Deference in The Prize Cases means the President decides his own war powers."** "[A] question to be decided by him" binds courts to his judgment that a state of war exists, not to his view of what powers he has. Executive opinions about executive power, most of what OLC cites in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]], are evidence of a claim, not of congressional acquiescence.
