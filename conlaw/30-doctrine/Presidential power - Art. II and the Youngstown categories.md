## Rule statement

Presidential power is relative to Congress: the President's authority to act "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself," and where it rests on the Constitution alone its strength varies with what Congress has said. The operative framework is Justice Jackson's concurrence in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]], not Black's opinion for the Court — authorization puts the President at his maximum, congressional silence leaves him in a "zone of twilight," and action against the express or implied will of Congress puts him at his "lowest ebb," where the Court can sustain him only by holding the power "conclusive and preclusive" and thereby disabling Congress. The contest underneath the framework is textual: Article II vests "the executive Power" without Article I's "herein granted" qualifier, and whether that vests a residual prerogative in Locke's sense — the power "to act according to discretion, for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it" — or merely allocates the powers Article II thereafter states is the question every hard executive-power problem returns to. Jackson answered that the Vesting Clause is not "a grant in bulk of all conceivable executive power"; Vinson in dissent, Miller in [[In re Neagle]], and the Office of Legal Counsel opinions in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] answer the other way.

## Elements

1. **Identify the asserted source of power.** An act of Congress, or the Constitution itself. If a statute authorizes the act, the constitutional question of inherent power never arises; the Youngstown government conceded that the statutory route was available and called it "much too cumbersome, involved, and time-consuming for the crisis," which is what put the case on constitutional ground.
2. **Sort what Congress has done into one of the three categories.**
   - *Category one — express or implied authorization.* Power "at its maximum," being the President's own plus all Congress can delegate. The 2001 AUMF in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] is the model, and it complies expressly with the War Powers Resolution's clear-statement rule.
   - *Category two — the zone of twilight.* Congress has neither granted nor denied; authority may be concurrent, and the result turns on "the imperatives of events and contemporary imponderables." [[In re Neagle]] and [[United States v. Cox]] sit here, and [[The Prize Cases]] sit here ex ante.
   - *Category three — the lowest ebb.* Congress has spoken against the act, expressly or by implication. Youngstown itself, [[Adams v. Richardson]], and the § 2262 non-enforcement in [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] sit here.
3. **Distinguish congressional silence from congressional refusal.** Youngstown is category three, not two, because Congress considered emergency seizure in the 1947 Taft-Hartley debates and chose mediation and cooling-off injunctions instead; Frankfurter treats that as clear "as if it had said 'You may not seize.'" Occupying a field with inconsistent statutory schemes counts as covering the subject.
4. **In category three, test whether the asserted power is exclusive.** Go clause by clause as Jackson did, and ask whether Congress is disabled from legislating on the subject at all: the Commander in Chief is not "Commander in Chief of the country, its industries and its inhabitants"; the Take Care Clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker" and is matched by the Due Process Clause; the Vesting Clause allocates the powers "thereafter stated."
5. **In category two, run Frankfurter's gloss test on executive practice.** A practice glosses "executive Power" only if it is "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned." Three seizures in six months of 1941 fail; the practice that the WPR was passed over a veto to contest fails for the same reason, because Congress questioned it.
6. **Ask whether the act is legislative in character (Black's independent route).** An order that recites policy reasons, proclaims rules of conduct and authorizes subordinate rulemaking is "like a statute," and Article I, § 1 vests that power in Congress; the President's role in lawmaking is confined to recommending and vetoing. Under Black the objection survives a statute; under Jackson a statute cures it.
7. **Locate the force: outward against an enemy, or inward against Americans.** Jackson's "widest latitude" is for force "turned against the outside world," which is why [[The Emancipation Proclamation]] (enemy resources in enemy territory) and the blockade in [[The Prize Cases]] stand where Truman's seizure of American mills in a domestic labor dispute falls.
8. **Check whether the emergency is doing improper work.** "[T]he emergency did not create power; it merely marked an occasion when power should be exercised" (Douglas). The Constitution's single emergency provision is the Suspension Clause, and it sits in Article I — see [[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]].

## Standard of review / burden

- In category three the burden is on the President, and it is close to insurmountable: the act can be sustained only by "disabling the Congress from acting upon the subject," so the claim must be that the power is exclusive, not merely that it is important.
- In category two there is no standard so much as an inquiry into practice and events; Frankfurter's gloss test supplies the evidentiary threshold, and the burden of showing a long, unbroken, unquestioned practice falls on the party invoking it.
- The Court gives the President no deference on **who holds the power**, even in war: it rejected the argument that delay is dangerous and that the President has better information, because Congress can grant emergency powers with "ease, expedition and safety."
- The Court does defer on **facts within the power**: in [[The Prize Cases]] whether the resistance had reached the level of war "is a question to be decided by him," and the proclamation is "official and conclusive evidence" of a state of war. Keep that deference separate from the allocation question.
- Where the claim is criminal liability rather than validity, [[Trump v. United States]] reallocates the burden: absolute immunity for conduct within "conclusive and preclusive" authority, and for other official acts "[i]t is ultimately the Government's burden to rebut the presumption of immunity."
- Much of the field has no standard because no case is justiciable. On war powers the district courts split — Dellums v. Bush held a challenge unripe but not a political question, because the Constitution supplies a clear standard requiring congressional authorization, while Ange v. Bush held the same dispute a political question — and the Supreme Court never reached the merits of Vietnam. Ripeness, not the political-question doctrine, is the real barrier, since a court cannot decide that Congress refused before Congress votes.
- Where courts are out, the checks are political: appropriations, the Senate's refusal to confirm or ratify, impeachment, and (Brown's point in [[United States v. Cox]]) disclosure to "an informed electorate."

## Exceptions

- **Conclusive and preclusive powers.** Powers Congress cannot regulate at all: the pardon, removal of executive officers, and the decision whom to investigate and prosecute, per [[Trump v. United States]]; recognition of foreign sovereigns is the one power the Court has sustained in category three. See [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]] and [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]].
- **Defensive war.** When war is made on the United States by invasion or organized rebellion, the President "is not only authorized but bound to resist force by force" without waiting for Congress, though "[h]e has no power to initiate or declare a war" ([[The Prize Cases]]). See [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
- **Protective power over federal instrumentalities.** The Take Care duty reaches "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself," so the President may protect a federal judge or the mails without waiting for Congress ([[In re Neagle]]).
- **Enforcement discretion in a particular case.** Declining to prosecute is executive and unreviewable ([[United States v. Cox]]); the limit is a general policy amounting to "an abdication of its statutory duty" ([[Adams v. Richardson]]). See [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
- **Executive agreements.** The President may bind the United States internationally without the Senate, and an agreement promising only what he can already do unilaterally is easy; the Supremacy Clause names treaties and not executive agreements, so the domestic-law effect is the contested part ([[A Note on Executive Agreements]]). See [[Supremacy and the oath - Art. VI]].
- **Reserved by Frankfurter, not decided:** a seizure that is "short, explicitly temporary" and self-terminating unless Congress approves, and what the President could do if Congress had never legislated on the subject at all.
- **Not decided:** whether the President may decline to enforce a statute he believes unconstitutional. [[The Thompson Memo]] qualifies its own abdication limit with the 1994 OLC position that the President must act in accordance with "the Constitution, which takes precedence over other forms of law," and [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] acts on it as to § 2262.
- **Not decided:** whether the President must obey a specific judicial judgment he believes unconstitutional. [[Ex parte Merryman]] is the case that poses it and the case in which nothing was done. See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Leading case

[[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]

- Holds that Truman's seizure of the steel mills to avert a strike was unlawful: no statute authorized it, the Commander in Chief Clause does not reach seizing property "to keep labor disputes from stopping production," which is "a job for the Nation's lawmakers, not for its military authorities," and the Take Care Clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker."
- The fact that drove it: Congress had considered emergency seizure in the 1947 Taft-Hartley debates and rejected it. Silence would have left a category-two argument; a refusal put the case at the lowest ebb.
- The second driving fact: the order was domestic and economic, directed at American mill owners, not at an enemy. Jackson's widest latitude is for force turned outward.
- Jackson's framework is the holding for exam purposes, and it became the law because it is the only opinion offering a rule for other cases: maximum, twilight, lowest ebb, and "conclusive and preclusive" as the sole exit from the third.
- Frankfurter supplies the competing route: "It is an inadmissibly narrow conception of American constitutional law to confine it to the words of the Constitution and to disregard the gloss which life has written upon them" — and then finds this practice insufficient.
- Jackson refuses inherent emergency power on consequences: the Framers "knew what emergencies were" and wrote only the Suspension Clause; emergency powers "would tend to kindle emergencies," as Weimar's Article 48 did.
- Jackson calls the argument that committing troops abroad enlarges domestic power the most "sinister and alarming" doctrine the Court could adopt, because the President would enlarge his own power by his own act.
- The sentence worth quoting: "With all its defects, delays and inconveniences, men have discovered no technique for long preserving free government except that the Executive be under the law, and that the law be made by parliamentary deliberations."

## Best counter-case

[[In re Neagle]]

- Holds that a deputy marshal who killed Terry while guarding Justice Field acted "in pursuance of a law of the United States" and could not be tried by California, even though no statute authorized bodyguards for judges: "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law.'"
- The distinguishing fact is the empty field. Congress had legislated in detail about marshals and said nothing about protecting judges, so the case is category two at worst; Miller never reaches for congressional authorization, which is why the opinion reads as independent Article II power.
- The second distinguishing fact is the subject: personal protection of a federal officer, not the regulation of private property or the settlement of a private economic dispute. Nobody's liberty or property was regulated by the order itself.
- **Arguing the President's side:** Black's own formula — power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself" — is satisfied verbatim, because Miller grounds the power in the Constitution; the Take Care Clause reaches "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself"; and on the consequences, a government that cannot protect the persons who carry on its work is "without the means of self-protection." Jackson's concurrence itself treats protective power as the real residue of inherent executive authority.
- **Arguing Congress's side:** Lamar's reading of "law" as enacted law is the one the Supremacy Clause supports, since Article VI makes supreme only the laws "made in Pursuance thereof"; the Take Care Clause is phrased as a duty imposed on the President, not as a grant of power, and sits among his obligations; and Miller offers a rhetorical question rather than an argument for the broader reading.
- **The fact that flips it:** a statute providing that marshals shall not accompany judges moves Neagle to category three and Black's opinion controls; an appropriation for judicial security moves it to category one and the case is easy.
- Runner-up counter-case: [[The Prize Cases]], where unilateral presidential force was upheld and later ratified — but note that Grier concedes "[h]e has no power to initiate or declare a war," so it supports response, not initiation, and the OLC "national interest" theory in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] needs more than the case gives.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Citing "Youngstown" for Black's opinion when the question calls for Jackson's.** Black's test is whether the act is legislative in character, and it would condemn some acts Congress had authorized; Jackson's test is what Congress has said. Naming which opinion you are using is worth more than reciting the categories.
- **Treating congressional silence as the lowest ebb.** Silence is category two. Category three requires an express or implied denial: a rejected amendment, an occupied field of inconsistent schemes, or a statutory prohibition. [[In re Neagle]], [[United States v. Cox]] and [[The Prize Cases]] are all silence cases, and the President wins all three.
- **Overstating the holding.** Youngstown did not hold that the President lacks emergency power in general, that the Commander in Chief Clause has no domestic application, or that practice never matters; it held that this seizure, against Congress's expressed will, was unlawful, and Frankfurter expressly reserved the short, self-terminating seizure.
- **Reading category three as automatic defeat.** It is defeat unless the power is conclusive and preclusive — and [[Trump v. United States]] converts that same category into a zone of absolute criminal immunity, so the more powers are held exclusive, the more conduct Congress can neither regulate nor punish. The move from "Congress cannot forbid this act" to "Congress cannot punish this act" is the point to argue about.
- **Counting appropriations as authorization.** Funding a deployment is the weakest form of category-one support, and War Powers Resolution § 8(a)(1) refuses to infer authorization from any appropriation unless it says so specifically; Kosovo is the fact pattern, where the House rejected a declaration, refused to order removal, and then passed a supplemental appropriation. Note the converse remedy problem: a President who objects to a funding restriction may decline to spend, but voiding the restriction creates no appropriation ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]). See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **Treating Frankfurter's gloss as "practice wins," and treating deference on facts as deference on power.** The gloss test has four requirements — systematic, unbroken, known to Congress, never questioned — and executive-branch opinions about executive power, which is most of what OLC cites in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]], are evidence of a claim rather than of acquiescence; separately, "a question to be decided by him" in [[The Prize Cases]] means the President's judgment that a state of war exists binds the courts, not that he decides what war powers he has.
