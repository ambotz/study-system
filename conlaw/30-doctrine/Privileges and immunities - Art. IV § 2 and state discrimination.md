## Rule statement

Article IV § 2 cl. 1 is a rule of equal treatment, not a grant of rights: a citizen of one state, while in another, is entitled to those privileges and immunities that are "in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments," and not to whatever the host state happens to confer on its own citizens ([[Corfield v. Coryell]]). It therefore forbids a state nothing except discrimination — a state may close a resource or an activity to everyone — and it creates no substantive entitlement that travels with the visitor. Modern doctrine keeps that structure and adds a second step: if the right is fundamental to the promotion of interstate harmony, the state must show a substantial reason for treating nonresidents differently and a close relation between the discrimination and that reason. The contest is what the clause actually decided: the famous catalogue is dictum, the holding is the narrow common-property exception that later doctrine has largely dismantled, and the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause borrowed Washington's phrase for the entirely different job of securing rights against a citizen's own state.

## Elements

1. **Is the claimant a citizen of another state?** The clause speaks of "[t]he Citizens of each State," so a corporation or trade association is outside it and must proceed, if at all, under the Commerce Clause. See [[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]].
2. **Does the law discriminate on the basis of state citizenship or residence?** A prohibition falling on "[n]o person residing in, or out of this state" alike raises no claim at all; in [[Corfield v. Coryell]] only the sixth section of New Jersey's act, keyed to "actual inhabitant and resident," was even arguable.
3. **Is the interest a fundamental privilege or immunity, or merely something this state gives its own?** Washington's category is rights "enjoyed by the citizens of the several states which compose this Union, from the time of their becoming free, independent, and sovereign"; the modern formulation asks whether the right is fundamental to the promotion of interstate harmony.
   - Protection by the government; life and liberty; the right "to acquire and possess property of every kind, and to pursue and obtain happiness and safety."
   - Passage through or residence in another state "for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits, or otherwise."
   - The benefit of the writ of habeas corpus, and the right "to institute and maintain actions of any kind in the courts of the state."
   - Taking, holding and disposing of property, real or personal.
   - "[A]n exemption from higher taxes or impositions than are paid by the other citizens of the state."
   - The elective franchise, but only "as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state in which it is to be exercised," which drains the entry of content.
4. **Check the common-property exception.** Where the state holds a resource for its citizens as "tenants in common," the clause is not "a grant of a cotenancy in the common property of the state, to the citizens of all the other states"; a privately owned several fishery is simply property and excludes "citizens or strangers" alike.
5. **If the right is fundamental, put the state to its justification.** It must show a substantial reason for the differential — typically that nonresidents are a peculiar source of the evil — and a close relation between the discrimination and that reason.
6. **Remember the internal qualifier.** Every right on the list is held "subject nevertheless to such restraints as the government may justly prescribe for the general good of the whole," so the clause never defeats an evenhanded regulation.

## Standard of review / burden

There is no tier-of-scrutiny label; the inquiry is two steps with a shifting burden. The claimant must first put the interest inside the category of fundamental privileges, and [[Corfield v. Coryell]] itself shows the category doing all the work — Washington never reaches justification, because the use of the state's common property is outside the clause from the start. Once the interest is inside, the burden moves to the state to supply a substantial reason and a close fit, and the modern cases apply that seriously: a bar-admission residency rule fell for want of one, while recreational access to a state's game did not even clear step one. The state nonetheless retains "considerable leeway in analyzing local evils and in prescribing appropriate cures," and the clause, "like many other constitutional provisions, . . . is not an absolute." Note the asymmetry with the other clause policing the same conduct: after [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] a court reviewing a nondiscriminatory state law under the dormant Commerce Clause proceeds with "extreme caution," treats the exercise as "a matter of 'extreme delicacy'" to be undertaken only "where the infraction is clear," and treats the balancing test as largely an evidentiary route to discriminatory purpose. Article IV § 2 step two is the stricter test, so where the plaintiff is a natural person and the line is drawn by citizenship, plead this clause first.

## Exceptions

- **Common property of the state.** [[Corfield v. Coryell]]'s holding: an unappropriated fishery in state waters may be reserved to residents, because otherwise the beds "might be totally exhausted and destroyed." The 1948 shrimping decision rejected the premise that a state owns its fisheries in any sense that permits hoarding, so the exception survives mainly for recreational uses.
- **Recreational rather than commercial access.** A 1978 decision sustained higher nonresident elk-hunting fees, which is Corfield's oyster bed in modern dress; commercial livelihoods and professional licensure sit on the other side of the line.
- **Political rights.** The franchise is equalized only on the state's own terms, and the clause has never been read to equalize the rights of participation that define membership in the state's political community.
- **Corporations and associations.** They are not "Citizens" for this clause, which is the doctrinal reason a challenge like the pork producers' had to be brought under the Commerce Clause.
- **Evenhanded prohibitions.** Nothing stopped New Jersey from closing the oyster beds to everyone, as the first and second sections of the same act did; the clause polices the line, not the restriction.
- **Municipal discrimination.** A city ordinance favoring its own residents is within the clause's reach even though it disadvantages other residents of the same state, but a record may be too thin to evaluate the city's justification.
- **Not decided: the relation between the two privileges-and-immunities clauses.** The Fourteenth Amendment's framers quoted Washington's catalogue as their gloss; the 1873 decision reading their clause down to rights of national citizenship left Article IV doing the work it had always done, and a right on Washington's list is therefore not automatically a right against one's own state.
- **Not decided: whether the antidiscrimination principle belongs here rather than in the dormant Commerce Clause.** [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] notes that some Justices think the principle "may be more appropriately housed elsewhere in the Constitution," and expressly declines to decide, while redistributing the extraterritoriality concern to structural "sovereignty and comity," the Due Process Clause and the Full Faith and Credit Clause.

## Leading case

[[Corfield v. Coryell]]

- Holds that New Jersey could forfeit a Philadelphia-owned sloop for dredging oysters in Maurice River Cove, because a share in the state's common property is not a privilege or immunity the clause protects.
- The fact that drove it is the nature of the resource: oysters are stationary and lie "in the water and soil" of the state, so the citizens "may be considered as tenants in common of this property," and the state's exclusive right "has never been ceded by that state, in express terms, to the United States."
- The consequence supplies the only real argument in the opinion — without a power to exclude, beds "which, by being exposed to too general use, may be exhausted" would be destroyed — while the rest of the reasoning is classification plus authority from Grotius and Rutherforth.
- The purpose stated is comity, quoting the corresponding article of the Articles of Confederation: the clause was "manifestly calculated" "the better to secure and perpetuate mutual friendship and intercourse among the people of the different states of the Union."
- The sentence worth quoting is the category, not the list: "We feel no hesitation in confining these expressions to those privileges and immunities which are, in their nature, fundamental; which belong, of right, to the citizens of all free governments."
- The sentence that decides the case: the legislature is not "bound to extend to the citizens of all the other states the same advantages as are secured to their own citizens" in "regulating the use of the common property of the citizens of such state."
- Two cautions on the authority of the opinion. It is one Justice sitting on circuit, so it binds no one, which is part of why it is remembered for its dicta; and it quotes the clause as "all the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several states," inserting a word the Constitution does not contain.

## Best counter-case

[[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]

- Holds that California may condition the in-state sale of pork on how the pigs were confined elsewhere, and that absent purposeful discrimination there is no "almost per se" rule against state laws with the "practical effect of controlling" out-of-state conduct — the outsider-burdening state law that survives.
- Three distinguishing facts keep it out of Article IV § 2: the plaintiffs were trade associations and corporations rather than citizens; they conceded that Proposition 12 "imposes the same burdens on in-state pork producers that it imposes on out-of-state ones"; and the law restricts a sale rather than denying any visitor a fundamental privilege.
- Arguing that outsiders should win anyway: the burden falls overwhelmingly on out-of-state firms because California imports nearly all the pork it consumes, and a market that large lets one state set national husbandry policy — the modern form of the pre-constitutional tariffs Gorsuch himself recounts, Rhode Island taxing imported "New-England Rum" and Virginia taxing "vessels coming within th[e S]tate from any of the United States."
- Arguing that the state should win: "[i]n our interconnected national marketplace, many (maybe most) state laws have the 'practical effect of controlling' extraterritorial behavior," so the petitioners' rule "would cast a shadow over laws long understood to represent valid exercises of the States' constitutionally reserved powers"; and if market size mattered, "voters in States with smaller markets are constitutionally entitled to greater authority to regulate in-state sales than voters in States with larger markets. So much for the Constitution's 'fundamental principle of equal sovereignty among the States.'"
- The reductio worth memorizing, because it shows how far the Court will go to keep nondiscriminatory laws alive: many states ban the sale of horsemeat, and on the dissent's view "all it would take is one complaint from an unhappy out-of-state producer and—presto—the Constitution would protect the sale of horsemeat."
- The transferable point for this module is the pairing of instruments. Article IV § 2 protects citizens and asks whether the state denied a visitor a fundamental privilege it grants its own; the dormant Commerce Clause protects "the interstate market . . . from prohibitive or burdensome regulations," not "particular . . . firms" or "particular structure[s] or methods of operation," and asks about protectionist purpose and effect. The Syllabus cites both halves together, "U. S. Const., Art. I, §8, cl. 3; Art. IV, §2."
- Note also that [[Corfield v. Coryell]] itself carried a dormant commerce claim and lost it, in a paragraph the casebook's editors replaced with a cross-reference — the same plaintiff losing under both clauses two centuries apart.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Reciting the catalogue as the holding. [[Corfield v. Coryell]] lists nothing resembling oyster-raking, and it decides the case by holding the activity outside the clause; an answer that quotes the list has quoted the dictum and skipped the rule.
- Treating the clause as a source of rights. Out-of-staters do not carry their own state's rights with them, and a Minnesota fishing licence is not good in Wisconsin; the clause equalizes, and a state that closes an activity to everyone violates nothing.
- Merging Article IV § 2 with the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause. One secures a visitor equal treatment in another state; the other was meant to secure rights against a citizen's own state and was read down in 1873. The irony worth keeping: the Fourteenth Amendment's framers borrowed a passage that was dictum in a case upholding a discriminatory state law.
- Relying on state ownership of a resource as a trump. That was Washington's rationale and it no longer carries commercial activity; the modern question is whether the state has a substantial reason, closely related, for treating nonresidents worse.
- Bringing a corporate client under Article IV § 2. Corporations are not citizens for the clause, so the claim has to be recast under the Commerce Clause — where, after [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]], a nondiscriminatory law is very hard to attack.
- Reading the elective-franchise entry as giving visitors the vote. The qualifier "as regulated and established by the laws or constitution of the state in which it is to be exercised" means the visitor gets the franchise on the state's terms, and residence is one of those terms; treat the entry as either a mistake or a nullity.
- Running the same test under both clauses. Article IV's step two — substantial reason and close relation — is stricter than the posture of "extreme caution" and "extreme delicacy" the dormant Commerce Clause now takes toward nondiscriminatory laws, so which clause is pleaded can decide the case.
- Assuming that a heavier practical burden on outsiders states a claim. Under Article IV the question is discrimination against citizens in a fundamental right; under the Commerce Clause after Pork Producers it is purposeful discrimination against the interstate market, and harm to particular producers' preferred "methods of operation" is not a cognizable burden — "[t]hat goes for pigs no less than gas stations."
- Citing the wrong year for [[Corfield v. Coryell]]. The casebook prints 1825; the decision is from the April 1823 term of the circuit court, and the conventional citation is 6 F. Cas. 546 (C.C.E.D. Pa. 1823).
