## Rule statement

A law can be made, changed, or repealed only by the same text passing both Houses and going to the President. Any action "legislative in its character and effect," meaning one that alters "the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch," must follow that "single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure" ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]). "Amendment and repeal of statutes, no less than enactment, must conform with Art. I," so a presidential power to cancel enacted provisions is void even with Congress's consent ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]). The contest is over classification: agencies alter private rights without § 7, so the rule rests on a presumption about which power each branch exercises, and White in Chadha and Scalia in Clinton say the real question is delegation.

## Elements

1. **Which branch acted, and what power is it presumed to be exercising?**
   - "When the Executive acts, he presumptively acts in an executive or administrative capacity," and when one House acts, it presumptively acts in its legislative role.
   - The branches are "not 'hermetically' sealed" but are "functionally identifiable."
   - The presumption, not the effects test, is what keeps agency rulemaking outside § 7.
2. **Is the action "legislative in its character and effect"?**
   - Does it alter "the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch"? In [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] the House resolution altered the rights of Chadha, the Attorney General and Executive Branch officials.
   - Is its legislative character "confirmed by the character of the congressional action it supplants"? Ask what Congress would have had to do without the device; if the answer is "pass a statute," the device is legislation.
   - Does it enact, amend or repeal? All three are treated alike, and a cancellation that prevents an item "from having legal force or effect" is a repeal "[i]n both legal and practical effect."
3. **Does one of the four express one-House powers apply?**
   - House impeachment (Art. I, § 2, cl. 5); Senate trial (Art. I, § 3, cl. 6); Senate confirmation and Senate treaty ratification (Art. II, § 2, cl. 2).
   - Where the Framers meant one House to act alone, "they did so in explicit, unambiguous terms," so no fifth power is implied.
4. **If no exception applies, did the identical text pass both Houses and go to the President?**
   - Both Houses must pass "precisely the same text," and the President must sign it or it must pass over a veto.
   - A process that yields "truncated versions of two bills that passed both Houses of Congress" produces a law whose text neither House voted on.
5. **Was the President's role confined to approving or returning the whole bill before enactment?**
   - "There is no provision in the Constitution that authorizes the President to enact, to amend, or to repeal statutes."
   - Washington's understanding was that the President must "approve all the parts of a Bill, or reject it in toto."
6. **Set aside consent, utility and practice.**
   - "Congress cannot alter the procedures set out in Article I, § 7, without amending the Constitution."
   - "The fact that Congress intended such a result is of no moment."
   - "[P]olicy arguments supporting even useful 'political inventions' are subject to the demands of the Constitution," and "the Framers ranked other values higher than efficiency."
7. **Classify any reserved congressional check by its form.**
   - One-House veto: void ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).
   - Two-House concurrent resolution not presented to the President: void, because cl. 3 requires presentment of "Every Order, Resolution, or Vote" needing both Houses' concurrence. War Powers Resolution § 5(c) is the example, and Nixon objected in 1973 that a concurrent resolution "does not normally have the force of law, since it denies the President his constitutional role in approving legislation" ([[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]]).
   - Committee veto: void a fortiori.
   - Joint resolution of disapproval, passed by both Houses and presented: valid, because it is an ordinary law.
   - Report-and-wait provisions, sunset clauses and expedited-rescission procedures: valid, because nothing changes the law except a later statute passed under § 7.
8. **If the executive acted under discretion a valid statute conferred, ask a delegation question instead.**
   - Statutory discretion to decline to spend, and substantive rulemaking that adds to the law, both leave the enacted text intact.
   - Scalia's point in Clinton is that § 7 no more forbids authorized "Executive reduction" than authorized "Executive augmentation"; the limits on both come from [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption:** The Court begins "with the presumption that the challenged statute is valid," but once an action is classified as legislative, § 7 is exclusive and no functional justification is weighed ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]).
- **Burden:** Whoever claims a one-House or presidential exception must find it in the text, because the four express unicameral powers show that the Framers spoke clearly when they meant to allow one.
- **Standard:** Formal and categorical. The Court concedes the procedures are "clumsy, inefficient, even unworkable," and answers that "those hard choices were consciously made by men who had lived under a form of government that permitted arbitrary governmental acts to go unchecked."
- **Standard:** Long practice aggravates rather than cures. With 295 veto-type procedures in 196 statutes since 1932, "our inquiry is sharpened rather than blunted by the fact that congressional veto provisions are appearing with increasing frequency." Burger cites Black's formalism in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]; White invokes Jackson.
- **Standard:** Silence is read as prohibition where the procedure was "the product of the great debates and compromises that produced the Constitution itself," so the absence of any provision for partial, post-enactment presidential repeal is "equivalent to an express prohibition" ([[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Not available. [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]] held the challenge justiciable, and the Houses' participation supplied adverseness where the Executive agreed with Chadha; in [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] the parties injured by the cancellations had standing. See [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- **What fills the gap:** Severability decides what the challenger actually wins. Chadha kept his suspension of deportation only because § 244(c)(2) was severable from the rest of the Act.
- **What fills the gap:** The Court decides on presentment and stops. [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] found it "unnecessary to consider" the District Court's alternative balance-of-powers holding, and said the historical briefing "illuminates the delegation issue but does not really bear on the narrow issue that is dispositive."

## Exceptions

- **The four unicameral powers.** House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation, and Senate treaty ratification are the only occasions on which one House acts alone with the force of law.
- **Delegated agency action.** Agencies alter private rights without bicameralism or presentment; White, quoting Jackson, calls them "a veritable fourth branch." The majority's answer is the presumption that the Executive acts executively, not an exception carved out of § 7.
- **Statutory discretion to decline to spend.** The tradition runs from the First Congress's "sum[s] not exceeding" appropriations through Jefferson's unspent gunboat money to *Train v. City of New York*, which rejected an unauthorized impoundment while "implicitly confirm[ing] that Congress may confer discretion upon the Executive to withhold appropriated funds" (Scalia in Clinton). See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **Joint resolutions of disapproval.** Congress may reserve review that runs through § 7 (the Congressional Review Act model), but the President can veto the disapproval, so restoring congressional control takes two-thirds of each House.
- **Each House's rulemaking power.** Art. I, § 5 is the argument for devices that define what counts as passage, such as separate enrollment of items after one package vote, or proposals deemed passed after thirty days and then presented (Chadha Note 3). The distinction from Chadha is thin: the final product goes to the President, and one House's vote alone never changes anyone's rights.
- **Bundling.** Nothing in the text limits the size of a bill, so a 500-page appropriations package leaves the President an all-or-nothing choice (Chadha Note 4); that is the problem the Line Item Veto Act tried to solve, and the holding in Clinton leaves it in place.
- **Not decided: a private bill against named individuals.** The Chadha majority says the House's result "could have been achieved, if at all, only by legislation requiring deportation." The words "if at all" point to Powell's adjudication rationale and to [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]] and [[United States v. Lovett (1946)]].
- **Not decided: a redrafted cancellation power.** The violation in Clinton rests on the Act's words "cancel" and "legal force or effect." Scalia says a power to "decline to spend" would be valid beyond "the slightest doubt," and Stevens concedes the spending-discretion tradition.
- **Not decided: severability of War Powers Resolution § 5(c).** The concurrent-resolution removal provision fails after [[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]; whether the rest of the statute, including the sixty-day clock in § 5(b), survives is open. See [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

## Leading case

[[INS v. Chadha (1983)]]

- **Holding:** 7–2 (Burger), the one-House veto in Immigration and Nationality Act § 244(c)(2) is unconstitutional, and with it every legislative veto of the same design; the House's action was "essentially legislative in purpose and effect" and required bicameral passage and presentment.
- **Driving fact:** The resolution "was not submitted to the Senate or presented to the President." Passed by both Houses and signed, the same measure would have been an ordinary private law.
- **Driving fact:** Without § 244(c)(2), neither House could have compelled the Attorney General to deport Chadha except by legislation, which is how the Court knows the veto was legislative.
- **Reasoning (Burger):** Madison worried at the Convention that presentment could be evaded "by the simple expedient of calling a proposed law a 'resolution' or 'vote,'" which is why cl. 3 exists; the four express unicameral powers confirm that no others were intended.
- **Separate opinions (Powell, concurring in the judgment):** The narrower ground is that "[w]hen Congress finds that a particular person does not satisfy the statutory criteria for permanent residence in this country it has assumed a judicial function"; "[t]he House did not enact a general rule." Powell's rationale would leave one-House vetoes of general agency rules intact.
- **Separate opinions (White, dissenting):** The veto is "an important if not indispensable political invention," and the Court "sounds the death knell for nearly 200 other statutory provisions." "If Congress may delegate lawmaking power to independent and Executive agencies, it is most difficult to understand Art. I as prohibiting Congress from also reserving a check on legislative power for itself." Rehnquist's dissent, joined by White, is omitted from the casebook.
- **Quote:** "With all the obvious flaws of delay, untidiness, and potential for abuse, we have not yet found a better way to preserve freedom than by making the exercise of power subject to the carefully crafted restraints spelled out in the Constitution."

## Best counter-case

[[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Chadha condemns a shortcut taken outside any statute's enactment; Clinton extends the rule to a power the President exercised under a statute that itself passed § 7, so ask whether the power granted lets the President change which enacted text is law (void) or only lets him use discretion the text leaves him (a delegation question).
- **Holding:** 6–3 (Stevens), the Line Item Veto Act violates the Presentment Clause, because a cancellation deprives an enacted item of "legal force or effect" and so is a partial repeal by the President alone. Clinton runs Chadha in reverse, protecting Congress's place in lawmaking rather than the President's.
- **Driving facts:** The cancellation came after enactment, and it reached part of a statute rather than the whole bill the constitutional return addresses; the President "meticulously followed" the Act's procedures.
- **Argue for invalidity (Stevens and Kennedy):** House passage, Senate passage of "precisely the same text," and the signature are all required, and "[i]f one paragraph of that text had been omitted at any one of those three stages," the law would not have been validly enacted. Kennedy adds that "[t]hat a congressional cession of power is voluntary does not make it innocuous," and "one Congress cannot yield up its own powers, much less those of other Congresses to follow."
- **Argue for validity (Breyer and Scalia):** Breyer: "To drive exactly 65 miles per hour on an interstate highway closely resembles an act that violates the speed limit. But it does not violate that limit." Scalia: presentment was "fully complied with," "there is not a dime's worth of difference" between cancelling a spending item and authorizing spending at the President's discretion, and the Act's title "has succeeded in faking out the Supreme Court."
- **Exam wedge:** The holding turns on drafting. Change "cancel" and "legal force or effect" to a discretion to decline to spend and the same practical power survives, which suggests the Court was protecting the quantum of power handed to the President, a delegation concern it could not reach under the slack intelligible-principle test.
- **Executive-side mirror:** [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] argues that a statute cannot "take away, by a mere legislative act, authorities which the President has properly exercised under the Constitution," and that "[t]he only way in which the constitutional powers of a branch of the Government can be altered is by amending the Constitution." That premise is the majority's own in Chadha and Clinton, aimed at Congress instead.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The defect in Chadha was that only one House acted."** Presentment is an independent requirement, so a two-House concurrent resolution fails too; that is why § 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution, removal of forces "if the Congress so directs by concurrent resolution," is void.
- **Running the argument straight off cl. 3.** By its terms cl. 3 covers only votes "to which the Concurrence of the Senate and House . . . may be necessary," and a one-House veto requires no concurrence. The textual case depends on first classifying the action as legislative under § 1 and cl. 2, which is where White's original-meaning reply lives.
- **"Both branches agreed, so the scheme is valid."** Agreement saved neither the legislative veto, which Presidents accepted "as the price for a broad delegation of authority," nor the Line Item Veto Act, which Congress passed and the President signed. [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] category one can enlarge the President's executive discretion but cannot give him a share in the § 7 process.
- **Overstating [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]].** The Court did not hold that the President may never decline to spend appropriated funds, and did not hold that the Act delegated too much; it held that a statutory power to cancel enacted provisions violates the Presentment Clause.
- **Treating Powell's concurrence as the holding.** The Court took the presentment route, which reaches every legislative veto; Powell's adjudication rationale would have left one-House vetoes of general agency rules standing.
- **Calling a signing statement a line-item veto.** A signing statement changes nothing in the law and a court remains free to disagree; a cancellation changes the statute's legal effect. See [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] and [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
