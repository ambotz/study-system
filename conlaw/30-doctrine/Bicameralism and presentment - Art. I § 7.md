## Rule statement

Article I, § 1 vests the legislative power in a Congress "which shall consist of a Senate and House," § 7, cl. 2 requires every bill to be presented to the President, and cl. 3 extends presentment to "Every Order, Resolution, or Vote" requiring the concurrence of both Houses; together they make "a single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure" that is exclusive. Whether an action must run that gauntlet "depends not on their form but upon 'whether they contain matter which is properly to be regarded as legislative in its character and effect'" — that is, whether it has "the purpose and effect of altering the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch" ([[INS v. Chadha]]). The rule binds both political branches and reaches unmaking as well as making: "Amendment and repeal of statutes, no less than enactment, must conform with Art. I," so a presidential cancellation of enacted provisions is a partial repeal and void ([[Clinton v. City of New York]]). Neither branch's agreement cures the defect, because "Congress cannot alter the procedures set out in Article I, § 7, without amending the Constitution." The contest is over the classification step rather than the procedure: agencies alter private rights every day without bicameralism or presentment, so the rule holds together only on the presumption that each actor exercises its own branch's power, and Scalia's charge in [[Clinton v. City of New York]] is that the real question in these cases is delegation, not presentment.

## Elements

1. **Identify the actor and apply the presumption that fixes the branch.** "When the Executive acts, he presumptively acts in an executive or administrative capacity," and when one House acts, it acts in its legislative sphere. The branches are "not 'hermetically' sealed" but are "functionally identifiable."
2. **Ask whether the action is "legislative in its character and effect."**
   - Does it alter "the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch"? In [[INS v. Chadha]] the House resolution altered the rights of Chadha, the Attorney General and Executive Branch officials alike.
   - Is its legislative character "confirmed by the character of the congressional action it supplants"? Ask what Congress would have had to do without the device: if the answer is "pass a statute," the device is legislation.
   - Does it enact, amend or repeal? All three are governed identically, and a cancellation that prevents a provision "from having legal force or effect" is a repeal "[i]n both legal and practical effect."
3. **Check the four express exceptions where one House acts alone with the force of law:** House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation of appointments, and Senate ratification of treaties. Where the Framers meant one House to act alone "they did so in explicit, unambiguous terms."
4. **If no exception applies, require both elements in full.** Both Houses must pass "precisely the same text," and the bill must be presented; a process that yields "truncated versions of two bills that passed both Houses of Congress" produces a law "whose text was not voted on by either House of Congress or presented to the President."
5. **Confirm that the President's participation was confined to approving or returning the whole bill before it became law.** "There is no provision in the Constitution that authorizes the President to enact, to amend, or to repeal statutes," and Washington's understanding was that the President must "approve all the parts of a Bill, or reject it in toto."
6. **Disregard consent, utility and practice.** "The fact that Congress intended such a result is of no moment"; "policy arguments supporting even useful 'political inventions' are subject to the demands of the Constitution"; and "the Framers ranked other values higher than efficiency."
7. **Classify the reserved check by its form, because form is the whole of the analysis at this step.**
   - One-House veto: void ([[INS v. Chadha]]).
   - Two-House concurrent resolution, not presented: void, because cl. 3 by its terms requires presentment of measures needing both Houses' concurrence — the defect in § 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution, which [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] identified ten years before Chadha.
   - Committee veto: worse still.
   - Joint resolution of disapproval, passed by both Houses and presented: valid, because it is an ordinary law.
   - Report-and-wait, sunset clauses and expedited-rescission procedures: valid, because nothing changes the law except a later statute passed under § 7.
8. **Where the action is executive discretion conferred by a validly enacted statute, ask a delegation question instead of a presentment question.** Statutory authority to decline to spend appropriated funds, and substantive rulemaking that adds to the law, both leave the enacted text intact; see [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]].

## Standard of review / burden

- The review is formalist and categorical: once the action is classified as legislative, § 7 is exclusive and no functional justification is weighed. The Court concedes the procedures are "clumsy, inefficient, even unworkable" and answers that "those hard choices were consciously made by men who had lived under a form of government that permitted arbitrary governmental acts to go unchecked."
- Long and widespread practice does not create an exception and is treated as aggravating rather than curative: with 295 veto-type procedures in 196 statutes since 1932, "our inquiry is sharpened rather than blunted by the fact that congressional veto provisions are appearing with increasing frequency." That is Black's formalism in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]], not Frankfurter's gloss of practice.
- Constitutional silence is read as prohibition where the procedure was "the product of the great debates and compromises that produced the Constitution itself": the absence of any provision for partial, post-enactment presidential repeal is "equivalent to an express prohibition."
- The burden of textual argument falls on whoever claims an exception, because the four express instances of unicameral action show that the Framers spoke clearly when they meant one House to act alone.
- Justiciability is not a route out. [[INS v. Chadha]] held the challenge was not a nonjusticiable political question, and the Houses' intervention supplied adverseness where the Executive Branch agreed with the challenger; [[Clinton v. City of New York]] found standing in the parties injured by the cancellations. Cross-reference [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- Severability is the threshold that decides what the plaintiff actually wins: Chadha kept his suspension only because § 244(c)(2) was severable from the rest of the Act.
- The Court reaches the presentment ground first and stops. [[Clinton v. City of New York]] found it "unnecessary to consider" both the delegation ground and the District Court's balance-of-powers holding, and said the historical briefing "illuminates the delegation issue but does not really bear on the narrow issue that is dispositive."

## Exceptions

- **The four unicameral powers**: impeachment by the House, trial by the Senate, Senate confirmation, and Senate ratification of treaties.
- **Delegated agency action.** Agencies alter private rights without bicameralism or presentment, which White calls "a veritable fourth branch"; the majority's answer is the presumption that the Executive acts executively, not an exception carved from § 7.
- **Statutory discretion to decline to spend.** The tradition runs from the First Congress's "sum[s] not exceeding" appropriations through Jefferson's unspent gunboat money to Train v. City of New York, which rejected an unauthorized impoundment while "implicitly confirm[ing] that Congress may confer discretion upon the Executive to withhold appropriated funds." See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **Joint resolutions of disapproval.** Congress may reserve review that runs through § 7, the Congressional Review Act model — with the catch that the President can veto the disapproval, so a determined President holds the action in place unless two-thirds of each House object.
- **Each House's rulemaking power** is the argument for devices that define what counts as passage, such as separate enrollment of items after a single package vote, or a scheme deeming proposals passed after thirty days and then presenting them. Whether that power can bear the weight is undecided, and the honest distinction from Chadha is thin: the final product goes to the President, and one House's vote alone never changes anyone's rights.
- **Bundling.** Nothing in the text limits Congress to small bills, so an appropriations package of 500 pages leaves the President an all-or-nothing choice; that is the practical problem the Line Item Veto Act was written to solve and the one the holding leaves in place.
- **Not decided: a private bill singling out named individuals.** The Chadha majority says the result "could have been achieved, if at all, only by legislation requiring deportation," and the hedge points at Powell's adjudication rationale and at [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]] and [[United States v. Lovett]].
- **Not decided: a redrafted cancellation power.** The violation in [[Clinton v. City of New York]] rests on the Act's own words "cancel" and "legal force or effect"; Scalia says a power to "decline to spend" would be valid beyond "the slightest doubt," and Stevens never denies it.
- **Not decided: severability of War Powers Resolution § 5(c).** The concurrent-resolution removal provision is unconstitutional after [[INS v. Chadha]]; whether the rest of the statute, including the sixty-day clock in § 5(b), stands is open. See [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

## Leading case

[[INS v. Chadha]]

- Holds 7–2 that the one-House veto in Immigration and Nationality Act § 244(c)(2) is unconstitutional, and with it every legislative veto of the same design: the House's action was "essentially legislative in purpose and effect" and required bicameral passage and presentment.
- The driving fact is the one the opinion states first: the resolution "was not submitted to the Senate or presented to the President." Passed by both Houses and signed, it would have been an ordinary private law.
- The second driving fact is what the device supplanted — without § 244(c)(2), neither House could have compelled the Attorney General to deport Chadha except by legislation — which is how the Court knows the veto was legislative in character.
- The textual argument runs from Madison's Convention worry that presentment could be evaded "by the simple expedient of calling a proposed law a 'resolution' or 'vote'," which is why cl. 3 exists, and from the four express unicameral powers.
- Powell concurs on a narrower ground the Court declines: "When Congress finds that a particular person does not satisfy the statutory criteria for permanent residence in this country it has assumed a judicial function," and "[t]he House did not enact a general rule." His rationale would leave one-House vetoes of general agency rules intact, which is why White says the Court should have taken it.
- White's dissent is the strongest functional case and the one to have ready: the veto is "an important if not indispensable political invention," without it Congress faces a "Hobson's choice" between impossibly specific laws and abdication, and "[i]f Congress may delegate lawmaking power to independent and Executive agencies, it is most difficult to understand Art. I as prohibiting Congress from also reserving a check on legislative power for itself."
- The sentences worth quoting are the procedural one — "a single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure" — and the closing one: "[W]ith all the obvious flaws of delay, untidiness, and potential for abuse, we have not yet found a better way to preserve freedom than by making the exercise of power subject to the carefully crafted restraints spelled out in the Constitution."

## Best counter-case

[[Clinton v. City of New York]]

- Holds 6–3 that the Line Item Veto Act violates the Presentment Clause, because a cancellation deprives an enacted item of "legal force or effect" and so is a partial repeal by the President alone; it runs [[INS v. Chadha]] in reverse, protecting Congress's place in lawmaking rather than the President's.
- It is the counter-case because every step of the Act's own passage complied with § 7 and the President "meticulously followed" the standards Congress set. Scalia's objection is that presentment "ha[s] been fully complied with" and the Act's title "has succeeded in faking out the Supreme Court."
- The distinguishing facts the majority relies on are two and only two: the cancellation came after enactment, and it reached part of a statute rather than the whole bill the constitutional return addresses.
- Argue the formalist side with the three-step enactment point — House passage, Senate passage of "precisely the same text," presidential signature — and the observation that "[i]f one paragraph of that text had been omitted at any one of those three stages," the law would not have been validly enacted; add Kennedy on why consent does not help: "That a congressional cession of power is voluntary does not make it innocuous," and "one Congress cannot yield up its own powers, much less those of other Congresses to follow."
- Argue the functional side with Breyer's literalism — "To drive exactly 65 miles per hour on an interstate highway closely resembles an act that violates the speed limit. But it does not violate that limit" — and with Scalia's delegation point, that "there is not a dime's worth of difference between Congress's authorizing the President to cancel a spending item, and Congress's authorizing money to be spent on a particular item at the President's discretion."
- The exam wedge is that the holding turns on the Act's drafting: change "cancel" and "legal force or effect" to a discretion to decline to spend and the same practical power survives, which is strong evidence that the Court was protecting something other than the words of § 7 — most likely the quantum of power handed to the President, which is a delegation concern the Court could not reach after the intelligible-principle test went slack.
- [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] supplies the executive-branch version of the same argument from the other direction: a statute cannot "take away, by a mere legislative act, authorities which the President has properly exercised under the Constitution," and "[t]he only way in which the constitutional powers of a branch of the Government can be altered is by amending the Constitution" — which is exactly the majority's own premise in both cases, pointed at Congress instead.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Saying the defect in [[INS v. Chadha]] was that only one House acted. Presentment is an independent requirement, so a two-House concurrent resolution fails too, which is why § 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution — removal of forces "if the Congress so directs by concurrent resolution" — is void, as [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] argued in 1973.
- Running the argument straight off cl. 3. By its terms cl. 3 covers only votes "to which the Concurrence of the Senate and House . . . may be necessary," and a one-House veto requires no concurrence; the textual case depends on first classifying the action as legislative under § 1 and cl. 2, which is where White's original-meaning reply lives.
- Thinking the agreement of both political branches saves a scheme. It saved neither the legislative veto, which Presidents accepted "as the price for a broad delegation of authority," nor the Line Item Veto Act, which Congress enacted and the President signed.
- Overstating [[Clinton v. City of New York]]. The Court did not hold that the President may never decline to spend appropriated funds, and did not hold that the Act delegated too much; it held that a statutory power to cancel enacted provisions violates the Presentment Clause, and it expressly declined to reach delegation.
- Treating Powell's concurrence as the holding. The Court took the presentment route, which reaches every legislative veto; Powell's adjudication rationale would have left one-House vetoes of general agency rules standing.
- Assuming a joint resolution of disapproval restores congressional control. It is a bill, so the President may veto it, and overriding takes two-thirds of each House — which is why Kennedy's leverage objection bites hardest where restoration requires a supermajority.
- Confusing a signing statement with a line-item veto. A signing statement changes nothing in the law and a court remains free to disagree; a cancellation changes the statute's legal effect. See [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] and [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
- Forgetting severability. Chadha kept his suspension only because the veto provision was severable, and the same question decides what survives of the War Powers Resolution after § 5(c) falls.
- Reading the rule to forbid agency lawmaking. Agencies alter rights without § 7 because the Executive presumptively acts executively; a student who takes Chadha's effects test literally has to explain why every rule is not a bill.
- Citing [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]] category one as a cure. Congressional authorization can enlarge the President's executive discretion but cannot give him a share in the § 7 process, which is precisely what [[Clinton v. City of New York]] decides.
