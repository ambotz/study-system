## Rule statement

Judicial review is not judicial supremacy: [[Marbury v. Madison]] holds that a court deciding a case must prefer the Constitution to a conflicting statute, and nothing in that holding makes the Court's exposition binding on officers who were not parties. The operative doctrine is [[Cooper v. Aaron]]'s — Article VI makes the Constitution supreme, Marbury makes "the federal judiciary . . . supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution," and a state officer's Article VI oath therefore forbids him to "war against the Constitution" as the Court has expounded it. Departmentalism answers from the same clause: "Each public officer who takes an oath to support the Constitution swears that he will support it as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others" ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]). The field has three positions rather than two — opinion supremacy ([[Cooper v. Aaron]], Douglas), judgment supremacy (Lincoln in 1858; Baude's *The Judgment Power*), and executive coordinacy reaching judgments themselves ([[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]]; Paulsen's "Merryman Power") — and an answer that collapses them into a fight about whether officials must "obey the Court" has already lost the points.

## Elements

1. **Identify the actor and the capacity in which he acts.** The same officer has different obligations in different capacities, and departmentalism is strongest where the officer exercises a power the Constitution gives him alone.
   - Legislative capacity — vetoing, voting, refusing to appropriate. [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] claims independence only here: "The authority of the Supreme Court must not, therefore, be permitted to control the Congress or the Executive when acting in their legislative capacities."
   - Executive capacity — prosecuting, pardoning, refusing to defend a statute, executing a decree. This is where the claim gets hard, because it is where a judgment operates.
   - State officer under a federal decree — [[Cooper v. Aaron]]'s setting, and the one setting in which the Supremacy Clause supplies a hierarchy.
   - A later court — [[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]'s judge, who refuses to apply the law in the case before him and leaves the statute on the books.
2. **Identify what obedience is demanded: the judgment or the opinion.** "Judicial decisions have two uses—first, to absolutely determine the case decided, and secondly, to indicate to the public how other similar cases will be decided when they arise" ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]).
3. **The judgment binds the parties.** Bates concedes it: a decision "binds only the parties to the case decided." Lincoln concedes it: "We do not propose that when Dred Scott has been decided to be a slave by the court, we, as a mob, will decide him to be free." Only Paulsen's Merryman Power denies it.
4. **The opinion is where the positions split.** [[Cooper v. Aaron]] says the interpretation itself "is the supreme law of the land"; Lincoln says the decision may not be treated as "a political rule, which shall be binding on the members of Congress or the President to favor no measure that does not actually concur with the principles of that decision."
5. **Test jurisdiction before treating non-compliance as defiance.** Baude's middle position — the executive "must obey judicial judgments, as long as the judiciary properly possessed jurisdiction" — reads [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] as a jurisdictional argument: habeas "is in the nature of an appeal," and no "judge, at chambers, can entertain an appeal, in any form, from a decision of the President." Paulsen's reply is that jurisdiction "gives away the game," because whoever decides the jurisdictional question decides everything.
6. **Ask whether the question is settled, and by what.** Madison's conditions are cumulative: repetition, duration, variety of form, concurrence of all three branches, acquiescence of the States, and of the people ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]). Jackson accepts the criterion and disputes the tally — "One Congress, in 1791, decided in favor of a bank; another, in 1811, decided against it." Lincoln's criteria are unanimity, absence of partisan bias, accordance with legal public expectation and the steady practice of the departments, and truthful historical premises, or else reaffirmation "through a course of years."
7. **Fix the axis: nation against State, or branch against branch.** Article VI creates a hierarchy between federal law and state law and binds "the Judges in every State"; among Congress, the President and the Court it creates none. Bates: the departments "are co-ordinate and coequal—that is, neither being sovereign, each is independent in its sphere," and the framers "did not even try . . . to create an arbiter among them."
8. **Name the remedy the departmentalist actually proposes.** Election of officials who will appoint new Justices and present new cases ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]); a "verdict of public opinion" delivered by a new Congress ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]); legislation against the precedent, as in the 1862 territorial slavery ban; and Article V.

## Standard of review / burden

There is no standard of review, because there is no tribunal. The Constitution supplies no arbiter among the three departments, so the question whether the President or Congress must follow the Court's reasoning is answered by practice and by political checks, not by a court: "if we allow one of the three to determine the extent of its own powers, and also the extent of the powers of the other two, that one can control the whole government, and has in fact achieved the sovereignty" ([[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]]). Where the claim is an executive refusal of a judicial order, the political-question route is Bates's: "the whole subject-matter is political and not judicial," and "no court or judge can take cognizance of the political acts of the President"; the check he leaves is Congress alone — "for any breach of that trust he is responsible before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal." Where the claim is against a State, the standard is ordinary supremacy and the burden is on the State: good faith is no defence, and disorder the State's own officers produced is no excuse ([[Cooper v. Aaron]]). Where the disputed clause generates no plaintiff, no court ever reaches it, and the interpretation is made by the political branches by default — [[Ex parte Levitt]] (a citizen and bar member has only "a general interest common to all members of the public") and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] ("We have no power per se to review and annul acts of Congress on the ground that they are unconstitutional"). Enforcement of the whole area falls to impeachment, appointments, elections, Article V, and Congress's control of jurisdiction and Court size ([[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]]).

## Exceptions

- Nullification is outside the doctrine. An officer's independent judgment governs the exercise of his own powers; it does not authorize defying a judgment in a case, and a Governor's "fiat" is not supreme law ([[Cooper v. Aaron]]).
- A question Madison's conditions have liquidated binds the officer against his own reading: "My construction of the Constitution on this point is not changed," yet Madison signed the 1816 charter ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]).
- Liquidation can come unstuck. Forty years of acquiescence in the Bank were withdrawn through the 1832 and 1834 elections, and no bank followed until 1913 ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- Lincoln's version is not pure departmentalism: decisions "when fully settled, should control, not only the particular cases decided, but the general policy of the country, subject to be disturbed only by amendments of the Constitution. More than this would be revolution" ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]).
- The executive branch may act on its own reading of a clause the Court has construed, and the reading may stand because no case arises: [[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]] told the Archivist that [[Dillon v. Gloss]]'s contemporaneity reasoning "is unpersuasive in both its specific arguments and in its broader methodology," and the Twenty-seventh Amendment exists because he acted on it.
- Not decided: whether the President must execute a judgment he believes unconstitutional. [[Ex parte Merryman]] produced no ruling on the point — Taney filed his record and sent it to the President, and his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome."
- Not decided: whether Congress may be required to treat a precedent as binding when it legislates. Congress banned slavery in the territories in 1862 against [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] and seated Hiram Revels in 1870 against its citizenship holding; no court passed on either.

## Leading case

[[Cooper v. Aaron]]

- Holds that a desegregation decree may not be postponed because of violence and disorder "directly traceable to the actions of legislators and executive officials of the State of Arkansas," and that the School Board, however good its faith, "stand[s] in this litigation as the agent[] of the State."
- The fact that drove it: the Governor and Legislature acted on the stated premise "that they are not bound by our holding in Brown," which is what provoked the supremacy passage.
- The supremacy holding: Article VI plus Marbury's "province and duty" line yield the proposition that "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution," so the Court's interpretation "is the supreme law of the land" binding every state officer.
- The oath is the operative text: "No state legislator or executive or judicial officer can war against the Constitution without violating his undertaking to support it."
- By its own account the passage was unnecessary: "What has been said, in the light of the facts developed, is enough to dispose of the case. However, we should answer the premise." It is a deliberate dictum and the only part anyone remembers.
- Signed individually by all nine Justices — a form used nowhere else — with Frankfurter concurring separately all the same.
- The claim that the principle "has ever since been respected by this Court and the Country as a permanent and indispensable feature of our constitutional system" is an argument from practice that Jefferson, Jackson and Lincoln contradict on the pages immediately before it in the casebook.

## Best counter-case

[[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]

- The same Article VI oath, read to the opposite result: the officer swears to support the Constitution "as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others," so "[t]he Congress, the Executive, and the Court must each for itself be guided by its own opinion of the Constitution."
- Parity, not supremacy: "The opinion of the judges has no more authority over Congress than the opinion of Congress has over the judges, and on that point the President is independent of both."
- The distinguishing fact is the capacity and the object of resistance. Arkansas defied a decree binding parties before the Court; Jackson declined to sign a new law. Cooper's rule survives the distinction and Jackson's veto does not offend it — say so, and the two cases stop looking inconsistent.
- Jackson does not need to overrule [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] to win: "in the case relied upon the Supreme Court have not decided that all the features of this corporation are compatible with the Constitution."
- He turns Marshall's deference into presidential authority: because "the 'degree of its necessity' . . . is a question exclusively for legislative consideration," it is "the exclusive province of Congress and the President to decide whether the particular features of this act are necessary and proper."
- Arguing for supremacy: Douglas's consistency point — "I want to know whether he is not bound to a decision which is contrary to his opinions just as much as to one in accordance with his opinions" — and the consequence that without it every State relitigates every right.
- Arguing for departmentalism: a theory of the Court's authority "has to last you through good decisions and bad ones," and supremacy would have entrenched [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]; Hamilton's own premise in [[The Federalist No. 78]] is constitutional supremacy, not judicial supremacy — "It only supposes that the power of the people is superior to both."
- The empirical answer the course supplies: the Bank War vindicated Jackson's position in fact ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]) and the law later adopted Cooper's.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Citing Marbury for Cooper's proposition. Marbury holds that a court must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute in the case before it; the sentence "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" is Cooper's, written in 1958, and appears nowhere in Marbury.
- Treating Cooper's supremacy passage as a holding. The Court says the case was already disposed of; the passage is dictum, and [[Cooper v. Aaron]] could have been written on judgment supremacy alone, since the Board was a party to a decree and the United States held an injunction against the state officials.
- Equating Lincoln with Faubus. Lincoln disclaimed resisting the judgment; Faubus used troops to block a federal decree. A departmentalist who respects judgments condemns Faubus without adopting Cooper.
- Reading Jackson's veto as a licence to disobey a court. The message claims independence only "when acting in their legislative capacities," and nothing in it says he would refuse to execute a decree against the United States.
- Reading [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] as a general power to ignore judgments. Bates's operative argument is jurisdictional and political-question — a judge at chambers cannot entertain "an appeal . . . from a decision of the President" — which is why Baude can read the opinion as consistent with judgment supremacy.
- Saying Taney ordered Merryman released and Lincoln refused. Taney issued a writ and an attachment, both resisted; his final order was only that the record be filed and transmitted to the President. What was disobeyed matters, because the whole question is what the court adjudged.
