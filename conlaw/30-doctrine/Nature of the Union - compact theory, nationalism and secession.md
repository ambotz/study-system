## Rule statement

No State may leave the Union by its own act, because the Constitution rests on the people rather than on the States as parties to a revocable compact. A secession ordinance and the acts giving it effect are "absolutely null" and "utterly without operation in law" ([[Texas v. White]]). Perpetuity runs both ways — "an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States" — and the only lawful exits are "through revolution, or through consent of the States." The rival compact theory holds that "each State is a sovereign, and thus may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever" ([[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate]]). Text cannot settle the contest, because "[u]nderstanding the nature of the Union was a predicate of—not a product of—constitutional interpretation" ([[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union|Campbell]]).

## Elements

1. **Who formed the Union, and who is sovereign in it?** Campbell's four founding-era positions:
   - Compact theory (Jefferson): "no national body politic existed, and the peoples of the several states remained the only true sovereigns."
   - Quasi-nationalism (Madison): neither "a consolidated Government" nor "a Confederated Gov[ernment]" but "a mixture of both," made by "the people in each of the States, acting in their highest sovereign capacity."
   - 1787 nationalism (Marshall): the States were sovereign before ratification and ratified one by one, yet the people "forged themselves together into a unitary whole," so the United States rests on a unitary national body politic.
   - 1776 nationalism (Wilson): "national sovereignty inhered in a national social contract created in 1776," so the nation existed before the States made anything.
2. **What interpretive rule follows from that answer?**
   - Compact theory imports the law-of-nations rule that "a legal instrument could be read to alienate sovereign rights only if it did so in clear and express terms," which yields Jefferson's strict construction in [[The Bank Debate]].
   - Madison's middle view seeks "a reasonable medium" between penal strictness and a laxity that "may vary its essential character."
   - Nationalist premises give the States no presumption, which is how [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] reaches "Let the end be legitimate." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
3. **Does the ratification history defeat the compact claim?** [[McCulloch v. Maryland|McCulloch]] locates the constituent power in the people: they met in their States because "No political dreamer was wild enough to think of breaking down the lines which separate the States," but their act "required not the affirmance, and could not be negatived, by the State governments."
4. **Does the perpetuity argument apply?** [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]], [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln]] and Chase take the same three steps:
   - The Articles declared the Union "perpetual."
   - The Constitution was ordained "to form a more perfect Union."
   - Therefore, "[w]hat can be indissoluble if a perpetual Union, made more perfect, is not?"
5. **Is admission a revocable compact?**
   - Chase: admission "was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final." With no compact, there is nothing for a sovereign principal to revoke.
   - Lincoln's July 4, 1861 message: "Much is said about the 'sovereignty' of the States; but the word, even, is not in the national Constitution," and the Union "created them as States."
6. **If the secession was void, what changed?** Nullity is not stasis: "Obligations often remained unimpaired, while relations are greatly changed." The State's rights as a member and its citizens' rights as citizens were suspended; the State's existence and their citizenship were not.
7. **Is federal force coercion of a State, or execution of law against individuals?**
   - Buchanan frames force as coercion of a State as such and finds no enumerated power for it.
   - Lincoln denies the question arises: void ordinances leave only individuals resisting federal law, which the militia power "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections" reaches.
   - [[The Prize Cases]] and [[Texas v. White]] adopt Lincoln's framing. See [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
8. **Which power restores a defeated State, and which branch holds it?**
   - Suppressing rebellion rests on "the power to suppress insurrection and carry on war."
   - Restoring the State's relations rests on the Guarantee Clause, and that power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," so executive-created governments are "provisional." See [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]].
   - The road not taken (Note 7): if the States had left, the region would be "territory" under Congress's plenary Territories Clause power and would re-enter only by admission under Article IV. See [[Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott]].
9. **Does the Union's structure also govern relations among the States?**
   - [[Corfield v. Coryell]] makes Art. IV § 2 a comity rule of equal treatment in rights "in their nature, fundamental"; the holding upheld New Jersey's residents-only oyster law, and the catalogue of rights is dictum.
   - [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] treats the dormant Commerce Clause antidiscrimination principle as an "effort to mediate competing claims of sovereign authority under our horizontal separation of powers," and a three-Justice plurality invokes the States' equal sovereignty against a market-size rule.
   - Route to [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]] and [[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Posture, not standard:** in [[Texas v. White]] the nature of the Union arose as a jurisdictional predicate. The Court's original jurisdiction extends to suits by a State, so whether Texas was still a State had to be decided before the bonds could be.
- **Method:** Chase declines to argue the merits — "It is needless to discuss, at length, the question whether the right of a State to withdraw from the Union for any cause, regarded by herself as sufficient, is consistent with the Constitution" — and reasons from the history and nature of the Union rather than from any text.
- **Political question (the dissent):** Grier: "Is Texas one of these United States? . . . This is to be decided as a political fact, not as a legal fiction," and "I am not disposed to join in any essay to prove Texas to be a State of the Union, when Congress have decided that she is not."
- **Political question (the dissent on jurisdiction only):** Swayne, with Miller, agrees the question is one "in relation to which this court is bound by the action of the legislative department of the government."
- **Precedent behind the dissent:** [[Luther v. Borden]] (1849) assigned the choice of a State's lawful government to the political branches. Chase discusses Luther and uses it for Congress's primacy over the guarantee, while deciding Texas's status for himself.
- **What fills the gap:** the war. The opinion registers an answer arms had already given, which is why neither Chase nor Davis offers a textual argument.

## Exceptions

- **Consent of the States:** [[Texas v. White]] reserves revocation "through revolution, or through consent of the States." Note 2 sets *expressio unius* (Art. IV § 3 provides a way in and none out) against the principle that the power to undo is incident to the power to do.
- **Revolution:** the right of resistance "exists independently of all constitutions," but "Secession is neither more nor less than revolution. It may or it may not be a justifiable revolution, but still it is revolution" ([[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]]).
- **Indestructible States:** "the perpetuity and indissolubility of the Union, by no means implies the loss of distinct and individual existence, or of the right of self-government by the States," and "without the States in union, there could be no such political body as the United States." See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- **Ordinary acts of a rebel government:** "absolutely null" cannot mean every marriage, deed, judgment and tax from 1861 to 1865. Chase voids the ordinance "and all the acts of her legislature intended to give effect to that ordinance," and *Horn v. Lockhart* (1873) later saved acts that neither furthered the rebellion nor impaired loyal citizens' rights.
- **Self-division:** Texas's admission permits division into as many as five States, terms never expressly repealed, so the "indestructible" State may be divisible on its own terms (Note 1; Kesavan and Paulsen, *Let's Mess with Texas*).
- **Not decided: release by Congress.** Whether the power to admit implies a power to let a State go.
- **Not decided: the expelled State.** Whether a State denied its senators, protection against invasion and its citizens' privileges and immunities could leave on a material-breach theory; Lincoln's arguments do not reach that case cleanly (Lincoln Note 4).
- **Not decided: the referendum.** Whether a clear majority obliges the Union to negotiate separation, as the Supreme Court of Canada held for Quebec in *Reference re Secession of Quebec* (1998) (Note 3).

## Leading case

[[Texas v. White]]

- **Holding:** (5–3; Chase) the secession ordinance and implementing acts "were absolutely null," so Texas "continued to be a State, and a State of the Union," her citizens remained citizens, and the insurgent government's sale of the bonds did not divest the State's title.
- **Driving fact:** the posture. A suit over United States bonds produced the most famous statement of the nature of the Union, as a ruling on whether a party could sue.
- **Concession:** Chase accepts the facts of secession in full — "the relations of Texas to the Union were broken up" so far as ordinances, legislation and votes could do it — and answers that the acts had no legal operation.
- **Reasoning:** the consequence argument does the work. On the contrary view "the State must have become foreign, and her citizens foreigners. The war must have ceased to be a war for the suppression of rebellion, and must have become a war for conquest and subjugation."
- **Quote:** "The Constitution, in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States." Also: admission was "something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final."
- **Guarantee Clause holding:** presidential governments in occupied districts are war measures and therefore provisional; "the power to carry into effect the clause of guaranty is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," with means tested as in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].
- **Separate opinions:** Grier (dissenting) notes Texas has no senators, no voice in the presidential election, and is a "rebel State" in the fifth military district, and asks "Wherein does the condition of Texas differ" from Dakota or the Indian tribes. On the merits he mocks the second fiction: Texas "now sets up the plea of insanity." Swayne, with Miller, joins Grier on jurisdiction only and the majority on the merits.

## Best counter-case

[[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate]]

- **Distinguishing line:** A secession claim resting on each State's sovereign power to revoke its grants for any reason fails under Texas v. White and Lincoln's contract argument; a claim resting on the other States' material breach of an express right survives those answers and meets only the perpetuity argument.
- **Premise:** secession is "an essential attribute of State sovereignty," not a remedy for a particular wrong, so Davis would have been bound by Mississippi's decision even "without sufficient provocation, or without an existing necessity."
- **Agency metaphor:** "each State is a sovereign, and thus may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever." A principal may revoke a delegation, and Davis cites no clause of the Constitution at all.
- **Nullification distinguished:** "Nullification and secession, so often confounded, are indeed antagonistic principles." Nullification is "a remedy which it is sought to apply within the Union, and against the agent of the States," which disarms Jackson's Force Bill as a precedent for coercion.
- **No-coercion argument:** stronger than [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]]'s because it meets Lincoln directly — "there are no laws of the United States to be executed within the limits of a seceded State," so what remains is war: "You may make war on a foreign State." The argument is parasitic, since it holds only if the secession was already valid.
- **Argue for Davis:** compact theory was the premise of Madison's and Jefferson's Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions of 1798, "sovereignty" attached to the States in ordinary founding-era usage, and Campbell's thesis is that no method settles federalism without first choosing a theory of the Union.
- **Argue against Davis:** [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln]]'s contract argument concedes the compact premise and still wins — "One party to a contract may violate it—break it, so to speak—but does it not require all to lawfully rescind it?"
- **Moderates versus radicals (Lincoln Note 4):** Davis is the radical position (secession "for whatever reason"); the moderates claimed a right to leave over a gross constitutional violation, namely Republican refusal to honor [[Dred Scott v. Sandford|Dred Scott]]'s holding on slaveholders' rights in the territories; Benjamin conceded that right "is not put down in the Constitution in quite so clear terms as the right to have two Senators." See [[Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott]].
- **Davis's ending:** "we but tread in the path of our fathers when we proclaim our independence, and take the hazard" is a revolutionary claim, which is what Buchanan said secession was all along.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Texas v. White is a purely nationalist case."** The "indestructible States" half is quoted more often for the States than against them, and it underlies [[Printz v. United States]] and the dissents in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]: the States' separate existence, not just their reserved powers, is constitutionally protected. The phrase confers no power on a State, however: the same opinion suspends Texas's membership rights and gives restoration to Congress.
- **"'Absolutely null' voids everything the rebel government did."** Applied literally it voids marriages, deeds, judgments and tax collections; the workable rule is *Horn v. Lockhart*'s, and a bona fide purchaser of bearer bonds has no place in Chase's theory.
- **"Buchanan, Lincoln and Davis are answering the same question."** [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] holds secession unlawful *and* coercion unlawful, coherent only if the sole response is war on a State as such. Lincoln never answers the coercion question; he denies it arises, which works only because the seceding States seized federal property. Had South Carolina let the customs house operate and the mails run, Lincoln would have had nothing to execute.
- **"Lincoln's four arguments are interchangeable."** Perpetuity alone proves too much, since it forbids dissolution even with every State's consent; the anarchy argument fails where no minority overrides a majority; the contract argument cuts against him after a material breach; and the July 4 Guarantee Clause argument assumes its conclusion, since a State lawfully out is not a State "in this Union."
- **"Compact theory, nullification and secession are one doctrine."** Davis insists nullification and secession are "antagonistic principles." The better counter is that both rest on each State's authority to judge the compact for itself, a premise with no stopping point once the State concludes the others will not be bound.
- **"Madison was always on one side."** [[The Federalist No. 10]] argues that the extended republic protects rights better than the States, where faction does its worst work; four years later in [[The Bank Debate]] Madison reads enumeration as "a grant of particular powers only, leaving the general mass in other hands." Cite the right Madison, and note that [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] keeps the States in their "proper orbit" rather than beneath the Union.
