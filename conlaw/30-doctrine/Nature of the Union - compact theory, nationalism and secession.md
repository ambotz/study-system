## Rule statement

The Constitution derives its authority from the people rather than from the States as sovereigns, so the Union is perpetual and no State can leave it by its own act: an ordinance of secession and the acts giving it effect are "absolutely null" and "utterly without operation in law" ([[Texas v. White]]). Perpetuity runs both ways, because the same Constitution reserves the States' powers and depends on their existence — "an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States" — and the only lawful exits are "through revolution, or through consent of the States." The competing account is compact theory: that the Constitution is an agreement among sovereign States, each of which "may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever" ([[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate]]). [[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]] supplies the organizing claim for the whole field, that "[u]nderstanding the nature of the Union was a predicate of—not a product of—constitutional interpretation."

## Elements

1. Fix the constituent power first, because the theory of sovereignty drives the interpretive rule rather than following from it. The four founding-era positions, in Campbell's scheme:
   - Compact theory (Jefferson): "no national body politic existed, and the peoples of the several states remained the only true sovereigns," so the instrument is read like an agreement among sovereigns under the law of nations.
   - Quasi-nationalism (Madison): neither "a consolidated Government" nor "a Confederated Gov[ernment]" but "a mixture of both," made by "the people in each of the States, acting in their highest sovereign capacity," and construed by "a reasonable medium" between penal strictness and laxity.
   - 1787 nationalism (Marshall): the States were sovereign before ratification and ratified state by state, yet the people "forged themselves together into a unitary whole," so the United States is a sovereign nation resting on a unitary national body politic.
   - 1776 nationalism (Wilson): "national sovereignty inhered in a national social contract created in 1776," so the nation was a freestanding entity before the States made anything.
2. Derive the interpretive consequence. On compact theory the law-of-nations rule applies and "a legal instrument could be read to alienate sovereign rights only if it did so in clear and express terms," which yields Jefferson's strict construction; on nationalist premises there is no presumption for the States, which is how [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] reaches "Let the end be legitimate." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
3. Test the ratification argument. [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] answers Maryland's compact theory by locating the constituent power in the people: they assembled in their States because "No political dreamer was wild enough to think of breaking down the lines which separate the States," but their act "required not the affirmance, and could not be negatived, by the State governments."
4. Run the perpetuity argument in its three steps, which [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]], [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]] and [[Texas v. White]] all take in the same order: the Articles declared the Union "perpetual"; the Constitution was ordained "to form a more perfect Union"; therefore "[w]hat can be indissoluble if a perpetual Union, made more perfect, is not?"
5. Answer the compact premise at its root. Admission "was something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final." If the Union is not a compact, there is nothing for a sovereign principal to revoke.
6. Separate nullity from stasis. Secession is legally void, but rebellion changes relations: "Obligations often remained unimpaired, while relations are greatly changed," so the State's rights as a member and its citizens' rights as citizens are suspended while the State's existence and their citizenship are not.
7. Characterize federal force. Buchanan frames it as coercion of a State as such and finds no enumerated power for it; Lincoln denies that the question arises, because void ordinances leave nothing but individuals resisting federal law, which the militia power "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections" reaches. [[The Prize Cases]] and [[Texas v. White]] both adopt Lincoln's framing. See [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
8. Allocate the restoration power. Suppressing rebellion rests on "the power to suppress insurrection and carry on war"; re-establishing a State's constitutional relations rests on the Guarantee Clause, and that power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," so executive-created governments are "provisional." See [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]].
9. Do not stop at the vertical axis. The Union's structure also fixes the States' relations with each other: [[Corfield v. Coryell]] makes Art. IV § 2 a comity rule securing equal treatment in rights that are "in their nature, fundamental," and [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] treats the dormant Commerce Clause antidiscrimination principle as mediating "competing claims of sovereign authority under our horizontal separation of powers," bounded by "the Constitution's 'fundamental principle of equal sovereignty among the States.'" See [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]] and [[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]].

## Standard of review / burden

There is no standard of review, because in [[Texas v. White]] the nature of the Union came up as a jurisdictional predicate rather than as a claim: the Court's original jurisdiction extends to suits by a State, so whether Texas was still a State had to be decided before the bonds could be. Chase declines even to argue the merits at length — "It is needless to discuss, at length, the question whether the right of a State to withdraw from the Union for any cause, regarded by herself as sufficient, is consistent with the Constitution" — and reasons instead from the history and nature of the Union, which means the holding rests on structure and inference rather than on any text. Grier's dissent supplies the political-question route and is the answer to give when asked who decides: "Is Texas one of these United States? . . . This is to be decided as a political fact, not as a legal fiction," and "I am not disposed to join in any essay to prove Texas to be a State of the Union, when Congress have decided that she is not"; Swayne and Miller agree that the question is one "in relation to which this court is bound by the action of the legislative department of the government." Luther v. Borden (1849) is the precedent behind that position, holding that which government is a State's lawful government belongs to the political branches, and Chase relies on it for Congress's primacy over Reconstruction while deciding Texas's status for himself. The honest account, and the one worth saying on an exam, is that the burden was carried by the war: the opinion registers an answer that had already been given, which is why neither Chase nor Davis offers a textual argument.

## Exceptions

- Consent of the States: [[Texas v. White]] expressly reserves revocation "through revolution, or through consent of the States," so a negotiated exit is undecided, and Note 2 sets the two arguments — *expressio unius* from Art. IV § 3, which provides a way in and none out, against the principle that the power to undo is incident to the power to do.
- Revolution: the right of resistance, in Buchanan's words, "exists independently of all constitutions" and is written into the Declaration of Independence; "Secession is neither more nor less than revolution. It may or it may not be a justifiable revolution, but still it is revolution."
- The reserved half of indestructibility: "the perpetuity and indissolubility of the Union, by no means implies the loss of distinct and individual existence, or of the right of self-government by the States," and "without the States in union, there could be no such political body as the United States." See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- Ordinary acts of a rebel government survive. "[A]bsolutely null" cannot mean what it says, or no marriage, deed, judgment or tax from 1861 to 1865 would stand; Chase's own rule voids the ordinance "and all the acts of her legislature intended to give effect to that ordinance," and Horn v. Lockhart (1873) drew the line at acts furthering the rebellion or impairing loyal citizens' rights.
- The State that may divide itself: Texas's 1845 admission permitted division into as many as five States, so the "indestructible" State may be destructible on its own terms (Kesavan and Paulsen, *Let's Mess with Texas*).
- Not decided: whether Congress's power to admit implies a power to release; whether a State expelled from the benefits of the Union — denied its senators, denied protection against invasion, denied its citizens privileges and immunities — could leave on a material-breach theory, which is the one case Lincoln's four arguments do not reach cleanly; and whether a clear referendum majority obliges the federation to negotiate separation, as the Canadian Supreme Court held in Reference re Secession of Quebec (1998).

## Leading case

[[Texas v. White]]

- Holds that the ordinance of secession and the implementing acts "were absolutely null," so Texas "continued to be a State, and a State of the Union," her citizens remained citizens, and the insurgent government's sale of the bonds did not divest the State's title.
- The fact that drove it is the posture: this is a suit over United States bonds, and the most famous statement of the nature of the Union is a ruling on whether a party could sue. Say that out loud before anything else.
- Chase accepts the factual premise entirely — "In all respects, so far as the object [of secession] could be accomplished by ordinances of the convention, by acts of the legislature, and by votes of the citizens, the relations of Texas to the Union were broken up" — and answers that the acts had no legal operation whatever.
- The consequence argument does the real work: on the contrary view "the State must have become foreign, and her citizens foreigners. The war must have ceased to be a war for the suppression of rebellion, and must have become a war for conquest and subjugation."
- The sentence to quote: "The Constitution, in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States." The second sentence to have ready: admission was "something more than a compact; it was the incorporation of a new member into the political body. And it was final."
- The Guarantee Clause holding is the case's other legacy and the one that decides Reconstruction: presidential governments in occupied districts are war measures and therefore provisional, while "the power to carry into effect the clause of guaranty is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," with means tested as in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]].
- Grier's dissent, with Swayne and Miller concurring on jurisdiction, is the best-argued part of the case: Texas has no senators, took no part in the presidential election, and is a "rebel State" in the fifth military district under the Act of March 2, 1867 — "Wherein does the condition of Texas differ" from Dakota or the Indian tribes? And on the merits, "[h]aving relied upon one fiction . . . she now relies upon a second one . . . She now sets up the plea of insanity."

## Best counter-case

[[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate]]

- The premise is that secession is "an essential attribute of State sovereignty," not a remedy for a particular wrong, and everything else follows: allegiance runs to the State, so Davis would have been bound by Mississippi's decision even "without sufficient provocation, or without an existing necessity."
- The distinguishing move is the agency metaphor: "each State is a sovereign, and thus may reclaim the grants which it has made to any agent whomsoever." A principal may revoke a delegation, and there is no text in the Constitution forbidding it — Davis cites no clause at all.
- Davis separates his position from nullification, which is the step that disarms Jackson's Force Bill precedent: "Nullification and secession, so often confounded, are indeed antagonistic principles," because nullification is "a remedy which it is sought to apply within the Union, and against the agent of the States," while secession begins where nullification has failed.
- His no-coercion argument is stronger than [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]]'s because it meets Lincoln head-on: "there are no laws of the United States to be executed within the limits of a seceded State," so what the Union proposes is war, not enforcement — "You may make war on a foreign State."
- Arguing for Davis: the compact theory was the orthodox reading in 1798, the word "sovereignty" attaches to the States in ordinary founding-era usage, and Campbell's own point is that no interpretive method settles federalism without first choosing a theory of the Union, which text alone cannot do.
- Arguing against Davis: the answer is [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]]'s contract argument, which concedes the compact premise and still wins — "One party to a contract may violate it—break it, so to speak—but does it not require all to lawfully rescind it?" — plus Lincoln's textual point that "the word, even, is not in the national Constitution," and the fact that Davis's argument is parasitic, since it holds only if the secession was already valid.
- The exam-useful distinction inside the Southern case: [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]]'s Note 4 separates "moderate" secessionists, who justified exit as a response to an egregious violation of the South's constitutional rights, from radicals who held that States could secede for any reason. Davis is the radical, and the moderate argument survives the refutation of state sovereignty while his does not.
- Davis's own ending concedes the point: "we but tread in the path of our fathers when we proclaim our independence, and take the hazard" is a revolutionary claim, which is what Buchanan said secession was all along.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Treating [[Texas v. White]] as a nationalist case only. The "indestructible States" half is quoted far more often for the States than against them, and it is the premise behind [[Printz v. United States]] and the dissents in [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]: the States' separate existence, not just their reserved powers, is constitutionally protected.
- Taking "absolutely null" literally. Applied to every act of a rebel legislature it voids marriages, deeds, judgments and tax collections; the workable rule is Horn v. Lockhart's, and the gap between Chase's language and that rule is the opinion's weakest seam. A bona fide purchaser of bearer bonds has no place in Chase's theory at all.
- Collapsing Buchanan into Davis. [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]] holds that secession is unlawful *and* that coercion is unlawful, a position coherent only on the assumption that the only available response is war against a State as such; Davis holds that secession is lawful, which is a different argument with a different weakness.
- Saying Lincoln answered Buchanan's coercion question. He never does; he denies that it arises, because void ordinances leave only individuals to be dealt with — and that reframing works only because the seceding States seized federal property. If South Carolina had let the customs house operate and the mails run, Lincoln's program would have had nothing to execute.
- Reading Lincoln's arguments as interchangeable. The perpetuity argument proves too much if taken alone, since it would forbid dissolution even by consent of all the States; the anarchy argument fails where no minority is overriding a majority; the contract argument cuts against him where the other parties have materially breached; and the Guarantee Clause argument in the July 4 message assumes its conclusion, since a State lawfully out of the Union is not a State "in this Union."
- Using compact theory and nullification as the same thing. Davis insists they are "antagonistic principles," and the counter is not that they are identical but that both rest on each State's authority to judge the compact for itself, a premise with no stopping point once the State concludes the others will not be bound.
- Mistaking the Article IV cases for secession cases. [[Corfield v. Coryell]] holds only that a State's common property lies outside Art. IV § 2 — the famous catalogue of fundamental rights is dictum in a case that upheld a discriminatory law — and [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] is a pleading decision about the interstate market, not a ruling on state sovereignty.
- Forgetting that the same Madison sits on both sides. [[The Federalist No. 10]] argues that the extended republic is safer for rights than the States, where faction does its worst work; [[The Bank Debate]] has him arguing four years later that enumeration is "a grant of particular powers only, leaving the general mass in other hands." Cite the right Madison for the point you are making, and note that [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] describes the same Union as an orbit rather than a hierarchy.
