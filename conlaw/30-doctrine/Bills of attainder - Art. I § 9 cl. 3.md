## Rule statement

Congress may not single out particular people and punish them by statute instead of leaving guilt and punishment to a court. The Clause reaches "legislative acts, no matter what their form, that apply either to named individuals or to easily ascertainable members of a group in such a way as to inflict punishment on them without a judicial trial" ([[United States v. Lovett]], building on *Cummings v. Missouri*). Form does not matter, so a pay cutoff that permanently bars named employees from government service is an attainder. The contest is Frankfurter's concurrence: the Clause is "defined by history," a historical bill of attainder specified an offense and declared guilt, and § 304 did neither on its face.

## Elements

1. **Is the measure a legislative act?**
   - "A bill of attainder is a legislative act which inflicts punishment without a judicial trial" (*Cummings*, quoted in Lovett).
   - The Clause reaches what Congress enacts, not what a court adjudges or an executive officer decides.
   - The vice is "the substitution of legislative determination of guilt and legislative imposition of punishment for judicial finding and sentence" (Frankfurter).
2. **Does it apply to named individuals or to easily ascertainable members of a group?**
   - Section 304 named Lovett, Watson and Dodd.
   - A general rider barring pay to anyone belonging to an organization advocating overthrow of the government had become standard in appropriations acts and was not challenged.
   - A measure that defunds an office rather than naming its holder is probably not specific, though Lovett's purpose-and-effect reading invites the question whether the office was abolished to remove the person.
3. **Does it inflict punishment?**
   - Permanent exclusion from government service, which "operates as a legislative decree of perpetual exclusion" from a chosen vocation, "is punishment, and of a most severe type."
   - Exclusion from a profession by loyalty oath was punishment in *Cummings v. Missouri* and *Ex parte Garland* (1867), neither of which has been overruled.
   - A disqualification for non-retributive reasons is not punishment: "A man may be forbidden to practice medicine because he has been convicted of a felony, or because he is no longer qualified" (Frankfurter).
   - Congress had otherwise imposed permanent exclusion from office only for "odious and dangerous crimes, such as treason, acceptance of bribes," and election interference by military officers, which marks the sanction as punitive.
   - A later case outside the casebook excerpt, *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services* (1977), asks whether the burden is punitive in history, function and motive.
4. **Was the punishment imposed without a judicial trial?**
   - In Lovett the substitute was a House Appropriations subcommittee that held secret hearings, excluded lawyers, described its work as "in the nature of an inquest of office," and found each respondent guilty of "subversive activity" and "unfit" for government employment.
5. **Does the form of the measure disguise the punishment?**
   - An appropriations rider or pay cutoff is judged by purpose and effect; § 304's purpose "was not merely to cut off respondents' compensation through regular disbursing channels but permanently to bar them from government service."
   - Punishment "through the instrumentality of an Act specifically cutting off the pay of certain named individuals" is "no less galling or effective than if it had been done by an Act which designated the conduct as criminal."
   - Where the text is silent, the Court reads the statute "in light of the circumstances leading to its passage," and rejects a narrower reading that "would completely frustrate the purpose of all who sponsored Section 304."

## Standard of review / burden

- **Standard:** No tier of scrutiny. The Clause is a flat prohibition, "a use of power which the Constitution unequivocally declares Congress can never exercise," and the only questions are specificity and punishment.
- **Burden:** The challenger must prove punishment, and the legislative record can carry that burden: the Dies speech attacking thirty-nine named employees, the subcommittee's "inquest," and its findings of guilt supplied the determination of guilt the text omitted.
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Congress's control of appropriations does not make the question political. Otherwise "congressional action, aimed at three named individuals, which stigmatized their reputation and seriously impaired their chance to earn a living, could never be challenged in any court," and "[o]ur Constitution did not contemplate such a result."
- **What fills the gap:** The case reaches court as an ordinary claim for pay for work performed, which the Court of Claims exists to decide; the agencies kept the three at work after their pay stopped.
- **What fills the gap:** The competing discipline is avoidance. For Frankfurter "the most fundamental principle of constitutional adjudication is not to face constitutional questions but to avoid them, if at all possible," and reading § 304 as a stoppage of disbursement that left the government's obligation to pay intact would have produced the same judgment.
- **Standard:** The Clause binds Congress whatever the President thinks. Roosevelt signed § 304 while calling it "not only unwise and discriminatory, but unconstitutional," and the Solicitor General joined the respondents on attainder and removal, so Congress authorized special counsel to defend the rider.

## Exceptions

- **General rules defined by conduct or membership.** A rider barring pay to members of organizations advocating overthrow of the government is not an attainder, because it names no one and rests on a class defined by conduct.
- **Regulatory or qualifying burdens.** Disqualification for want of qualification, or after conviction at trial, is not punishment in the attainder sense (Frankfurter's medicine example).
- **Punishment after judicial trial.** The Clause channels punishment to the courts; exclusion from office imposed on conviction for treason or bribery is the model Lovett contrasts with § 304.
- **Ordinary appropriations choices.** Congress may generally refuse to fund operations it disagrees with, because no money leaves the Treasury without a law; the limits come from other provisions, including this one. See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **Defunding an office rather than a person.** Section 2262 of the 2011 appropriations act defunded several positions advising the President, and the objection raised against it was separation of powers, not attainder ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
- **Naming one person without punishing.** Specificity is necessary but not sufficient; the statute aimed at one former President's papers was upheld in *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services*, a case outside the casebook excerpt.
- **Not decided: removal and due process.** Lovett rested on attainder alone and did not reach whether § 304 usurped the President's removal power or denied due process; Frankfurter notes that even a mere stoppage of disbursement raises the question "whether Congress can treat three employees of the Government differently from all others." See [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].
- **Not decided: a private bill against a named alien.** The Chadha majority says the House's result "could have been achieved, if at all, only by legislation requiring deportation," and Powell's concurrence treats Congress's deciding the case of six named people as a "judicial function"; the words "if at all" leave room for an attainder objection to such a bill ([[INS v. Chadha]]; [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]]).
- **Not resolved: the original meaning.** Whether Frankfurter's historical test (a specified offense plus a declaration of guilt) or Black's purpose-and-effect test states the Clause's meaning is open; the Court answered Frankfurter with *Cummings* and *Garland*, not with history.

## Leading case

[[United States v. Lovett]]

- **Holding:** Section 304 of the Urgent Deficiency Appropriation Act of 1943, cutting off the pay of three named federal employees unless the President reappointed them with the Senate's consent, is a bill of attainder; the Court affirmed the Court of Claims judgments for the three.
- **Votes:** Black wrote for the Court; Frankfurter, joined by Reed, concurred only in the judgment on the narrow statutory reading; Jackson took no part; there was no dissent.
- **Driving fact:** The legislative trial behind the rider: secret subcommittee hearings, counsel excluded, an "inquest of office," a rejected "strong appeal" from the Secretary of the Interior to keep Lovett, and findings of "subversive activity" and unfitness.
- **Driving fact:** The Senate voted 69 to 0 against § 304 and yielded only because the House refused to pass any appropriation without it, which shows how one chamber can force a measure through a must-pass bill.
- **Reasoning (Black):** The rider is not "a mere stoppage of disbursing routine"; it was "designed to force the employing agencies to discharge respondents and to bar their being hired by any other governmental agency."
- **Separate opinions (Frankfurter):** Specific clauses "were defined by history," and every historical bill of attainder specified an offense and declared guilt; quoting Holmes, "legislatures are ultimate guardians of the liberties and welfare of the people in quite as great a degree as the courts."
- **Quote:** An earlier amendment against all thirty-nine employees Dies had attacked drew the charge of "legislative lynching," and the enacted rider "operates as a legislative decree of perpetual exclusion" from a chosen vocation.

## Best counter-case

[[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]

- **Distinguishing line:** A funding bar aimed at positions, with no legislative finding of guilt, raises a separation-of-powers question rather than an attainder question; a funding bar aimed at named people on a record of legislative findings of disloyalty is an attainder.
- **The collision:** Congress used an appropriations rider against the executive in 2011, as in 1943, but no one claimed attainder, which tests how much work the Clause actually does.
- **Driving fact:** Section 2262 withheld funds for several positions advising the President directly, not for identified individuals, and carried no finding of guilt or unfitness, so Obama objected under the President's "well-established authority to supervise and oversee the executive branch, and to obtain advice."
- **Argue for attainder anyway:** If a position is defunded in order to remove the person holding it, [[United States v. Lovett]] looks at purpose and effect rather than form, and a legislative record could supply the same proof the subcommittee's findings supplied in 1943.
- **Argue against attainder:** A funding denial is the ordinary operation of the Appropriations Clause, no one has a constitutional right to be paid from the Treasury, and on Frankfurter's historical test there is no specified offense and no declaration of guilt.
- **Remedial asymmetry:** Obama announced that "the executive branch will construe section 2262 not to abrogate these Presidential prerogatives," which is category three in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]], and even a void restriction creates no appropriation. Roosevelt's path in Lovett was different: sign, object, comply, and let the injured employees sue. See [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]] and [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Looking for a crime.** The Clause is not limited to criminal punishment; § 304 designated no conduct as criminal and was still an attainder, because permanent exclusion from a vocation is punishment.
- **"It is only an appropriation, so it is Congress's call."** "[N]o matter what their form" is the operative phrase, and the Court expressly rejected the argument that a pay cutoff is an unreviewable political matter.
- **"Any statute that names a person is an attainder."** Specificity is necessary but not sufficient; there must also be punishment, imposed without a judicial trial.
- **Ignoring Frankfurter's historical objection.** A bill of attainder classically specified an offense and declared guilt; § 304 did neither on its face, and Black answered with precedent (*Cummings* and *Garland*), not history. Say which side has the better original-meaning argument and why.
- **"Lovett holds that Congress may not defund executive officers."** The removal claim was argued and not reached; the holding binds Congress through Art. I, § 9, cl. 3 whatever the President's removal power turns out to be.
- **Assuming the Court will always look behind the text.** The Court did so because the punitive purpose was documented in committee findings; a budget cut eliminating the same three jobs with no findings of disloyalty lacks the retributive element, and the result likely changes.
