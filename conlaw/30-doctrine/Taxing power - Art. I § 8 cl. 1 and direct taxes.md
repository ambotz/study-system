## Rule statement

The taxing power is "the first power listed in the Article I, Section 8 enumeration" and is an independent power: the Court "has held that the Taxing Clause power is an independent power that can be used to achieve goals not attainable under the other enumerated powers," so Congress may tax what it could not regulate. Two express rules govern the form of a tax — direct taxes must be apportioned among the States by population, and "Duties, Imposts and Excises" must be uniform — and because a uniform tax and an apportioned tax are "mutually inconsistent" for anything distributed unevenly relative to population, classification decides everything. "Direct" is read functionally and narrowly: apportionment "is only to be adopted in such cases where it can reasonably apply," which confines the category to capitations and taxes on land ([[Hylton v. United States]]), with income taxes removed from the rule by the Sixteenth Amendment. The implied limit is that an exaction which is really a penalty must be supported by some other enumerated power, and whether it is a penalty is answered "on the very face of its provisions" ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]) through factors — size, scienter, enforcing agency — that [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] applies functionally and in the opposite direction. The contest is whether the tax/penalty line has content at all: [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] is the only case in the line to strike a tax down and has never been enforced since, and the casebook's alternative reading is that United States v. Kahriger proves the Child Labor Tax Case "couldn't come up with an administrable line between taxes and penalties."

## Elements

1. **Ask whether the exaction is a "tax" for constitutional purposes, which is a functional question and not a matter of the label.** Congress's word controls statutory questions such as the Anti-Injunction Act — "It is up to Congress whether to apply the Anti-Injunction Act to any particular statute" — but its choice "does not, however, control whether an exaction is within Congress's constitutional power to tax."
   - Does it produce revenue? Revenue is "the essential feature of any tax," and a tax survives even where "the intent to curtail and hinder, as well as tax," is manifest and "the revenue obtained is negligible."
   - Is the amount prohibitory or merely onerous? An exaction that "will be far less than the price of insurance, and, by statute, . . . can never be more" leaves "a reasonable financial decision"; 10 percent of an employer's entire net profits, owed the same "whether he used five hundred children for a year or one child for a day," is prohibitory.
   - Is there a scienter requirement? Scienter is "associated with penalties, not with taxes."
   - Who collects it? Collection "solely by the IRS through the normal means of taxation" points to a tax; enforcement by an agency "whose normal function is the advancement and protection of the welfare of the workers" points to a penalty.
   - Does the statute declare the taxed conduct unlawful? A penalty is "punishment for an unlawful act or omission," and Congress "did not think it was creating four million outlaws."
   - Does the statute set out "a detailed and specified course of conduct in business" and exact a heavy payment for departing from it? That combination is what converts a tax into a penalty, and weight alone is not the test.
2. **If it is a penalty, find an independent enumerated power to support it.** The independence of the taxing power "matters only where the same sovereign holds both powers," so where the regulated subject belongs to the States the label is the question rather than the answer; see [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
3. **If it is a tax, classify it, because the form rules follow from the class.** Paterson's three classes: direct taxes; "Duties, imposts, and excises"; and "All other classes of an indirect kind, and not within any of the classifications enumerated under the preceding heads."
4. **Test directness by whether apportionment can sensibly be applied.** "As all direct taxes must be apportioned, it is evident that the Constitution contemplated none as direct but such as could be apportioned. If this cannot be apportioned, it is, therefore, not a direct tax in the sense of the Constitution."
5. **If direct, apportion: each State's share must track its census population,** which in practice only a capitation satisfies together with uniformity, since a head tax has the same rate everywhere and yields rate times population in each State.
6. **If a duty, impost or excise, apply uniformity: "[a] uniform tax has the same rate in every state."**
7. **Check the Sixteenth Amendment, but only for what it covers.** It exempts taxes "on incomes, from whatever source derived" and nothing else, so a tax on wealth is not reached by it and the [[Hylton v. United States]] question decides whether it must be apportioned.
8. **Check the Export Clause,** which bars taxes on articles exported from any State.
9. **Run the federalism check in both directions.** Congress may tax state banks because that is "the action of the whole on a part," while a State may not tax the Bank of the United States because that is "the action of a part on the whole"; and a State has "no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).

## Standard of review / burden

- The presumption runs strongly for the statute, and Bailey says so while refusing to apply it: "Out of a proper respect for the acts of a co-ordinate branch of the government, this court has gone far to sustain taxing acts as such, even though there has been ground for suspecting, from the weight of the tax, it was intended to destroy its subject. But in the act before us the presumption of validity cannot prevail, because the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions."
- Motive is not reviewable as such. McCray v. United States refused to examine the oleomargarine excise's obvious purpose, because reviewing motive would mean "the abuse by one department of the government of its lawful powers is to be corrected by the abuse of its powers by another department," and the remedy "lies, not in the abuse by the judicial authority of its functions, but in the people." Taft does not abandon that rule; he relocates the inquiry to the statute's text, which is the move every tax/penalty argument has to make or defeat.
- The weight of a tax is for Congress. Veazie Bank v. Fenno upheld a tax on state bank notes heavy enough to destroy them, because "the responsibility of the Legislature is not to the courts, but to the people by whom its members are elected" — while adding the dictum that there are "certain virtual limitations, arising from the principles of the Constitution itself," which is the only textual foothold [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] ever had.
- Where a statute has two readings, one unconstitutional, the Court takes the other if it is "fairly possible," not if it is the most natural. [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] concedes that "[t]he most straightforward reading of the mandate is that it commands individuals to purchase insurance" and upholds it as a tax anyway, which means the saving construction — not the tax analysis — is doing the decisive work.
- On classification, if the question is doubtful the Court defers to "[t]he deliberate decision of the National Legislature," though Chase in [[Hylton v. United States]] decides the carriage tax is not doubtful and offers his two-item list of direct taxes expressly as a view on which he does "not give a judicial opinion."
- There is no standard on "general Welfare": courts "should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress," which is why the real fighting over regulatory taxes happens in the tax/penalty inquiry and, for conditional grants, under [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
- The structural limit that substitutes for a standard is that a tax can only take money: it "is limited to requiring an individual to pay money into the Federal Treasury, no more," and it "leaves an individual with a lawful choice to do or not do a certain act."

## Exceptions

- **The Sixteenth Amendment removes income taxes from apportionment and nothing else.** A wealth tax, a tax on unrealized gain, or any levy that is not "on incomes" still faces the Hylton question.
- **The Export Clause is an absolute bar,** unlike apportionment and uniformity, which are rules of form.
- **The penalty limit of [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]].** It has never been overruled, and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] preserves it as the outer bound by quoting through Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch that a tax may become "a mere penalty with the characteristics of regulation and punishment."
- **A federal tax confers no license.** The License Tax Cases upheld federal fees on intrastate lottery and liquor businesses Congress admittedly could not regulate, but held that "Congress cannot authorize a trade or business within a State in order to tax it"; the license implies "nothing except that the licensee shall be subject to no penalties under national law, if he pays it."
- **Intergovernmental immunity, with its own carve-outs.** [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] bars a State from taxing the operations of a federal instrumentality, but the holding "does not extend to a tax paid by the real property of the bank, in common with the other real property within the State," nor to a tax on residents' interests in it "in common with other property of the same description throughout the State."
- **Not decided: whether a federal wealth tax is direct.** The casebook poses exactly that hypothetical; Moore v. United States (2024) upheld the Mandatory Repatriation Tax on the narrow ground that an entity's income may be attributed to its shareholders, expressly declining to decide whether realization is constitutionally required and saying nothing about wealth or net worth.
- **Not decided: whether a tax set at $0 is still a tax.** After Congress zeroed the shared responsibility payment, the argument that a payment raising no revenue could no longer be a tax under United States v. Kahriger was never reached, because California v. Texas dismissed for lack of standing.
- **Not reconciled: [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] and Kahriger.** Kahriger's levy was a flat annual occupational tax on bookmakers with no schedule of prescribed conduct and no non-revenue enforcement agency; Bailey's was a heavy exaction keyed to a detailed code of employment practice, with scienter and Labor Department inspection. If that distinction fails, there is no line.
- **Not settled: whether the Sixteenth Amendment vindicates or repudiates Pollock.** The casebook's own question is whether the Amendment shows Pollock was wrong, or concedes it was right and that only an amendment could change the result.

## Leading case

[[NFIB v. Sebelius]]

- Holds (Part III-C, for the Court, 5–4) that the shared responsibility payment "may reasonably be characterized as a tax" within Art. I, § 8, cl. 1, and that it is not a direct tax requiring apportionment.
- The driving facts are the features that answer each Drexel factor: the payment is made by "taxpayer[s]" with their returns, is not owed below the filing threshold, is computed from taxable income, dependents and filing status, lives in the Internal Revenue Code, is collected by the IRS, and produces revenue — "the essential feature of any tax."
- The direct-tax holding rests on [[Hylton v. United States]]: a capitation is paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance," so a payment "triggered by specific circumstances" is not one, and Pollock's extension to taxes on personal property was undone by the Sixteenth Amendment.
- The answer to the objection that taxing inactivity is as troubling as regulating it comes in three parts: "the Constitution does not guarantee that individuals may avoid taxation through inactivity"; the taxing power still has the Drexel limit; and a tax "leaves an individual with a lawful choice to do or not do a certain act."
- The windows hypothetical is the cleanest statement of the functional method: a $50 IRS payment for owning a house without energy-efficient windows, adjusted by income and paid with the return, is plainly a tax, and "that conclusion should not change simply because Congress used the word 'penalty' to describe the payment."
- The sentence to quote is the limiting one: "Upholding the individual mandate under the Taxing Clause thus does not recognize any new federal power. It determines that Congress has used an existing one."
- Count the votes: the commerce and Necessary and Proper holdings are Roberts writing for himself with the joint dissent agreeing separately, the tax holding is 5–4 for the Court, and only Roberts is in the majority on every question — so "the Court held" is correct for the tax holding and wrong for the others.

## Best counter-case

[[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]

- Holds 8–1 that the Child Labor Tax Law is a penalty rather than a tax, because "[i]t provides a heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business," and that a penalty on child labor needs a regulatory power that [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] said Congress did not have.
- The distinguishing facts are the same three [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] later turns over — the exaction was 10 percent of *entire net profits* regardless of the scale of the violation, it applied only to a knowing departure with a defense for reasonable mistake, and the Secretary of Labor could inspect alongside the Commissioner of Internal Revenue — plus a fourth Roberts never had to face: the statute itself prescribed minimum ages, a daily and weekly hours cap, and the earliest and latest hours a child could work.
- The decisive contextual fact is timing: Congress passed the tax within months of losing [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]], and the Court held the case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart." The 8–1 vote is the tell, since three Justices who had dissented in Hammer still voted to strike the tax.
- Argue the taxpayer's side with Taft's federalism sentence: "To give such magic to the word 'tax' would be to break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states," and with his epistemic one — "a court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children within the age limits prescribed. . . . How can we properly shut our minds to it?"
- Argue the government's side with the concession Taft himself makes: taxes may be laid "with the primary motive of obtaining revenue from them and with the incidental motive of discouraging them by making their continuance onerous," and "[t]hey do not lose their character as taxes because of the incidental motive." The line is one "of degree," and Bailey identifies factors without supplying a threshold.
- The best structural argument against Bailey is [[United States v. Darby]], which rejected the pretext inquiry for the commerce power outright — motive is "for the legislative judgment upon the exercise of which the Constitution places no restriction" — and overruled [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]], removing the premise on which Bailey's second step depended.
- The best structural argument for Bailey is the one the note makes on the merits rather than institutionally: a shipment ban operates on goods crossing state lines, while the child-labor tax operated inside the factory and dictated hours; the tax was the more intrusive statute even though it was formally the gentler one.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Thinking the Sixteenth Amendment ended the direct-tax question. It exempts taxes "on incomes, from whatever source derived" and nothing else, so a wealth tax still runs straight into Hylton and Pollock.
- Treating Congress's label as decisive either way. The word controls the Anti-Injunction Act and does not control constitutional power; [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] holds that a thing called a tax is a penalty, and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] holds that a thing called a penalty is a tax.
- Confusing uniformity with apportionment, or assuming a tax can satisfy both. "A uniform tax has the same rate in every state. An apportioned tax must have the same amount per person in every state," and only a capitation satisfies both — which is why classifying a tax as direct is usually fatal to it.
- Citing Pollock as good law. Its holding that a tax on income from property is direct was undone by the Sixteenth Amendment, and [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] returns to Hylton's narrow category.
- Overstating [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]. It is the only decision in the whole line to strike a tax, it survives mainly as a limit nobody has enforced since, and Steward Machine Co. v. Davis and Kahriger both distinguished it rather than followed it.
- Saying [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] expanded the taxing power. Roberts says the opposite, and the holding's leverage comes from the saving-construction step — the Court took a "fairly possible" reading over the "most straightforward" one.
- Forgetting that a capitation is itself a direct tax that must be apportioned. It is the one tax to which apportionment "can reasonably apply," which is exactly why NFIB has to show the payment is *not* a capitation.
- Citing [[Hylton v. United States]] as precedent for judicial review. The facts were stipulated, the 125 carriages were "almost certainly untrue" and chosen to reach the jurisdictional minimum, the United States apparently paid Hylton's lawyers, and Hamilton argued for the government.
- Treating the License Tax Cases as making the taxing power a general police power. A federal tax may burden what Congress cannot regulate, but "Congress cannot authorize a trade or business within a State in order to tax it," so the tax buys revenue and immunity from federal penalties, not permission.
- Assuming a state tax on anything federal is void under [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]. The holding reaches the operations of a federal instrumentality, not nondiscriminatory taxes on its real property or on residents' shares, and Marshall deliberately declined to rest on the fact that Maryland's tax singled the Bank out.
