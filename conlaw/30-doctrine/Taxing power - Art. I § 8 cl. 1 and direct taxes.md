## Rule statement

Congress may tax almost anything, so long as the exaction is really a tax and is laid in the required form. The Court treats the taxing power as "an independent power that can be used to achieve goals not attainable under the other enumerated powers." Direct taxes must be apportioned by population and "Duties, Imposts and Excises" must be uniform, so classification usually decides validity; [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]] confines apportionment to cases "where it can reasonably apply." An exaction that functions as a penalty needs some other enumerated power ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]), judged by function rather than label ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]). The contest is whether that line has content: no court has enforced Bailey since, and the casebook asks whether [[United States v. Kahriger]] shows there is no "administrable line between taxes and penalties."

## Elements

1. **Does the exaction function as a tax, whatever Congress called it?** Congress's label controls statutory questions such as the Anti-Injunction Act, but that choice "does not, however, control whether an exaction is within Congress's constitutional power to tax." Weigh the factors [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]] identified and [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] applied in reverse:
   - **Revenue.** Producing revenue is "the essential feature of any tax"; a tax survives though "the intent to curtail and hinder, as well as tax," is manifest and "the revenue obtained is negligible" ([[United States v. Kahriger|Kahriger]]).
   - **Size.** A payment "far less than the price of insurance," which by statute "can never be more," leaves paying "a reasonable financial decision"; Bailey's 10 percent of a year's entire net profits, owed in full for one child employed for one day, was "prohibitory."
   - **Scienter.** A knowledge requirement points to a penalty, because "scienter requirements are typical of punitive statutes" (Roberts, describing Bailey).
   - **Enforcing agency.** Collection "solely by the IRS through the normal means of taxation" points to a tax; enforcement by the Department of Labor, "an agency responsible for punishing violations of labor laws, not collecting revenue," points to a penalty.
   - **Unlawfulness.** A penalty is "punishment for an unlawful act or omission"; a statute attaching no consequence beyond the payment has not outlawed the conduct, and Congress "did not think it was creating four million outlaws."
   - **Regulatory code.** A statute that "provides a heavy exaction for a departure from a detailed and specified course of conduct in business" is a penalty; weight alone is not the test.
2. **If the natural reading is a command, is a tax reading "fairly possible"?** Where the "most straightforward reading" is a command no other power supports, the Court asks whether reading the provision as a tax is "fairly possible," not whether it is the most natural reading.
3. **If the exaction is a penalty, what other enumerated power supports it?** The label matters only across sovereigns: the difference "may be immaterial" where one sovereign holds both powers, "but not so when one sovereign can impose a tax only, and the power of regulation rests in another" (Taft). Route to [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]] or [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
4. **If the exaction is a tax, is it direct?** Paterson's three classes are direct taxes; "Duties, imposts, and excises"; and "All other classes of an indirect kind."
   - **Test.** "As all direct taxes must be apportioned, it is evident that the Constitution contemplated none as direct but such as could be apportioned" (Iredell).
   - **Settled direct taxes.** A capitation, paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance," and a tax on land.
   - **Personal property.** *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.* added taxes on personal property and its income; after the Sixteenth Amendment, NFIB says, the Court "continued to consider taxes on personal property to be direct taxes."
   - **Outside the category.** A tax "triggered by specific circumstances" is not a capitation, and a tax on expense or consumption is indirect (Paterson).
5. **Does the tax satisfy the form rule for its class?** "A uniform tax has the same rate in every state. An apportioned tax must have the same amount per person in every state."
   - **Direct.** Each State's share must track its census population; only a capitation satisfies apportionment and uniformity at once, because its base is population.
   - **Duty, impost or excise.** The rate must be geographically uniform; a tax laid only in some States fails whatever its class.
6. **Does the Sixteenth Amendment reach it?** The Amendment exempts taxes "on incomes, from whatever source derived" and nothing else, so a wealth tax still faces element 4.
7. **Is it a tax on exports?** The Export Clause bars taxes on articles exported from any State.
8. **Is the tax a lever on the States rather than on taxpayers?** A payroll tax with a credit for contributions to approved state funds, designed to induce state legislation, was challenged as coercion of the States, not as a penalty ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]]); that question routes to [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
9. **Is one government taxing the other?** A State may not tax the operations of a federal instrumentality, because that is "the action of a part on the whole," while Congress taxing state banks is "the action of the whole on a part" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption:** Taxing acts are sustained even where the weight of the tax suggests an intent to destroy its subject; in Bailey "the presumption of validity cannot prevail, because the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions."
- **Not reviewed — motive:** [[McCray v. United States|McCray]] refused to examine the oleomargarine excise's purpose, because otherwise "the abuse by one department of the government of its lawful powers is to be corrected by the abuse of its powers by another department."
- **Bailey's workaround:** Taft does not abandon McCray's rule; he reads purpose from the statute's text rather than from legislative motive, which is the move every tax/penalty argument must make or defeat.
- **Not reviewed — weight:** *Veazie Bank v. Fenno* left the size of a tax to Congress, because "the responsibility of the Legislature is not to the courts, but to the people."
- **Veazie's dictum:** Veazie added that there are "certain virtual limitations, arising from the principles of the Constitution itself," which Bailey turned into a holding.
- **Standard — construction:** A "fairly possible" tax reading suffices, so in NFIB the saving construction, not the tax analysis, did the decisive work.
- **Standard — classification:** Chase in [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton]] would defer to "[t]he deliberate decision of the National Legislature" in a doubtful case, and offered his two-item list of direct taxes as a view on which he did "not give a judicial opinion."
- **What fills the gap:** A tax can only take money; the taxing power "is limited to requiring an individual to pay money into the Federal Treasury, no more," and it "leaves an individual with a lawful choice to do or not do a certain act."
- **Purpose limit — hand-off:** The clause's "general Welfare" purpose gets near-total deference; that inquiry belongs to [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].

## Exceptions

- **Sixteenth Amendment:** Removes income taxes from apportionment and nothing else; a wealth tax or any levy not "on incomes" still faces the direct-tax question. The casebook counts the Amendment as the third time a Supreme Court decision was reversed by amendment ([[Amendment process - Art. V]]).
- **Export Clause:** An absolute bar, unlike apportionment and uniformity, which are rules of form.
- **Penalty limit:** Bailey has never been overruled. NFIB keeps it as the outer bound, quoting through *Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch* that a tax may become "a mere penalty with the characteristics of regulation and punishment," and lists [[United States v. Butler|Butler]] with Bailey as cases "invalidating punitive exactions."
- **No federal license:** The *License Tax Cases* upheld federal fees on intrastate lottery and liquor businesses Congress could not regulate, but "Congress cannot authorize a trade or business within a State in order to tax it"; the license implies "nothing except that the licensee shall be subject to no penalties under national law, if he pays it."
- **Intergovernmental immunity carve-outs:** [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s bar "does not extend to a tax paid by the real property of the bank, in common with the other real property within the State," nor to a nondiscriminatory tax on residents' shares in the Bank.
- **Not decided — wealth tax:** The casebook poses a federal tax on all wealth. *Moore v. United States* (2024) upheld the Mandatory Repatriation Tax on the narrow ground that an entity's income may be attributed to its shareholders, expressly declining to decide whether realization is required and saying nothing about wealth.
- **Not decided — the mandate as a direct tax:** The joint dissent in NFIB raised the Direct Tax Clause question without deciding it. The casebook asks whether the payment is a head tax on the uninsured or, because it runs through the income-tax system, an income tax saved by the Sixteenth Amendment.
- **Not decided — a $0 tax:** After Congress zeroed the shared responsibility payment, the argument that a payment raising no revenue is no longer a tax under Kahriger was never reached, because *California v. Texas* (2021) dismissed for lack of standing.
- **Not reconciled — Bailey and Kahriger:** Kahriger's tax was a flat occupational levy on bookmakers with no prescribed code of conduct and no non-revenue enforcing agency; Bailey's was a heavy exaction keyed to a detailed employment code, with scienter and Labor Department inspection. If that distinction fails, there is no line.
- **Not settled — Pollock and the Amendment:** The casebook asks whether the Sixteenth Amendment shows Pollock was wrong, or concedes that Pollock was right and that only an amendment could change the result.

## Leading case

[[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]

- **Holding:** Part III-C, for the Court (5–4; Roberts, with Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan), holds that the shared responsibility payment "may reasonably be characterized as a tax" under Art. I, § 8, cl. 1 and is not a direct tax requiring apportionment.
- **Driving facts:** The payment is made by "taxpayer[s]" with their returns, is not owed below the filing threshold, is computed from taxable income, dependents and filing status, sits in the Internal Revenue Code, is collected by the IRS, and raises revenue — about $4 billion a year by 2017.
- **Direct tax:** The payment is not a capitation, which is paid "without regard to property, profession, or any other circumstance," because it is "triggered by specific circumstances," and it is "plainly not a tax on the ownership of land or personal property."
- **Tax on inactivity:** Roberts (Part III-C) gives three answers: "the Constitution does not guarantee that individuals may avoid taxation through inactivity"; the Bailey limit still exists; and a tax takes only money and leaves "a lawful choice."
- **Windows hypothetical:** A $50 IRS payment for owning a house without energy-efficient windows, adjusted by taxable income and paid with the return, is plainly a tax, and "[t]hat conclusion should not change simply because Congress used the word 'penalty' to describe the payment."
- **Separate opinions:** The joint dissent (Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Alito) argues "[t]he issue is not whether Congress had the power to frame the minimum-coverage provision as a tax, but whether it did so," pointing to § 5000A's title "Requirement to maintain minimum essential coverage" and the word "penalty" used eighteen times; "we have never held—never—that a penalty imposed for violation of the law was so trivial as to be in effect a tax."
- **Quote:** "Upholding the individual mandate under the Taxing Clause thus does not recognize any new federal power. It determines that Congress has used an existing one."
- **Division of labor:** The Medicaid coercion holding (Part IV) belongs to [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]], and the commerce holding to [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]]; only the tax holding is an opinion of the Court on the merits of the mandate.

## Best counter-case

[[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]

- **Distinguishing line:** An exaction is a penalty when the statute itself prescribes a detailed code of conduct and punishes departures with a heavy charge untied to the size of the violation, and it remains a tax when it is modest, revenue-raising, IRS-collected and leaves paying a lawful choice.
- **Holding:** The Child Labor Tax Law is a penalty, not a tax (8–1; Taft, C.J.; Clarke, J., dissenting without opinion), and a penalty on child labor needs a regulatory power that [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] said Congress lacked; the case "cannot be distinguished from that of Hammer v. Dagenhart."
- **Driving facts:** 10 percent of entire net profits regardless of the scale of the violation, liability only for a knowing departure, and Labor Department inspection — the three factors NFIB turns over — plus a fourth NFIB never faced: the statute itself fixed minimum ages, daily and weekly hours, and the earliest and latest hours a child could work.
- **Timing:** Congress passed the tax after losing Hammer, and three Justices who had dissented in Hammer voted to strike the tax.
- **Argue for the taxpayer:** Taft's federalism sentence — "To give such magic to the word 'tax' would be to break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states" — and his epistemic one: "a court must be blind not to see that the so-called tax is imposed to stop the employment of children."
- **Argue for the government:** Taft concedes that taxes imposed "with the incidental motive of discouraging" an activity "do not lose their character as taxes because of the incidental motive"; Bailey names factors without a threshold, and McCray forbids policing purpose.
- **Argue against Bailey's premise:** [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] rejected the pretext inquiry for the commerce power — motive is "for the legislative judgment" — and overruled Hammer, removing the premise on which Bailey's second step depended.
- **Argue for Bailey on the merits:** A shipment ban operates on goods crossing state lines, while the child-labor tax operated inside the factory and dictated hours, so the tax was the more intrusive statute though formally the gentler one.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The Sixteenth Amendment ended the direct-tax question."** The Amendment exempts only taxes "on incomes, from whatever source derived," so a wealth tax still runs into Hylton, Pollock and the open question in *Moore v. United States*.
- **"Congress's label decides whether it is a tax."** The label controls the Anti-Injunction Act and not constitutional power: Bailey holds that a thing called a tax is a penalty, and NFIB holds that a thing called a penalty is a tax.
- **"Apportionment and uniformity are the same requirement."** A uniform tax has the same rate everywhere; an apportioned tax has the same amount per person everywhere. Only a capitation satisfies both, which is why classifying a tax as direct is usually fatal to it and why NFIB had to show the payment was not a capitation.
- **"Pollock is dead, so only capitations and land taxes are direct."** The Sixteenth Amendment displaced Pollock's income-tax holding, but Roberts says in NFIB that the Court "continued to consider taxes on personal property to be direct taxes."
- **"NFIB expanded the taxing power."** Roberts says the holding "does not recognize any new federal power"; the leverage came from the saving construction, which took a "fairly possible" reading over the "most straightforward" one.
- **"Hylton is solid precedent for judicial review."** The facts were stipulated, the 125 carriages were "almost certainly untrue" and chosen to reach the jurisdictional minimum, the United States apparently paid Hylton's lawyers, and Hamilton argued for the government.
