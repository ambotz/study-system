## Rule statement

The judicial power extends only to "Cases" and "Controversies," so a federal court may decide a constitutional question only as a step in resolving an actual dispute between adverse parties, and a plaintiff must show a concrete injury, fairly traceable to the defendant, redressable by a favorable ruling ([[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]]). The older formulation is the operative one on an exam: a private individual "must show that he has sustained, or is immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury," and "it is not sufficient that he has merely a general interest common to all members of the public" ([[Ex parte Levitt]]). Standing asks who may sue; ripeness and mootness ask when. The contest is whether these are Article III commands at all — the three elements appear nowhere in the text, and the originalist question is whether "Cases" and "Controversies," read with the 1793 refusal in [[The Correspondence of the Justices]], require an injured plaintiff or only a real and adverse dispute.

## Elements

1. **An actual dispute between adverse parties, not a request for advice.** The Justices in 1793 refused twenty-nine purely legal questions from the executive because deciding them would violate "the lines of separation drawn by the Constitution between the three departments," would prejudge cases that might reach "judges of a court in the last resort," and because the Opinion Clause "seems to have been purposely as well as expressly united to the executive departments."
2. **Injury in fact.** The injury must be concrete and particularized, actual or imminent, and not shared with the public at large; a threatened injury is too speculative where it is unclear whether the plaintiff is affected at all.
3. **Traceability.** The injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, which fails where the government might have acted under some other legal authority, or where a taxpayer's bill cannot be tied to one appropriation.
4. **Redressability.** A favorable ruling must be likely to remedy the injury.
   - An injunction redresses only a continuing violation or the likelihood of a future one; allegations of "only past infractions" fail.
   - Damages, including nominal damages, redress a past violation, so the same plaintiff may have standing for one remedy and not the other.
5. **Ripeness, if the suit comes too early.** Two parts: the hardship from withholding review — a likelihood of prosecution with substantial consequences unless the plaintiff abandons allegedly lawful conduct, certain enforcement not yet begun, or substantial collateral hardship — and the fitness of the issues and the record, where the more "purely legal" the question the likelier it is ripe.
6. **Mootness, if the suit comes too late.** A case is moot "when the requisite personal injury that gave rise to the case or controversy ceases for some reason to exist," through death, settlement, or repeal or expiration of the challenged action.
7. **Passivity, as the background premise.** "The judicial power is by its nature devoid of action; it must be put in motion in order to produce a result," and a judge who "pronounces upon a law without resting upon a case . . . clearly steps beyond his sphere, and invades that of the legislative authority" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).

## Standard of review / burden

The plaintiff bears the burden on all three elements, they "must be satisfied in every single federal case," and they are jurisdictional rather than waivable: [[Ex parte Levitt]] and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] both dismiss "without considering the merits of the constitutional questions." Suits between the branches get an "especially rigorous" standing analysis ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]). Congress cannot manufacture standing: a statute purporting to let any citizen sue does not convert a generalized grievance into an Article III injury. Where no plaintiff can be found, nothing fills the gap judicially, and the question moves to the political branches and their oaths — the Ineligibility Clause was debated by the President, the Attorney General and the Senate and never by a court, and Justice Black sat for thirty-four years. Tocqueville states the cost as a feature: some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit; and even when such a contestation is possible, it may happen that no one cares to bring it." Standing is a threshold separate from the political-question route: a plaintiff who clears injury, traceability and redressability may still be turned away because the Constitution commits the question to another department or supplies no rule of law ([[A Map of Article III]]), and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] blurs the two by calling a State's abstract Tenth Amendment claim "political, and not judicial in character."

## Exceptions

- **Municipal taxpayers.** A resident may enjoin an illegal municipal expenditure, because the relation is "direct and immediate," resembling a stockholder's; the federal taxpayer's relation "is very different" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
- **Establishment Clause taxpayer suits.** A 1968 exception lets federal taxpayers challenge congressional appropriations on Establishment Clause grounds; the casebook calls the distinction "not convincing," and later cases confine it so that it reaches neither discretionary executive spending nor tax credits.
- **States suing in their own right.** Jurisdiction survives for proprietary rights, dominion over air and soil, quasi-sovereign rights "actually invaded or threatened," and boundary disputes — but not for "the naked contention that Congress has usurped the reserved powers of the several states by the mere enactment of the statute."
- **No parens patriae against the United States.** "[I]t is the United States, and not the state, which represents them as parens patriae."
- **Institutional standing of a chamber of Congress.** The House may sue executive officers for drawing money "without a valid appropriation," because that injury "would eliminate Congress's role vis-a-vis the Executive"; it may not sue over the executive's misreading of a statute, because "Article I is not a talisman" ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]). See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **Mootness carve-outs.** Collateral consequences, such as a claim to back salary; injuries "capable of repetition as to that party, but likely to evade final judicial review"; and voluntary cessation where the defendant "remains free to resume it later." There is no exception for a case that is merely important.
- **Structural provisions raised defensively.** Appointments and removal limits are enforced routinely when the plaintiff is someone the challenged officer is regulating, investigating or suing — the posture in [[Buckley v. Valeo]] — which is why [[Ex parte Levitt]] failed and those cases did not. See [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].
- **Not decided: whether a House of Congress may sue the executive.** [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] is a single district-judge opinion; the appeal was held in abeyance, the payments stopped in 2017 and the case settled, so no appellate court ruled on the House's standing there and the Supreme Court has never decided the question.
- **Not decided: whether the three-part test is what Article III requires.** Qui tam and relator suits at the founding are the standard evidence for a looser reading of "Cases" and "Controversies."

## Leading case

[[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]

- Holds that neither a State nor a federal taxpayer may challenge a federal spending statute merely by alleging that Congress exceeded its powers; both suits are dismissed "for want of jurisdiction, without considering the merits of the constitutional questions."
- The fact that decides the State's case is that it had accepted nothing and was required to do nothing: the Maternity Act "imposes no obligation but simply extends an option," and any inducement "may be effectively frustrated by the simple expedient of not yielding."
- The fact that decides the taxpayer's case is the size and shape of her stake: her interest is "shared with millions of others" and the effect of any expenditure on her taxes is "so remote, fluctuating and uncertain" that equity cannot act.
- The floodgates reasoning is stated as a self-evident objection: "[i]f one taxpayer may champion and litigate such a cause, then every other taxpayer may do the same," as to every other appropriation act, and "[t]he bare suggestion of such a result, with its attendant inconveniences, goes far to sustain the conclusion."
- The conception of judicial review underneath it is departmentalist: the Court has "no power per se to review and annul acts of Congress," and review "amounts to little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment," with any injunction running against "the acts of the official, the statute notwithstanding." See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- The sentence worth quoting: a party must show "not only that the statute is invalid, but that he has sustained or is immediately in danger of sustaining some direct injury as the result of its enforcement, and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally."
- Pair it with [[Ex parte Levitt]], which applies the same rule in one per curiam paragraph to the most prominent constitutional question of 1937 and says only that an interest "of a citizen and a member of the bar of this Court . . . is insufficient."

## Best counter-case

[[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]

- Holds that "[t]he House of Representatives as an institution would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the Treasury without a valid appropriation," so an injury shared by no private person and by no individual member can still be judicially cognizable.
- The distinguishing fact is that the claimed role belongs to the plaintiff alone. Frothingham's interest was "shared with millions of others"; the House's is a specific constitutional command, Art. I § 9 cl. 7, that names no one else, and the court insists the theory "is not about how Section 1402 is being applied, but rather how it is funded."
- The second distinguishing fact is the failure of the political remedy. The Secretaries argued the House could simply defund; the court called them "apparently oblivious to the irony," since the House had eliminated the funding and the executive "simply finds money elsewhere without consequence," so "the purse strings would be cut."
- Arguing for standing: without a suit the one check the political tools cannot enforce is empty; "there will never be a history of litigation until the first lawsuit is filed"; and an institutional legislature was allowed to sue in a 2015 redistricting case the court relies on.
- Arguing against: interbranch suits upset "the finely wrought balance," individual members were denied standing in the 1997 line-item-veto litigation, and a statute struck down the next year fell at the suit of injured private parties instead — the ordinary route. Note too that the court decided the constitutional/statutory line by assuming the House's version of the appropriations facts, which comes close to deciding the merits in order to decide standing.
- The same suit illustrates mootness from the other end: the executive stopped the payments in 2017 and the parties settled, so voluntary cessation plus settlement is how interbranch suits characteristically end without an appellate ruling.
- Hold [[Ex parte Levitt]] in reserve as the counter-example that proves the rule from the other direction: the clearest formal rule in the Constitution, and no plaintiff who could raise it.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Reading [[Ex parte Levitt]] as a holding that Justice Black's appointment was lawful. The Court said nothing about Art. I § 6 cl. 2; a dismissal for want of standing decides only who may ask, and the question moved to the President, the Senate and Black's own oath.
- Assuming Congress can fix a standing problem by statute. A citizen-suit provision authorizing anyone to enforce a structural clause does not create Article III injury, so the generalized-grievance bar is not a pleading defect that better drafting cures.
- Merging standing with the political-question doctrine. They are separate bars — a plaintiff with a concrete injury can still lose on political-question grounds — even though [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] calls the State's abstract claim "political, and not judicial in character."
- Running injury and forgetting traceability and redressability. Frothingham fails all three, and the timing doctrines fail independently; an answer that stops at "no concrete injury" leaves points on the table.
- Treating the municipal-taxpayer rule as an analogy that carries. The rule rests on the "peculiar relation of the corporate taxpayer to the corporation," and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] refuses to extend it to the Treasury.
- Concluding that a State has standing whenever it pleads the Tenth Amendment. Massachusetts lost because it had accepted nothing and was commanded to do nothing; a State ordered to administer a federal program is in the position of the sheriffs in [[Printz v. United States]], and a State whose existing grants are threatened is in the position of the States in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]. See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- Citing [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] as settled law. It is a district court decision that no appellate court reviewed and that the parties settled, and another judge of the same court later declined to follow it.
- Calling the 1793 letter itself an advisory opinion and stopping there. Every court may decide whether it has jurisdiction, and a refusal to act decides nothing about anyone's rights; the real force of the exchange is that the Convention considered and rejected both advisory opinions and a Council of Revision.
- Assuming that a doctrine's obscurity is an accident. [[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]] defends incidental review precisely because "legislation is protected from wanton assailants," which means the unenforceable clause is the price of the system, not a defect to be argued away.
