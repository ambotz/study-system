## Rule statement

A federal court may decide a constitutional question only while resolving an actual dispute brought by a plaintiff with something personal at stake. The modern test requires "a concrete injury," "fairly traceable to the defendant," that "would be redressable by a favorable ruling," in "every single federal case" ([[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]]). The older formulation: a private individual must show "a direct injury," and "a general interest common to all members of the public" is not enough ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]). Standing asks who may sue; ripeness and mootness ask when. The contest is whether these are Article III commands at all: the elements appear nowhere in the text, and the originalist question is whether "Cases" and "Controversies," read with [[The Correspondence of the Justices]], require an injured plaintiff or only a real, adverse dispute.

## Elements

1. **Is there an actual dispute between adverse parties, not a request for advice?**
   - The Justices in 1793 refused twenty-nine purely legal questions from the executive ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]).
   - Their grounds: "the lines of separation drawn by the Constitution between the three departments"; their position as "judges of a court in the last resort," who might later face the same questions in a real case; and the Opinion Clause, which "seems to have been purposely as well as expressly united to the executive departments."
2. **Has the plaintiff suffered an injury in fact?**
   - The injury must be concrete and particularized, actual or imminent, and not shared with the public at large.
   - A threatened injury is too speculative where it is unclear whether the plaintiff is affected at all (*Clapper v. Amnesty International*, 5–4).
3. **Is the injury fairly traceable to the defendant?**
   - Traceability fails where the government might have acted under "some other legal authority" (*Clapper*).
   - Traceability fails where a taxpayer's bill cannot be tied to one appropriation ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Frothingham]]).
4. **Will a favorable ruling likely redress the injury?**
   - An injunction redresses only a continuing violation or the likelihood of a future one; allegations of "only past infractions" fail (*Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*).
   - Damages, even nominal damages, redress a past violation, so the same plaintiff may have standing for one remedy and not the other (*Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*).
5. **Is the suit ripe, or has it come too early?** Two parts.
   - Hardship from withholding review: a likelihood of prosecution with substantial consequences unless the plaintiff abandons allegedly lawful conduct; certain enforcement not yet begun; or substantial collateral hardship.
   - Fitness of the issues and the record: the more "purely legal" the question, the likelier it is ripe.
6. **Is the suit moot, or has it come too late?**
   - A case is moot "when the requisite personal injury that gave rise to the case or controversy ceases for some reason to exist" — death, settlement, or repeal or expiration of the challenged action.
   - *DeFunis v. Odegaard*: moot, because the applicant was about to graduate and "nothing concrete turned on resolving the constitutional issue."
7. **Background premise: is the court being asked to act on its own motion?**
   - "The judicial power is by its nature devoid of action; it must be put in motion in order to produce a result" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).
   - A judge who "pronounces upon a law without resting upon a case . . . clearly steps beyond his sphere, and invades that of the legislative authority."
   - A plaintiff who clears every element may still lose on the political-question doctrine, a separate bar (see Standard of review).

## Standard of review / burden

- **Burden:** The plaintiff bears it on all three elements, in every federal case ([[Standing, Mootness, and Ripeness]]).
- **Jurisdictional, not waivable:** [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] both dismiss without reaching the merits — Mellon "for want of jurisdiction, without considering the merits of the constitutional questions."
- **Heightened scrutiny for interbranch suits:** Suits between the branches get an "especially rigorous" standing analysis ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]]).
- **Congress cannot manufacture standing (outside the assigned readings):** Modern doctrine holds that a statute letting any citizen sue does not convert a generalized grievance into an Article III injury; the casebook excerpt does not state this rule.
- **What fills the gap:** Where no plaintiff exists, the question moves to the political branches and their oaths. The Ineligibility Clause was considered by the President, the Attorney General and the Senate and never by a court, and Justice Black sat thirty-four years ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]).
- **The admitted cost:** Some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit; and even when such a contestation is possible, it may happen that no one cares to bring it" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** A separate threshold. A plaintiff with injury, traceability and redressability may still be turned away because the Constitution commits the question to another department or supplies no rule of law ([[A Map of Article III]]; [[Luther v. Borden]]). [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] blurs the two by calling a State's abstract Tenth Amendment claim "political, and not judicial in character."

## Exceptions

- **Municipal taxpayers:** A resident may enjoin an illegal municipal expenditure because the relation is "direct and immediate," resembling a stockholder's; the federal taxpayer's relation "is very different" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
- **Establishment Clause taxpayer suits:** *Flast v. Cohen* (1968) lets federal taxpayers challenge appropriations on Establishment Clause grounds. The casebook calls the distinction "not convincing," and the Roberts Court confined it so that it reaches neither discretionary executive spending (*Hein*) nor tax credits (*Winn*).
- **States suing in their own right:** Jurisdiction survives for proprietary rights, dominion over air and soil, quasi-sovereign rights "actually invaded or threatened," and boundaries — but not for "the naked contention that Congress has usurped the reserved powers of the several states by the mere enactment of the statute."
- **No parens patriae against the United States:** "[I]t is the United States, and not the state, which represents them as parens patriae."
- **Institutional standing of a chamber of Congress:** The House may sue executive officers for drawing money "without a valid appropriation," an injury that would eliminate the House's constitutional role. The House may not sue over the executive's misreading of a statute, because "Article I is not a talisman" ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]]). See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
- **Mootness carve-outs:** Collateral consequences, such as Powell's claim to back salary (*Powell v. McCormack*); injuries "capable of repetition as to that party, but likely to evade final judicial review"; and voluntary cessation by a defendant who "remains free to resume it later." There is no exception for a case that is merely important.
- **Structural provisions raised defensively:** Appointments and removal limits are enforced when the challenger is someone the officer is regulating, investigating or suing — [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]], [[Morrison v. Olson]], [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] — which is why [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] failed and those cases did not. See [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].
- **Not decided: whether a House of Congress may sue the executive.** [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]] is a single district-judge opinion that settled on appeal. The D.C. Circuit later accepted House standing under the Appropriations Clause in *U.S. House of Representatives v. Mnuchin* (2020), but the Supreme Court has never decided the question.
- **Not decided: whether the three-part test is what Article III requires.** Qui tam and relator suits at the founding are the usual evidence for a looser reading of "Cases" and "Controversies."

## Leading case

[[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]

- **Holding:** Neither a State nor a federal taxpayer may challenge a federal spending statute merely by alleging that Congress exceeded its powers; both suits are dismissed "for want of jurisdiction, without considering the merits of the constitutional questions" (Sutherland, J.).
- **Driving fact (the State):** Massachusetts had accepted nothing and was required to do nothing. The Maternity Act "imposes no obligation but simply extends an option," and any inducement could be defeated by "the simple expedient of not yielding."
- **Driving fact (the taxpayer):** Frothingham's interest is "shared with millions of others," and the effect of any expenditure on her taxes is "so remote, fluctuating and uncertain" that equity cannot act.
- **Reasoning — floodgates:** "If one taxpayer may champion and litigate such a cause, then every other taxpayer may do the same," as to every appropriation, and "[t]he bare suggestion of such a result, with its attendant inconveniences, goes far to sustain the conclusion."
- **Reasoning — the nature of review:** The Court has "no power per se to review and annul acts of Congress"; review "amounts to little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment," and an injunction runs against "the acts of the official, the statute notwithstanding." See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- **Quote:** A party must show "not only that the statute is invalid, but that he has sustained or is immediately in danger of sustaining some direct injury as the result of its enforcement, and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally."
- **Companion:** [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] applies the same rule in one per curiam paragraph to the most prominent constitutional question of 1937: an interest "of a citizen and a member of the bar of this Court" is "insufficient."

## Best counter-case

[[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]]

- **Distinguishing line:** An injury to a constitutional role that belongs to the plaintiff alone is concrete and particularized; an interest the plaintiff shares with every citizen or taxpayer is not.
- **Holding (Collyer, J., D.D.C.):** "The House of Representatives as an institution would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the Treasury without a valid appropriation."
- **First distinguishing fact:** Frothingham's interest was "shared with millions of others"; the House's rests on Art. I, § 9, cl. 7, a specific command whose theory "is not about how Section 1402 is being applied, but rather how it is funded."
- **Second distinguishing fact:** The political remedy had failed. The Secretaries said the House could defund; the court found them "apparently oblivious to the irony," since the House had defunded and the executive "simply finds money elsewhere without consequence," so "the purse strings would be cut."
- **Argue for standing:** Without a suit, the one check the political tools cannot enforce is empty; "there will never be a history of litigation until the first lawsuit is filed"; and an institutional legislature was allowed to sue in *Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission* (2015).
- **Argue against standing:** Interbranch suits upset "the finely wrought balance." Individual members were denied standing against the Line Item Veto Act in *Raines v. Byrd* (1997), and the statute fell the next year in [[Clinton v. City of New York (1998)]] at the suit of injured private parties — the ordinary route. The court also assumed the House's version of the appropriations facts, which comes close to deciding the merits in order to decide standing.
- **Mootness from the other end:** The executive stopped the payments in 2017 and the parties settled, so voluntary cessation plus settlement is how interbranch suits characteristically end without an appellate ruling.
- **Hold in reserve:** [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] — the clearest formal rule in the Constitution, and no plaintiff who could raise it.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Levitt held Justice Black's appointment lawful."** The Court said nothing about Art. I, § 6, cl. 2. A dismissal for want of standing decides only who may ask; the question moved to the President, the Senate and Black's own oath.
- **"Standing and political question are the same bar."** They are separate: a plaintiff with a concrete injury can still lose on political-question grounds, even though [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] calls the State's abstract claim "political, and not judicial in character."
- **"No concrete injury" as the whole answer.** Frothingham fails injury, traceability and redressability, and the timing doctrines can defeat a suit independently; run every element.
- **"The municipal-taxpayer rule carries over to the Treasury."** The rule rests on the "peculiar relation of the corporate taxpayer to the corporation," and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] refuses to extend it.
- **"A State has standing whenever it pleads the Tenth Amendment."** Massachusetts lost because it had accepted nothing and was commanded to do nothing. A State ordered to administer a federal program stands where the sheriffs stood in [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]; a State whose existing grants are threatened stands where the States stood in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]. See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- **"[[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell (2015)]] is settled law."** It is a district-court decision that no appellate court reviewed and that the parties settled; another judge of the same court later declined to follow it, and only the D.C. Circuit's later *Mnuchin* decision supports House standing at the appellate level.
- **"The 1793 letter is itself an advisory opinion."** Every court may decide whether it has jurisdiction, and a refusal to act decides nothing about anyone's rights. The deeper point is that the Convention considered and rejected both advisory opinions and a Council of Revision ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]).
- **"An unenforceable clause is a defect to be argued away."** [[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]] defends incidental review because "legislation is protected from wanton assailants"; the unenforceable clause is the price of the system.
