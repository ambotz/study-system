## Rule statement

Constitutional arguments come in five types (text, historical context, structure, precedent and practice, and consequences), and a good answer uses several, then says which one controls ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]). No authority ranks the five; the casebook leaves open whether all are legitimate and whether some apply only when others are "ambiguous or inconclusive." Practice is the one type with a developed internal test, Madison's liquidation: a practice "sufficiently early, long, or settled" can "fix" or "liquidate" the meaning of an ambiguous provision ([[The Decision of 1789]]; [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]). The live contest is whether practice does independent work: [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] gives it "a considerable impression" and then disclaims reliance, [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette|Madison]] makes it decisive, and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]] counts the same record the other way.

## Elements

1. **Text: what did the words mean, "in their literary context, to informed readers," when enacted?**
   - Evidence: contemporaneous writings, dictionaries, and legal texts the drafters borrowed. A term of art carries its established technical legal meaning.
   - Intratextual comparison: Article I, § 10's "absolutely necessary" against Article I, § 8's "necessary"; the Tenth Amendment's omission of "expressly," which the Articles of Confederation contained ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
   - Definite articles and collocations: Taney's reading of "**the** territory" and of "needful rules and regulations" against "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" ([[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]; see [[Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott]]).
   - The dictionary problem: Johnson's 1785 *Dictionary* gives *necessary* as "[n]eedful; indispensably requisite" and *proper* as "[f]it; accommodated; adapted; suitable." Johnson serves Jefferson and Hamilton equally, so the interpreter must choose the dictionary and the sense ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
2. **Historical context: what problem was the provision meant to solve, and what did the drafters intend?**
   - Sources: the ratification debates, The Federalist, the Convention records. For later amendments the framers were reacting to earlier understandings, so the relevant history is different.
   - Convention records used directly: on August 17, 1787, "declare" replaced "make" war, "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks" ([[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]).
   - Rejected proposals: Jefferson argued that the Convention refused an incorporation power. The answers are that the record is silent on why, a rejected general power is not a rejected incidental one, and unsuccessful proposals are weak evidence ([[The Bank Debate]]; [[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
   - Hamilton's contrary rule: intent "is to be sought for in the instrument itself," and "arguments drawn from extrinsic circumstances, regarding the intention of the convention, must be rejected" ([[The Bank Debate]]).
3. **Structure: which of the three structural variants is in play?** Name the variant rather than saying "structure."
   - Relationship of provisions: Article II's Vesting Clause read as a grant, with the Senate's role in appointment a narrow exception ([[The Decision of 1789]]).
   - Location: the Necessary and Proper Clause sits "among the powers of Congress, not among the limitations on those powers" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
   - The institutional arrangement as a whole: supremacy, enumeration, and Marshall's representation argument, "[i]n the legislature of the Union alone, are all represented" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
4. **Precedent and practice: is there a prior interpretation by a court or another branch, and what kind of practice is it?**
   - A single case or episode counts, and the rationale given counts, not only the result.
   - Liquidation, the term Madison used in The Federalist No. 37 and Baude's own scholarly subject ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]). Baude's three requirements: an indeterminate text; a deliberate course of practice, not mere drift; and a settlement the public accepts ([[The Decision of 1789]]).
   - Madison's own conditions: repetition, duration, variety of form, concurrence of all three branches, concurrence of the states, and acquiescence of the people at large ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]).
   - Founding-era practice as evidence of original meaning: "practice and acquiescence . . . commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer," and "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled" ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]).
   - Historical gloss, a weaker claim: Frankfurter in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] gives weight to executive practice that is "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned," without requiring one deliberate decision.
   - Modern use: [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] treats 1789 as having "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the removal power, and [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] relies on the First and Second Banks' insulation from the President to carve out the Federal Reserve.
   - Jackson's competing test accepts the criterion and disputes the tally: precedent decides only "where the acquiescence of the people and the States can be considered as well settled," and the 1791, 1811, 1815 and 1816 votes make "the precedents drawn from that source . . . equal" ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).
5. **Consequences: which reading produces better results, argued at the level of whether this kind of power should exist?**
   - The question is whether Congress should have this kind of power, not whether this statute is good policy; consequences usually combine with structure or practice.
   - State both directions: a narrow reading would "hobble the national government's ability to solve problems the states could not solve by themselves"; a broad one would "risk concentrating all governmental power in the national government" ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
   - Reductio is the usual form: Jefferson's "boundless f[ie]ld of power"; Madison's chain of implications that "will reach every object of legislation"; Marshall's states that "may tax the mail; they may tax the mint; they may tax patent rights" ([[The Bank Debate]]; [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
6. **Which type controls here, and why?**
   - The five routinely point different ways on the same question, so an answer that lists modalities without choosing among them has not made an argument.
   - Practice for sorting: [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] Note 1 (Black on text and structure; Frankfurter on practice; Jackson on all five, consequences above all) and [[The Post Roads Debate]] (text from Livermore, practice from Bourne, structure from Madison, consequences from White).

## Standard of review / burden

- **No standard of review:** Modes of argument are not a doctrine applied to a party's conduct; they are how every other doctrine is built. The real question is who decides and how much deference each decider owes.
- **Who decides in litigation:** The Court claims the choice ("by this tribunal alone can the decision be made") but leaves "the degree of its necessity" to Congress, so the judicial-deference line, not a burden of proof, does the work ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
- **Who decides outside litigation:** Each officer swears to support the Constitution "as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others," so Congress and the President argue in the same five types with no reviewing court ([[The Bank Debate]]; [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]). See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- **Burden on a claim of settlement:** The party asserting liquidation must show Madison's conditions. A settlement once made can be withdrawn through elections, as the Bank War showed ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- **Political question:** Where the validity of an amendment is at issue, [[Coleman v. Miller]] suggests, without a controlling majority, that Congress decides or that the question is political; see [[Amendment process - Art. V]].

## Exceptions

- **Practice cannot cure a clear text.** "[A] bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long acquiesced in, and Marshall confines the argument from practice to "a doubtful question" in which "the great principles of liberty are not concerned" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
- **Liquidation reaches only indeterminate provisions.** That is how Madison's 1791 opposition to the Bank and his 1816 signature both stand: the 1791 argument showed the text doubtful, not that it plainly forbade a bank ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]).
- **Modern practice is not founding practice.** Against ninety years of for-cause commissions, [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] says the Court has "never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power" and that "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere." [[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]] struck the legislative veto despite hundreds of statutes.
- **A vote settles only what it decided.** The Decision of 1789 may reflect a "mixed coalition," so only its rejection of Senate consent and of impeachment-only removal is "clear" ([[The Decision of 1789]]). The Post Roads vote mixed expediency with constitutionality, so it is weak evidence of liquidation ([[The Post Roads Debate]]).
- **A liquidation can come unstuck.** The Bank had forty years of practice and a Supreme Court judgment behind it, and the Bank War undid it for seventy years; liquidation may be a claim about settlement at a moment rather than a permanent fixing ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- **Practice against apparently clear text.** Presidential war-making is long-standing, yet the casebook asks whether it liquidates the clause or whether "a great deal of the nation's practice with respect to war powers has been unconstitutional"; the initiation question looks too clear for liquidation to operate ([[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]; see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]]).
- **Natural law and the law of nations.** These are a source the five-type list omits, and they can carry a case: Grotius and Rutherforth do most of the work in [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]], where text is barely used and precedent not at all.
- **Sovereignty theory as a premise.** Compact theory generates the law-of-nations rule that sovereign rights are alienated only "in clear and express terms," which is strict construction arrived at before any clause is read ([[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]]; see [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]]).
- **Not decided:** whether all five types are legitimate, how they rank, whether some draw content from others, and whether all are always in play ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).
- **Not decided:** whether a hard-to-amend Constitution licenses courts to update it by interpretation, or whether Article V shows the document is not to be "amended" by interpretation ([[The Riddles of Article V]]; see [[Amendment process - Art. V]] and [[Constitutional themes and the dead hand]]).

## Leading case

[[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]

- **Holding:** Congress may choose any means "appropriate" and "plainly adapted" to a legitimate end, and the degree of necessity is legislative, not judicial: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."
- **Driving fact:** No enumerated power mentions a bank or a corporation, so the case could not be decided on text alone and Marshall had to say what else counts.
- **All five types on one question:** text ("necessary" against § 10's "absolutely necessary"; no "expressly" in the Tenth Amendment); structure (placement among powers; supremacy; representation); purpose (a constitution "intended to endure for ages to come"); consequences (oaths, the criminal code, mail robbery, taxing the mail and the mint); and practice (1791 through 1816).
- **Ranking against practice:** Practice "ought to receive a considerable impression" on a doubtful question about the powers of "those who are equally the representatives of the people," but the arguments from practice "are not made under the impression, that, were the question entirely new, the law would be found irreconcilable with the constitution." The casebook asks whether McCulloch should simply have affirmed on settled practice.
- **Kind of instrument:** "[W]e must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding" is an argument about a document that marks "only its great outlines," paired with "intended to endure for ages to come"; it is not a licence to update.
- **Structure alone:** The tax holding rests on structure with no text behind it, the cleanest example in the course of one mode operating alone: "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and "all inconsistencies are to be reconciled by the magic of the word CONFIDENCE" will not do.
- **Quote:** Inquiring into the degree of necessity "would be to pass the line which circumscribes the judicial department, and to tread on legislative ground"; [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]] turned that passage into presidential authority over the same question.

## Best counter-case

[[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]

- **Distinguishing line:** When two interpreters invoke the same original understanding and reach opposite results, prefer the one who asks what the words meant in law and what the legal facts were over the one who asks what the framers expected or would have tolerated.
- **Same mode, opposite results:** Taney says the Constitution "must be construed now as it was understood at the time of its adoption," and Curtis also argues from 1787; the opposite outcomes are the strongest evidence that a modality does not determine a result. See [[Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott]] for the doctrine.
- **Taney's method:** He asks who was "in the minds of the framers" and treats founding-era racial attitudes and the framers' own conduct as controlling, even against general words he concedes "would seem to embrace the whole human family." That is expected-application reasoning.
- **Curtis's method:** He asks what the words meant in law ("natural-born citizen," "naturalization," "the people") and shows that free Black men were citizens, and voters, in five of the thirteen states at the founding.
- **Argue for Curtis:** He wins on Taney's own premise. Taney conceded that everyone recognized as a state citizen at adoption "became also citizens of this new political body," and Curtis proved the class existed, so attack the history, not the method.
- **Argue for Taney's side of the method question:** When history runs out, Curtis's own warning applies: if "the theoretical opinions of individuals are allowed to control its meaning," "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men." That is an argument for tighter textual and historical discipline, not against it.
- **Text stretched past the text:** Taney turns the Fifth Amendment's permission to deprive with "due process of law" into a substantive bar on a class of legislation. Curtis's Cuba hypothetical refutes it: if Congress may free slaves brought in from Cuba under the commerce power, a like regulation in a territory cannot violate the Fifth Amendment.
- **Other same-question, different-mode pairs:** [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] decides the Bank on text, structure, practice and consequences with no argument from original meaning. [[Trump v. Slaughter (2026)|Trump v. Slaughter]] (6–3) and [[Trump v. Cook (2026)|Trump v. Cook]] (5–4, on a stay application) use the same founding-era method with different coalitions: Slaughter reads 1789 as liquidating at-will removal, and Cook reads the First and Second Banks as a tradition of insulation that survives it, over Thomas's objection that the Banks "possessed no sovereign power."
- **Hardest form of the question:** [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] decided right and remedy before holding it had no jurisdiction, and Dred Scott decided jurisdiction and then kept going. If Marbury's ordering was acceptable, say what makes Dred Scott's different; one answer is that Marbury's surplus discussion invalidated nothing, while Dred Scott's voided a statute.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Listing the five types and stopping.** The casebook's questions are about ranking, whether some types draw content from others, and whether some apply only when others run out. Name the controlling type and say why.
- **Treating "practice" as one idea.** Liquidation claims a practice fixed what the Constitution *means*; historical gloss claims only that long, unbroken practice known to Congress deserves weight; ordinary precedent claims neither. [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]'s twelve years of founding-era circuit riding and the eighty years of withdrawals in *United States v. Midwest Oil* are different kinds of evidence.
- **Citing McCulloch for the proposition that practice settled the Bank question.** Marshall says he would reach the same result "were the question entirely new," so practice is a makeweight there. [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] is the authority for practice as decisive, and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] is the evidence that the settlement did not hold.
- **Reading "it is a constitution we are expounding" as licence for living constitutionalism.** In context it is a rule for construing grants of *means* in a document that marks "only its great outlines."
- **Treating Dred Scott as proof that originalism fails.** Curtis is the better originalist in the same case, and his complaint is that the majority departed from original meaning in favour of "the theoretical opinions of individuals"; the case is evidence against bad history, not against the method.
- **Sliding between the two senses of "settled."** Legally settled means no court will revisit the question; politically settled means no actor will reopen it. The Bank was the first after 1819 and never the second ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
