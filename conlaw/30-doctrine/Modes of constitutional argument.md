## Rule statement

Constitutional argument runs in five types — text, historical context, structure, precedent and practice, and consequences — and the course expects both halves of the work from them: what the Supreme Court has said the Constitution means, and where the doctrine may be off track ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]). No authority ranks the five: the casebook leaves open whether all are legitimate, how they rank against one another, whether some draw their content from others, and whether all five are always in play or some apply only when others run out. The one type with a developed internal test is practice, through Madison's idea of liquidation: a genuinely indeterminate provision can be fixed by a deliberate course of practice that the public accepts, and the settlement then counts as the Constitution's meaning rather than as deference to tradition ([[The Decision of 1789]]; [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]). The live contest is whether practice and consequences do independent interpretive work or merely confirm conclusions already reached from text and structure — [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] gives practice "a considerable impression" and then disclaims reliance on it, while [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette|Madison]] makes it decisive and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]] counts the same record to the opposite conclusion.

## Elements

1. **Text** — the meaning of the words "in their literary context, to informed readers," as enacted, evidenced by contemporaneous writings, dictionaries and the legal texts the drafters borrowed; a term of art carries its established technical legal meaning, and prior legal usage controls there.
   - Intratextual comparison: Article I § 10's "absolutely necessary" against Article I § 8's "necessary" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]); the Tenth Amendment's omission of "expressly," which the Articles of Confederation contained.
   - Definite articles and collocations: Taney's reading of "**the** territory" and of "needful rules and regulations" against "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" ([[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]).
   - The dictionary problem: Johnson's 1785 *Dictionary* gives *necessary* as "[n]eedful; indispensably requisite" and *proper* as "[f]it; accommodated; adapted; suitable," which serves Jefferson and Hamilton equally, so the interpreter must choose the dictionary and the sense ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
2. **Historical context** — the setting, the problem the text was meant to solve, and the drafters' intentions or goals, drawn from the ratification debates, The Federalist and the Convention records; for later amendments the relevant framers were reacting to earlier understandings, so the history is a different history.
   - Convention records used directly: the August 17, 1787 substitution of "declare" for "make" war, "leaving to the Executive the power to repel sudden attacks" ([[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]).
   - Rejected proposals as evidence: Jefferson's claim that the Convention refused an incorporation power, answered by the silence of the record about why and by the weakness of unsuccessful proposals generally ([[The Bank Debate]]).
   - Hamilton's contrary rule, that intent "is to be sought for in the instrument itself" and "arguments drawn from extrinsic circumstances, regarding the intention of the convention, must be rejected" ([[The Bank Debate]]).
3. **Structure** — three distinct variants, to be named separately rather than run together.
   - The relationship of provisions to one another: Article II's Vesting Clause read as a grant to which the Senate's role in appointment is a narrow exception ([[The Decision of 1789]]).
   - A provision's location: the Necessary and Proper Clause sits "among the powers of Congress, not among the limitations on those powers" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
   - The institutional arrangement as a whole: supremacy, enumeration, and Marshall's representation argument that "[i]n the legislature of the Union alone, are all represented" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
4. **Precedent and practice** — earlier interpretations by the courts **or by the other branches**, either as a single episode or as a practice "sufficiently early, long, or settled" to "fix" or "liquidate" an ambiguous provision; the rationale given counts, not merely the result.
   - Liquidation, stated as three requirements: an indeterminate text; a deliberate course of practice, not mere drift; and a settlement the public accepts ([[The Decision of 1789]]).
   - Madison's own conditions, each a separate test: repetition, duration, variety of form, concurrence of all three branches, concurrence of the States, and acquiescence of the people at large ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]).
   - Founding-era practice as evidence of original meaning rather than as tradition: "practice and acquiescence . . . commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer," and "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]).
   - Modern deployment: [[Trump v. Slaughter]] treats 1789 as having "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the removal power, and [[Trump v. Cook]] uses the First and Second Banks' insulation from the President to carve the Federal Reserve out of that same rule.
   - Jackson's competing test, which accepts the criterion and disputes the tally: precedent decides "except where the acquiescence of the people and the States can be considered as well settled," and the 1791, 1811, 1815 and 1816 votes make "the precedents drawn from that source . . . equal" ([[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]]).
5. **Consequences** — which reading produces the better results on a stated view of the good, argued at the level of whether Congress should have this kind of power rather than whether this statute is good policy, and usually combined with structure or practice.
   - Both directions must be stated: a narrow reading would "hobble the national government's ability to solve problems the states could not solve by themselves"; a broad one would "risk concentrating all governmental power in the national government" ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
   - Reductio as the usual form: Jefferson's "boundless f[ie]ld of power," Madison's chain of implications that "will reach every object of legislation," Marshall's States taxing "the mail . . . the mint . . . patent rights" ([[The Bank Debate]]; [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
6. **Then rank** — say which type controls this provision and why, because the five routinely point different ways on the same question; an answer that lists modalities without choosing among them has not made an argument.

## Standard of review / burden

There is no standard of review here, because modes of argument are not a doctrine a court applies to a party's conduct; they are how every other doctrine gets built, so the burden question resolves into who decides and how much deference each decider owes. Within litigation, the Court claims the choice for itself — "by this tribunal alone can the decision be made" — while conceding that "the degree of its necessity" is for Congress, so the judicial-deference line, not a burden of proof, does the work ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]). Outside litigation the question is departmentalist rather than adjudicative: each officer swears to support the Constitution "as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others," so Congress and the President argue in these same five types with no reviewing court at all, as the whole of [[The Bank Debate]] and [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] demonstrate; see [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]]. Where the question is whether an interpretation has been settled by practice, the effective burden falls on the party asserting settlement to show Madison's conditions, and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] shows that a settlement once made can be withdrawn through elections. Where Article V validity is at issue the political-question route is available: *Coleman v. Miller* (1939) suggests, without a controlling majority, either that Congress possesses the power to judge a ratification or that its acceptance is a political question, and *LaVergne v. Bryson* (3d Cir. 2012) disposed of a ratification suit on standing and political-question grounds without reaching the merits ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]; [[The Riddles of Article V]]).

## Exceptions

- Practice cannot cure a clear text: "a bold and daring usurpation might be resisted" however long acquiesced in, and Marshall confines the argument from practice to "a doubtful question" in which "the great principles of liberty are not concerned" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland]]).
- Liquidation therefore reaches only indeterminate provisions, which is how Madison's 1791 opposition to the Bank and his 1816 signature can both stand: the 1791 argument showed the text doubtful, not that it plainly forbade a bank ([[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]]).
- A liquidation can come unstuck. The Bank had forty years of practice and a Supreme Court judgment behind it, and the Bank War undid it for seventy years — so liquidation may be a claim about settlement at a moment rather than a permanent fixing ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- Practice was on the ground in war powers for two centuries, and the casebook still asks whether it liquidates the clause or whether "a great deal of the nation's practice with respect to war powers has been unconstitutional"; the initiation question looks too clear for liquidation to operate ([[Madison's Notes on the War Power]]; see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]]).
- Natural law and the law of nations are a sixth source the five-type list omits, and they can carry a case: Grotius and Rutherforth do most of the work in [[Corfield v. Coryell]], where text is barely used and precedent not at all.
- A theory of sovereignty can operate as a premise prior to interpretation rather than as a mode within it — compact theory generates the law-of-nations rule that a sovereign's rights are alienated only "in clear and express terms," which is strict construction arrived at before any clause is read ([[Campbell, Four Views on the Nature of the Union]]; see [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]]).
- Not decided: whether all five types are legitimate, how they rank, whether some draw their content from others, and whether all are always in play ([[Types of Constitutional Argument]]).
- Not decided: whether a hard-to-amend Constitution licenses courts to update it by interpretation, or whether Article V's existence shows the document is not to be "amended" by interpretation at all ([[The Riddles of Article V]]; see [[Amendment process - Art. V]] and [[Constitutional themes and the dead hand]]).

## Leading case

[[McCulloch v. Maryland]]

- Runs all five types end to end on one question, which is why it is the model answer: text ("necessary" against § 10's "absolutely necessary"; the omission of "expressly" from the Tenth Amendment), structure (the Clause's placement among powers rather than limitations; supremacy; representation), purpose (a constitution "intended to endure for ages to come"), consequences (oaths, the criminal code, mail robbery, taxing the mail and the mint), and practice (1791 through 1816).
- Holds that Congress may choose any means "appropriate" and "plainly adapted" to a legitimate end, and that the degree of necessity is legislative, not judicial: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."
- The fact that drove the interpretive method: no enumerated power mentions a bank or a corporation, so the case could not be decided on text alone and Marshall had to say what else counts.
- Ranks the types expressly, and against practice. Practice "ought to receive a considerable impression" on a doubtful question about the division of power between equal representatives of the people, but the arguments from practice "are not made under the impression, that, were the question entirely new, the law would be found irreconcilable with the constitution."
- Supplies the structural argument for reading a founding document at all: "[W]e must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding" — an argument about the kind of instrument, paired with "intended to endure for ages to come," not a licence to update.
- Shows the tax holding resting on structure with no text behind it, which is the cleanest example in the course of a mode operating alone: "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and "all inconsistencies are to be reconciled by the magic of the word Confidence" will not do.
- The sentence worth quoting on judicial role: inquiring into the degree of necessity "would be to pass the line which circumscribes the judicial department, and to tread on legislative ground" — which [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank|Jackson]] then turned into presidential authority over that same degree of necessity.

## Best counter-case

[[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]

- Taney and Curtis both claim to read the Constitution "as it was understood at the time of its adoption" and reach opposite results, which is the strongest available evidence that a modality does not determine an outcome.
- The distinguishing move is what each treats as the historical fact. Taney asks what the framers "intended" and who was "in the minds of the framers," and treats founding-era racial attitudes and the framers' own conduct as controlling even against general words he concedes "would seem to embrace the whole human family" — expected-application reasoning. Curtis asks what the words meant in law ("natural-born citizen," "naturalization," "the people") and what the legal facts were, showing that free Black men were citizens and electors in five States in 1787.
- Curtis wins on Taney's own premise: Taney conceded that everyone recognized as a state citizen at adoption "became also citizens of this new political body," and Curtis proved the class existed. Argue this side by attacking the history, not the method.
- Argue the other side by pressing what happens when the history runs out. Curtis's own warning is that if "the theoretical opinions of individuals are allowed to control its meaning," "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men," which is an argument for tight textual and historical discipline, not against it.
- Taney's due process holding is the counter-example on consequences and structure running loose: the Fifth Amendment's permission to deprive with "due process of law" becomes a substantive bar on a class of legislation, refuted by Curtis's Cuba hypothetical — if Congress may free slaves brought in from Cuba under the commerce power, a like regulation in a territory cannot violate the Fifth Amendment.
- Same-question, different-modes counters worth naming alongside: [[Jackson, Veto Message on the Bank]] decides the Bank on text, structure, practice and consequences with no argument from original meaning at all; [[Corfield v. Coryell]] decides Article IV § 2 on natural law and the law of nations, with text barely used and precedent not at all; [[Trump v. Slaughter]] and [[Trump v. Cook]] split 6–3 and 5–4 on the same 1789–1791 practice, one reading it as liquidating at-will removal and the other as chartering a bank the President did not control.
- The hardest form of the exam question: [[Marbury v. Madison]] decided right and remedy before holding it had no jurisdiction, and Dred Scott decided jurisdiction and then kept going. If Marbury's ordering was acceptable, say what makes Dred Scott's different — one answer is that Marbury's surplus discussion invalidated nothing while Dred Scott's voided a statute.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Listing the five types and stopping. The casebook's questions are about ranking, whether some types draw their content from others, and whether some apply only when others run out; an answer that names modalities without choosing among them has not argued anything.
- Treating "practice" as a single idea. Liquidation claims a practice fixed what the Constitution *means*; historical gloss claims only that a long, unbroken, known-to-Congress practice deserves judicial weight; and ordinary precedent claims neither. [[Stuart v. Laird]]'s twelve years of founding-era circuit riding and the eighty-year withdrawals in *United States v. Midwest Oil* are not the same kind of evidence.
- Citing [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] for the proposition that practice settled the Bank question. Marshall gives practice "a considerable impression" and then says he would reach the same result "were the question entirely new," so practice is a makeweight there; [[Madison, Letter to Lafayette]] is the case for practice as decisive, and [[The Death of the Second Bank]] is the evidence that the settlement did not hold.
- Reading "it is a constitution we are expounding" as licence for living constitutionalism. In context it is a rule for construing grants of *means* in a document that marks "only its great outlines," and it sits beside "intended to endure for ages to come."
- Treating [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] as proof that originalism fails. Curtis is the better originalist in the same case, and his complaint is that the majority departed from the original meaning in favour of "the theoretical opinions of individuals"; the case is evidence against bad history, not against the method.
- Sliding between the two senses of "settled." Legally settled means no court will revisit it; politically settled means no actor will reopen it. The Bank was the first after 1819 and never the second, and most of the unit's disagreements are about which sense is being claimed ([[The Death of the Second Bank]]).
- Assuming the Convention's rejection of a proposal proves its meaning. The record is usually silent on why a proposal was dropped, a rejected general power is not the same as a rejected incidental one, and Hamilton's rule rejects Convention intent as extrinsic altogether ([[The Bank Debate]]).
