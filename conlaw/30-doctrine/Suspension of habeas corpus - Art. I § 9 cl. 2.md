## Rule statement

"The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it," and the operative rule is that the suspending power belongs to Congress alone: the privilege "could not be suspended, except by act of congress," so the President may neither suspend it himself nor "delegate that discretionary power to a military officer" ([[Ex parte Merryman]]). Even during a valid suspension the military may not try or hold a civilian in disregard of due process, the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement and the speedy-trial right, which Taney called "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend." The contest is over who decides and what follows from disobedience: Lincoln's administration answered through [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] that the departments are "co-ordinate and coequal," that a judicial determination "binds only the parties to the case decided," and that "the whole subject-matter is political and not judicial," so no judge at chambers may command the President — which turns the Suspension Clause into the sharpest test of the judgment-supremacy question worked out in [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Elements

1. **A predicate condition: "Rebellion or Invasion."** Nothing less triggers the clause; it is "temporary and exceptional," meant "only to meet a pressing emergency" ([[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]]).
2. **A judgment that "the public Safety may require it."** Who makes that judgment is the fighting question: Bates invokes Luther v. Borden for deference to the President's determination that an insurrection exists, while [[Ex parte Merryman]] treats the whole subject as a legal question for the courts.
3. **An act of the suspending body.** On Taney's rule, an act of Congress; Lincoln's April 27, 1861 authorization to General Scott was private, and Taney stresses that "No official notice has been given" by proclamation or otherwise.
4. **Scope, in time and place.** Lincoln's authorization ran to Maryland and to moving troops; a suspension is measured by the emergency, and Congress ratified presidential suspension by statute in 1863, which moves later suspensions into the category of express congressional authorization.
5. **What suspension does and does not do.** It withdraws the privilege of the writ — the remedy — and does not by itself authorize military trial of a civilian, a search without warrant, or indefinite detention "upon vague and indefinite charges, without any proof" ([[Ex parte Merryman]]).
6. **The separate question of compliance.** If a court issues the writ and the executive refuses, ask whether the court had jurisdiction. Bates's objection is jurisdictional and political-question based: habeas "is in the nature of an appeal," and "it will hardly be seriously affirmed, that a judge, at chambers, can entertain an appeal, in any form, from a decision of the President."
7. **The separate question of jurisdiction-stripping.** Congress may withdraw a statutory route to the Supreme Court in habeas cases under the Exceptions Clause, even as to a pending, argued appeal, and the Court "will not inquire into the motives of the legislature" ([[Ex parte McCardle]]). See [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].

## Standard of review / burden

There is no tiered standard: the question is allocation, not degree. On the merits of who may suspend, [[Ex parte Merryman]] treats the answer as settled law, "admitted on all hands," and rests on the clause's placement in Art. I, § 9 — a list of limits on Congress — and on the writ's history as a check on the Crown, so that executive suspension "would therefore be absurd." Lincoln's answer, as the casebook frames it, is that the clause is written in the passive voice and names no branch, that the power is designed for emergencies, and that only the President can act when Congress cannot meet. In Youngstown terms, Lincoln acted with Congress absent, which is at best the twilight zone; the 1863 statute moved later suspensions to the strongest footing, and a suspension in the face of a congressional refusal would be at the lowest ebb ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]) — see [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]]. Where the executive is the respondent, Bates supplies the political-question route: "no court or judge can take cognizance of the political acts of the President," and the President "is responsible before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal," which routes the check to [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]] rather than to a court. Where jurisdiction has been withdrawn by statute, the Court examines only Congress's "power under the Constitution," and "[w]ithout jurisdiction the court cannot proceed at all in any cause" ([[Ex parte McCardle]]). When the writ is defied, the court's own remedy is exhausted: Taney recorded that his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome," filed the proceedings and left enforcement to the President's duty to "take care that the laws be faithfully executed."

## Exceptions

- The clause itself is the exception: in "Cases of Rebellion or Invasion" the privilege may be withdrawn, which is the Constitution's only express emergency provision and the reason Jackson stressed in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]] that it sits in Article I.
- The rights that suspension does not reach. Taney treats due process, the warrant requirement and the speedy-trial guarantee as beyond even congressional suspension, so a valid suspension of the privilege is not a licence for military trial of civilians while the courts are open.
- Independent statutory authority to arrest and hold. Bates rests the President's power "to arrest and hold in custody persons known to have criminal intercourse with the insurgents" on the oath, the Take Care Clause and the militia and insurrection acts of 1795 and 1807 — a power he presents as distinct from suspending the writ. See [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
- Jurisdictional withdrawal as a practical exception. After [[Ex parte McCardle]] the Court could not hear a habeas appeal under the repealed 1867 route, though the repeal "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised," and months later Ex parte Yerger took the same kind of case through the 1789 Act.
- The limit on that exception: Congress may remove "a particular class of cases" but may not "prescribe a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way," which is the line Chase himself drew in United States v. Klein.
- Not decided: whether the President may suspend temporarily when Congress cannot be convened, the only version of Lincoln's claim the readings treat as fairly debatable.
- Not decided: whether the executive must obey a habeas judgment of a court that plainly had jurisdiction. Merryman produced no further order to release, so the case never squarely adjudged the point.
- Not decided: whether Congress could close every route to every federal court in habeas cases; [[Ex parte McCardle]] left the 1789 Act and original habeas open and did not face total stripping.

## Leading case

[[Ex parte Merryman]]

- Taney, sitting alone as Circuit Justice in Baltimore in May 1861, held that only Congress may suspend the writ and that the President may not delegate the choice whether to obey judicial process to a military officer.
- The driving facts: Lincoln authorized suspension by private order to General Scott, with no proclamation; Merryman, a civilian, was seized at night and held at Fort McHenry; General Cadwalader refused to produce him, and service of the contempt attachment was refused at the fort.
- The merits argument is text and structure: the clause sits in Art. I, § 9 among limits on Congress, and the writ historically existed to check the executive.
- The rights holding is separate and often missed — due process, the Fourth Amendment and the speedy-trial right "have been disregarded and suspended, like the writ of habeas corpus, by a military order, supported by force of arms," so that "the people of the United States are no longer living under a government of laws."
- The remedy is the lesson: Taney issued no order of release, filed the record, and sent it to the President, whose Take Care duty required him "to determine what measures he will take to cause the civil process of the United States to be respected and enforced."
- Worth quoting: his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome."
- Lincoln's public answer, in the July 4, 1861 message, never named Taney: "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" — and, more quietly, "It was not believed that any law was violated."
- The companion wartime ruling is [[The Prize Cases]], where the Court sustained measures Lincoln took with Congress out of session; see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

## Best counter-case

[[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]]

- The Attorney General's formal opinion of July 5, 1861, the administration's answer to Merryman and the fullest executive statement of departmentalism in the course; it answers two questions, whether the President may arrest and hold suspected insurgents, and whether he may refuse to obey a writ of habeas corpus.
- The co-ordinacy premise: the departments "are co-ordinate and coequal — that is, neither being sovereign, each is independent in its sphere," and "if we allow one of the three to determine the extent of its own powers, and also the extent of the powers of the other two, that one can control the whole government."
- The distinguishing move is jurisdictional, not defiant. Habeas "is in the nature of an appeal," the subject-matter "is political and not judicial," and so Bates denies "that he is under any obligation to obey such a writ, issued under such circumstances" — a narrower claim than a general power to ignore judgments.
- Arguing the executive side: the President alone must "take care that the laws (all the laws) be faithfully executed" and swears to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution," which makes him "in a peculiar manner, and above all other officers, the guardian of the Constitution"; the reciprocity point is the strongest — if the President is bound by the courts' principles, "so also is the judiciary bound by the principles laid down by the President," and "[d]epartments co-ordinate and coequal, and yet reciprocally subordinate to each other! That cannot be."
- Arguing the judicial side: Taney's Article I placement argument is the better textual reading; Bates barely engages the clause's text and rests on who decides; and if the President may decline to execute judgments, every judicial decision becomes "a mere advisory opinion, for the president to decide at leisure whether to obey."
- The scholarly fight to have ready. Paulsen's "Merryman Power": there is "no defensible middle ground" between judicial supremacy and executive non-execution of decrees. Baude's judgment power: the executive "must obey judicial judgments, as long as the judiciary properly possessed jurisdiction," which he offers as the best explanation of Bates's own opinion. Paulsen's reply is that jurisdiction "gives away the game" — "Who decides whether a particular judgment falls within the scope of judicial 'jurisdiction'?" The best defense of Baude is that jurisdiction is a familiar legal category policed in ordinary litigation, and a line need not be self-enforcing to be principled.
- The consistency trap to name first: Lincoln in [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] disclaimed resisting the judgment in [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] while denying that its reasoning bound him as a "political rule"; Lincoln in 1861 had an officer refuse a writ in a specific case. Bates's jurisdictional theory is the only thing that reconciles them.
- The institutional counter-case is [[Ex parte McCardle]]: Congress, not the President, closed the courthouse door, and the Court accepted it without inquiring into a motive everyone knew — a reminder that the political branches have a lawful route to the same result that defiance reaches unlawfully.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Saying Lincoln defied an order to release Merryman. Taney issued a writ and a contempt attachment, both refused, and his final opinion ordered only that the record be filed and transmitted to the President. What was adjudged matters, because the whole judgment-supremacy debate turns on it.
- Treating [[Ex parte Merryman]] as a Supreme Court decision. It is Taney alone as Circuit Justice, and Bates's answer exploits exactly that — "a judge, at chambers" — so the case binds nobody as precedent.
- Assuming the Suspension Clause names a branch. It does not; it is written in the passive voice. Taney's answer is structural (placement in Art. I, § 9, and the writ's history against the Crown), not a quotation from the clause.
- Treating suspension as authority for military trial or for detention on no evidence. Suspension withdraws the remedy; [[Ex parte Merryman]] holds that the underlying guarantees are untouched, and calls them laws "which congress itself could not suspend."
- Conflating departmentalism about opinions with disobedience of judgments. [[Cooper v. Aaron]] concerns state officials resisting a federal decree, and the Supremacy Clause supplies a hierarchy between nation and State that does not exist among the federal departments — which is Bates's co-ordinacy argument, and why Cooper can be right about Arkansas without settling Merryman.
- Citing [[Ex parte McCardle]] for the proposition that Congress may strip all jurisdiction. The repeal reached only appeals under the 1867 Act, the Court said so in the opinion's last paragraph, and Ex parte Yerger took the next case through the 1789 route.
