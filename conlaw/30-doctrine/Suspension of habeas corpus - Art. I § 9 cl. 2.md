## Rule statement

Only Congress may suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and only during a rebellion or invasion that endangers the public safety. [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] held that the privilege "could not be suspended, except by act of congress," so the President can neither suspend it himself nor "delegate that discretionary power to a military officer." Suspension withdraws only the remedy: Taney called due process, the warrant requirement and the speedy-trial right "great and fundamental laws, which congress itself could not suspend." The contest is over who decides and what follows when the writ is refused. [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus|Attorney General Bates]] answered that a judgment "binds only the parties to the case decided" and that "the whole subject-matter is political and not judicial," which makes the Clause the sharpest test of [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath|judgment supremacy]].

## Elements

1. **Is there a "Rebellion or Invasion"?**
   - Nothing less triggers the Clause.
   - Jackson in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]: aside from suspension of the writ, the Framers "made no express provision for exercise of extraordinary authority because of a crisis."
   - Bates describes the President's suspending power as "temporary and exceptional," meant "only to meet a pressing emergency, when the judiciary is found to be too weak to insure the public safety."
2. **Who judged that "the public Safety may require it"?**
   - Bates relies on [[Luther v. Borden]] for judicial deference to the President's determination of an emergency.
   - On Taney's view the suspending power, and so the judgment of necessity, belongs to Congress.
3. **Did Congress act?**
   - On Taney's rule an act of Congress is required.
   - Lincoln's April 27, 1861 authorization to General Scott was a private order; Taney stresses that "No official notice has been given to the courts of justice, or to the public, by proclamation or otherwise."
   - Congress authorized presidential suspension by statute in 1863, which puts later suspensions on express congressional authority.
4. **Is the suspension confined to the emergency in place and time?**
   - Lincoln's order reached Maryland and the movement of troops to the capital, when Congress could not safely be convened in Washington.
   - Lincoln's strongest version of his claim is a temporary suspension until Congress convenes and can decide, which is the posture he was in when he reported to Congress on July 4, 1861.
5. **Does the detention rest on anything beyond the suspension?**
   - Suspension withdraws the remedy; it does not authorize arrest "upon vague and indefinite charges, without any proof," a search without a warrant, or denial of a speedy trial "in a court of justice" ([[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]]).
   - Bates grounds a separate power "to arrest and hold in custody persons known to have criminal intercourse with the insurgents" in the oath, the Take Care Clause and the acts of 1795 and 1807; see [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] and [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
6. **If the executive refuses the writ, did the court have jurisdiction?**
   - Bates: habeas "is in the nature of an appeal," and "it will hardly be seriously affirmed, that a judge, at chambers, can entertain an appeal, in any form, from a decision of the President."
   - Bates adds that "no court or judge can take cognizance of the political acts of the President."
   - The obedience question routes to [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
7. **Has Congress withdrawn the route to the Court instead?**
   - Congress may withdraw a statutory route to the Supreme Court in habeas cases under the Exceptions Clause, even for a pending, argued appeal ([[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]]).
   - The Court will not inquire into motive: "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature." See [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Standard:** there is no tiered scrutiny; the question is allocation of the power, not degree of justification.
- **Presumption:** Taney treats congressional exclusivity as settled law, "admitted on all hands." The casebook's summary of his grounds: the Clause's placement in Art. I, § 9 marks it as a limit on Congress's legislative power, and the writ historically checked the King, so executive suspension "would therefore be absurd."
- **Lincoln's reply:** the Clause does not specify who exercises the power; the power was designed for emergencies; suspension was necessary to execute the laws; and only the President can act when Congress is out of session.
- **Burden (Youngstown):** Lincoln acted with Congress absent, which is the twilight zone at best; the 1863 statute moved later suspensions to the strongest footing; a suspension in the face of a congressional refusal would be at the lowest ebb. See [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]].
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Bates's route — the subject is "political and not judicial," and the President "is responsible before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal"; see [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].
- **Not reviewed (jurisdiction withdrawn):** where Congress has repealed the route, the Court examines only Congress's "power under the Constitution," and "[w]ithout jurisdiction the court cannot proceed at all in any cause" ([[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]]).
- **What fills the gap:** when the writ is defied, the court's own remedy is exhausted. Taney recorded that his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome," filed the proceedings and left enforcement to the President's duty to "take care that the laws be faithfully executed."

## Exceptions

- **The Clause itself:** in "Cases of Rebellion or Invasion" the privilege may be withdrawn. The Clause is the Constitution's only express emergency provision, and its location in Article I cuts for Taney.
- **Rights suspension does not reach:** Taney treats due process, the warrant requirement and the speedy-trial guarantee as beyond even congressional suspension, so a valid suspension is not a licence to hold a civilian without process.
- **Independent power to arrest and hold:** Bates presents the President's power to arrest suspected insurgents, grounded in the oath, the Take Care Clause and the acts of 1795 and 1807, as distinct from any power to suspend the writ.
- **Jurisdictional withdrawal:** after [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] the Court could not hear a habeas appeal under the repealed 1867 route, but the repeal "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised," and months later *Ex parte Yerger* took the same kind of case through the 1789 Act.
- **The Klein limit:** Congress may remove "a particular class of cases" but may not "prescribe a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way," the line Chase himself drew in *United States v. Klein*.
- **Not decided:** whether the President may suspend temporarily when Congress cannot be convened, the only version of Lincoln's claim the readings treat as fairly debatable.
- **Not decided:** whether the executive must obey a habeas judgment of a court that plainly had jurisdiction. Merryman produced no order to release, so the case never squarely adjudged the point.
- **Not decided:** whether Congress could close every route to every federal court in habeas cases; [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] left the 1789 Act and original habeas open and did not face total stripping.
- **Not decided:** whether the executive may hold a citizen without charge absent a suspension. Scalia, J. (joined by Stevens, J.), dissenting in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld* — printed in the casebook but not assigned — reads the founding-era writings to mean that "the only constitutional alternatives are to charge the crime or suspend the writ."

## Leading case

[[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]]

- **Holding:** Taney, sitting alone as Circuit Justice in Baltimore on May 28, 1861, held that only Congress may suspend the writ and that the President may not leave to a military officer the choice whether to obey judicial process.
- **Driving facts:** Lincoln authorized suspension by private order to General Scott, with no proclamation; Merryman, a civilian, was seized at night and held at Fort McHenry; General Cadwalader declined to produce him, and service of the contempt attachment was refused at the fort.
- **Reasoning:** Taney's excerpt treats congressional exclusivity as "admitted on all hands"; the casebook's Note 1 supplies the text-and-structure argument — placement in Art. I, § 9 among limits on Congress, and the writ's history as a check on the executive.
- **Rights holding:** due process, the Fourth Amendment and the speedy-trial right "have been disregarded and suspended, like the writ of habeas corpus, by a military order, supported by force of arms," so that "the people of the United States are no longer living under a government of laws."
- **Remedy:** Taney issued no order of release; he filed the record and sent it to the President, whose Take Care duty required him "to determine what measures he will take to cause the civil process of the United States to be respected and enforced."
- **Quote:** his power "has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome."
- **Lincoln's answer:** the July 4, 1861 message never named Taney — "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" — and, more quietly, "It was not believed that any law was violated."
- **Companion ruling:** [[The Prize Cases (1863)|The Prize Cases]] sustained measures Lincoln took with Congress out of session; see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].

## Best counter-case

[[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Bates justifies refusing the writ because "a judge, at chambers" lacked jurisdiction over a political subject, not because the President may ignore judgments generally, so on Baude's reading a refusal is defensible only where the issuing court lacked jurisdiction.
- **What it answers:** the Attorney General's formal opinion of July 5, 1861 addresses whether the President may arrest and hold suspected insurgents, and whether he may refuse to obey a writ of habeas corpus.
- **Co-ordinacy premise:** the departments "are co-ordinate and coequal—that is, neither being sovereign, each is independent in its sphere," and "if we allow one of the three to determine the extent of its own powers, and also the extent of the powers of the other two, that one can control the whole government."
- **Argue for the executive:** the President alone must "take care that the laws (all the laws) be faithfully executed" and swears to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution," which makes him "in a peculiar manner, and above all other officers, the guardian of the Constitution."
- **Argue reciprocity:** if the President is bound by the judiciary's principles, "so also is the judiciary bound by the principles laid down by the President" — "Departments co-ordinate and coequal, and yet reciprocally subordinate to each other! That cannot be."
- **Argue for the courts:** Taney's Article I placement argument is the better textual reading, and Bates barely engages the Clause's text. If the President may decline to execute judgments, every judicial decision risks becoming "a mere advisory opinion, for the president to decide at leisure whether to obey" (the casebook's Note 6).
- **The scholarly fight:** Paulsen's "Merryman Power" holds there is "no defensible middle ground" between judicial supremacy and executive non-execution of decrees. Baude's judgment power holds that the executive "must obey judicial judgments, as long as the judiciary properly possessed jurisdiction," and offers that as the best explanation of Bates's opinion.
- **Paulsen's reply and the defense:** Paulsen says jurisdiction "gives away the game" — "Who decides whether a particular judgment falls within the scope of judicial 'jurisdiction'?" The best defense of Baude is that jurisdiction is a familiar legal category policed in ordinary litigation, and a line need not be self-enforcing to be principled.
- **The consistency question:** Lincoln in [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] disclaimed resisting the judgment in [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]] while denying that its reasoning bound him as a "political rule"; Lincoln in 1861 had an officer refuse a writ in a specific case. Bates's jurisdictional theory is the only thing that reconciles the two.
- **The institutional counterpoint:** in [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] Congress, not the President, closed the courthouse door, and the Court accepted it without inquiring into a motive everyone knew — a lawful route to the result that defiance reaches unlawfully.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Lincoln defied an order to release Merryman."** Taney issued a writ and a contempt attachment, both refused, and his final opinion ordered only that the record be filed and transmitted to the President. What was adjudged matters, because the judgment-supremacy debate turns on it.
- **"Merryman is a Supreme Court decision."** Taney sat alone as Circuit Justice, and Bates exploits that — "a judge, at chambers." The casebook, by contrast, says Taney ruled "from the bench" in the circuit court; either way the ruling carries the weight of one Justice on circuit, not of the Court.
- **"The Suspension Clause names Congress."** The Clause does not say who suspends. Taney's answer is structural (placement in Art. I, § 9, and the writ's history against the Crown), not a quotation from the Clause.
- **"Suspension authorizes military detention or trial."** Suspension withdraws the remedy; [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]] treats the underlying guarantees as untouched, calling them laws "which congress itself could not suspend."
- **"[[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] settles Merryman."** Cooper concerns state officials resisting a federal decree, and the Supremacy Clause supplies a hierarchy between nation and State that does not exist among the federal departments. That gap is Bates's co-ordinacy argument, and it is why Cooper can be right about Arkansas without settling Merryman.
- **"McCardle lets Congress strip all habeas jurisdiction."** The repeal reached only appeals under the 1867 Act, the Court said so in the opinion's last paragraph, and *Ex parte Yerger* took the next case through the 1789 route.
