## Rule statement

Article I, § 1 vests the legislative powers "herein granted" in Congress, and Congress may not transfer to another branch "powers which are strictly and exclusively legislative" — but a statute that confers discretion is valid so long as Congress "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." The test is not demanding: the Court has invalidated a delegation exactly twice, both in 1935, and has upheld standards as thin as "public interest," "fair and equitable," "requisite to protect the public health," and feasibility. The operative move in practice is interpretive, not constitutional — as [[Gundy v. United States]] puts it, the inquiry "always begins (and often almost ends) with statutory interpretation," so a narrowing construction usually disposes of the challenge before the constitutional question is reached. The contest is over whether that test should exist at all: three Justices in Gundy would replace it with Gorsuch's three founding-era categories, Alito would reconsider it given a majority, and the founding-era debate in [[The Post Roads Debate]] ended with Madison's anti-delegation side winning the vote.

## Elements

1. **Construe the statute before reaching the Constitution.** Read the delegating text with "text, context, purpose and history," and adopt the narrower reading if it is available. In Gundy, "specify the applicability" of the registration requirements to pre-Act offenders was read to mean *how* to apply them in the transition, not *whether* to apply them, and that reading made the delegation "distinctly small-bore."
2. **Identify what kind of power was transferred.** On the dissent's definition, legislative power is the power to adopt "generally applicable rules of conduct governing future actions by private persons"; discretion over the government's own operations or property, which is what the post roads bill concerned, is not legislative on that definition at all.
3. **Look for the intelligible principle in three parts.** Congress must state (a) the general policy, (b) the boundaries of the delegated authority, and (c) the person or body directed to conform to it. A statute that "supplies no standards for any trade, industry, or activity," and that "does not undertake to prescribe rules of conduct" but "authorizes the making of codes to prescribe them," fails — that is Hughes's unanimous reasoning in the 1935 poultry-code case described in [[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]].
4. **Benchmark against what has been upheld.** The comparison, not an abstract standard, does the work: if the challenged standard is more definite than "public interest" or "fair and equitable," it survives. Feasibility standards are "ubiquitous in the U.S. Code."
5. **Run Gorsuch's alternative framework in the alternative.** His three permissible categories are: Congress makes the policy and the executive may "fill up the details"; Congress prescribes the rule and makes its application depend on executive fact-finding; and Congress assigns non-legislative responsibilities or discretion over matters already within the executive's own power, such as foreign affairs. A delegation that fits none of the three is invalid on his view, whatever principle Congress recited.
6. **Ask whether the delegation is policed by some other doctrine instead.** Gorsuch's "hydraulic pressures" point: the Court enforces the limit under other names — the major questions doctrine, void for vagueness, and the structural cases, [[INS v. Chadha]] (delegation to one House) and [[Clinton v. City of New York]] (a power to cancel enacted text). See [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
7. **Check what Congress reserved to police the delegation, and whether the reservation is lawful.** A one-House or two-House veto is void after Chadha; a sunset clause, a report-and-wait provision, and a joint resolution of disapproval presented to the President all survive, because nothing changes the law except a law passed under Article I, § 7.

## Standard of review / burden

- The challenger bears the burden, and the standard is deferential by design: the Court has "almost never felt qualified to second-guess Congress" on the degree of policy judgment left to those executing the law.
- The real screen is constitutional avoidance. Because the inquiry begins with statutory interpretation, a plurality that reads the delegation narrowly never reaches the constitutional test; the Gundy plurality's statutory analysis occupies most of the opinion and the constitutional question "all but answers itself" afterward.
- The consequentialist thumb on the scale is stated openly: "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional," because "Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives."
- There is no heightened review for delegations carrying criminal penalties. Gundy involved a criminal registration regime enforced by prosecution and affecting about half a million people, and the plurality applied the ordinary test; Gorsuch's contrary position is a dissent.
- Vote-counting is part of the standard here. Gundy was a 5–3 judgment with a four-Justice plurality, Alito concurring only in the judgment and refusing to "single out the provision at issue here for special treatment," and Kavanaugh not participating — so the intelligible principle test survived by default rather than by majority endorsement.
- Where the delegation concerns war or foreign affairs the review is weaker still, because the subject overlaps the President's own power: the 2001 AUMF's "those nations, organizations, or persons he determines" survives the intelligible-principle test (the principle being the September 11 perpetrators and harborers) and also falls in Gorsuch's third category ([[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).
- Nothing in the doctrine is resolved by practice alone: a House vote declining to delegate, as in [[The Post Roads Debate]], is not a holding, because members voted on expediency and constitutionality together.

## Exceptions

- **Filling up the details.** Congress makes the policy; the executive works out its application. Sedgwick's formulation is the ancestor: "it [is] sufficient that the House should establish the principle, and then leave it to the Executive to carry it into effect."
- **Contingent legislation.** Congress prescribes the rule and conditions its operation on an executive finding of fact.
- **Powers that overlap Article II.** Foreign affairs, command, and other subjects already within executive power tolerate delegations that would fail elsewhere — see [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]] and [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
- **The government's own operations.** Delegations about government property and internal administration, rather than rules governing private conduct, fall outside the doctrine on Gorsuch's definition — which is why the post roads debate is harder for his position than it first appears, since Madison's side won a debate about a subject his own test would leave unregulated.
- **The necessity carve-out that swallows the rule.** Sedgwick's reductio is still the strongest practical argument: if no part of Congress's power were delegable, Congress would have to "go in a body to borrow every sum," "turn coiners, and work in the Mint themselves," and even "act the part of executioners."
- **Not decided: whether some powers are undelegable regardless of principle.** Whether Congress may delegate the power to declare war, to tax, to appropriate or to impeach is open; the AUMF's "he determines" and the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution's "as the President determines" put the war question squarely ([[Declarations of War]]).
- **Not decided: the future of the test itself.** Three Justices would replace the intelligible principle, which Gorsuch says was "plucked" from a case where it meant something narrower and has become a "misadventure"; Alito would reconsider it with a full Court. After the casebook the Court applied the test again and upheld the delegation 6–3, so no revival has come.
- **Closed off, not excepted: the legislative veto.** Congress's own answer to broad delegation is unavailable after [[INS v. Chadha]], which is White's point that Chadha took away the check while leaving the delegation in place, leaving Congress a "Hobson's choice" between impossibly specific laws and abdication.

## Leading case

[[Gundy v. United States]]

- Holds that SORNA § 20913(d), which gives the Attorney General "the authority to specify the applicability" of the registration requirements to pre-Act offenders, is a valid delegation, and reaffirms the intelligible principle test without adding to it.
- The fact that drove it is the plurality's construction: read in context, the statute requires registration of all pre-Act offenders "as soon as feasible" and leaves the Attorney General only transitional questions, so the delegation is "distinctly small-bore" and "easily passes muster."
- The fact that drove the other way, for the dissent, is the subject matter: the delegation governs private conduct backed by criminal penalties, and on the government's own earlier reading the Attorney General could impose "all of the statute's requirements, some of them, or none of them" on half a million people.
- The vote is the holding for exam purposes: a plurality of four, Alito concurring in the judgment only, three dissenters proposing a replacement, and Kavanaugh not participating — there is no majority opinion.
- Gorsuch supplies the alternative test and the affirmative case for enforcing it: delegation defeats the purposes of bicameralism and presentment, because laws would be "fewer, slower, more consensual, more stable and more accountable" if Congress had to make them, and it invites "finger-pointing."
- Alito's concurrence is the case's real doctrinal event: he agrees the doctrine has let through "extraordinarily capacious standards" since 1935, but says it would be "freakish" to strike this statute alone, and joins neither the plurality's constitutional nor its statutory analysis.
- The sentence worth quoting is the plurality's necessity argument: "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional."
- Gorsuch's answer to the temptation to leave a sympathetic defendant to his fate: "It would be easy enough to let this case go" — the rule that protects sex offenders protects everyone else.

## Best counter-case

[[The Post Roads Debate]]

- The House in December 1791 rejected Sedgwick's motion to strike the list of post roads from the Post Office bill and substitute "by such route as the President of the United States shall, from time to time, cause to be established." Madison's side won, and every modern argument appears in the debate, made by members of the First and Second Congresses with no precedent to lean on.
- The distinguishing fact from Gundy is the constitutional text itself: Article I, § 8 gives Congress power "To establish Post Offices and post Roads," so Livermore's argument is that "it is as clearly their duty to designate the roads as to establish the offices; and he did not think they could with propriety delegate that power, which they were themselves appointed to exercise."
- **Arguing against the delegation:** Madison's structural point that Sedgwick's arguments "admit of such construction as will lead to blending those powers so as to leave no line of separation whatever," and that a subject "expressly committed to Legislative determination by the Constitution" cannot be alienated; Page's slippery slope that if the House can hand the post office to the President "it may leave to him any other business of legislation," and adjourn; and White's corruption point, that a Postmaster could tell a town "If you do not send such a man to Congress, you shall have no post office."
- **Arguing for the delegation:** Sedgwick's distinction that "the establishment of principles was the peculiar province of the former, and the execution of them, that of the latter" — which is not a claim that legislative power may be delegated but a claim that route selection is not legislative at all, and is therefore the root of the intelligible principle; Bourne's argument from practice, that the House had already let the President mark out excise districts; and the competence argument that the executive, "responsible to the people," will decide with more care than members voting their local interests.
- **The fact that undercuts everyone:** Benson's observation that the bill as written designated no post offices at all, so Congress was already delegating the offices while arguing about the roads.
- **The middle position worth having ready:** Hartley's sunset — limit the law to three to five years and see — which is a lawful check after [[INS v. Chadha]] where a reserved veto is not, because a sunset operates by the terms of a law that went through Article I, § 7.
- **How much the vote proves:** it is evidence of original meaning and a candidate for liquidation, but a chamber's vote declining to delegate is not an adjudication that delegation is unconstitutional, and members voted expediency and constitutionality together.
- Runner-up counter-authority: [[The Non-Delegation Doctrine's Good Year]], the two 1935 invalidations. Use it when the hypothetical involves a standardless transfer to private trade groups, and note that the poultry-code decision was unanimous, including Brandeis and Cardozo, so it cannot be dismissed as the work of the conservative bloc.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Saying Gundy "reaffirmed" the intelligible principle test as though a majority did.** Four Justices applied it, three would replace it, one concurred only in the judgment and refused to endorse either the constitutional or the statutory analysis, and one did not participate. The most common error about the case is treating the plurality as an opinion of the Court.
- **Reciting the intelligible principle and stopping.** The plurality spends most of its opinion on statutory interpretation, because the constitutional question follows from the construction. An answer that never narrows the statute has skipped the step that decides most real cases.
- **Treating the 1935 cases as a general anti-delegation doctrine.** They are two decisions in one year, the only two, and the statute in the poultry case delegated to private trade groups with the President's discretion "virtually unfettered." Sunstein's line is the frame: "one good year, and 211 bad ones."
- **Citing Gorsuch's three categories as law.** They are a dissent's proposed replacement, drawn from founding-era cases, and the later decision upholding a delegation with a full Court went the other way; label them as such and then use them, since they are the framework Baude's "places the doctrine may be off track" question invites. The same care applies to "he determines" in a force authorization, which passes the intelligible-principle test and fits Gorsuch's third category, so the real question there is whether the Declare War Clause requires Congress itself to name the enemy ([[Modern Applications of the War Power]]).
- **Assuming the post roads debate supports Gorsuch.** Post roads concern the government's own operations rather than rules governing private conduct, so on his own definition of legislative power the debate is outside the doctrine — and the anti-delegation side won it anyway.
- **Confusing the nondelegation question with the bicameralism question.** [[Clinton v. City of New York]] is decided on presentment, not delegation, and the Court expressly declined to reach delegation; Scalia's charge that it is "a non-delegation doctrine case masquerading as a bicameralism and presentment case" is the argument to make, not the holding to assert. The related drafting trap is assuming a broad delegation plus a reserved congressional check is safer than the delegation alone: after [[INS v. Chadha]] the check is void and the delegation stands, so redraft to a sunset, report-and-wait, or joint resolution of disapproval.
