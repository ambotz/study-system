## Rule statement

Congress may regulate a State on the same terms as private parties, but it may not order a State's legislature to legislate or its officers to administer a federal program. The Tenth Amendment adds nothing to the enumerated-powers inquiry; it "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]). The operative rule comes from [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]: Congress may not "command the States' officers, or those of their political subdivisions, to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program." Outside that rule the States' protection is "one of process rather than one of result" ([[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]]), and Congress may still buy cooperation with conditional grants short of coercion ([[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|South Dakota v. Dole]]; [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]). The contest is over grounding: Printz concedes "no constitutional text speaking to this precise question," while the dissent argues from the enumerated powers and the Article VI oath binding state officers.

## Elements

1. **Is the federal law within an enumerated power?**
   - The Tenth Amendment is "declaratory of the relationship between the national and state governments as it had been established by the Constitution" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]]).
   - The Amendment omits the word "expressly," which [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]] read into it; a law outside every grant fails for that reason alone. Route to [[Commerce power - Art. I § 8 cl. 3]] and [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
2. **Whom does the law regulate?**
   - Private individuals only: no state-sovereignty problem, however much state policy is displaced; "the Framers explicitly chose a Constitution that confers upon Congress the power to regulate individuals, not States." Displacing contrary state law is ordinary preemption; see [[Supremacy and the oath - Art. VI]].
   - A State alongside private actors, as employer or market participant: [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] controls and there is no immunity, because SAMTA "faces nothing more than the same minimum-wage and overtime obligations that hundreds of thousands of other employers, public as well as private, have to meet."
   - The State as a government, told how to govern: go to element 3.
   - Boundary: a law burdening States alone, such as a minimum wage for state employees only, weakens Garcia's premise, and no reading decides it.
3. **Does the law command a state organ to govern?** All three forms are barred:
   - A command to the legislature to enact a program or take title to waste ([[New York v. United States]]).
   - A command to executive officers "in their official capacities as state officers" to administer a federal program ([[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]); naming the officer instead of the State changes nothing.
   - A prohibition on state legislation (*Murphy v. NCAA*, 7–2): the do/don't-do distinction "is empty," because "[t]he basic principle—that Congress cannot issue direct orders to state legislatures—applies in either event."
   - Outside the element: state courts, which the Supremacy Clause binds by name ("the Judges in every State"), so Congress may require them to hear federal claims (*Testa v. Katt*). Note 2's question is whether a sheriff running background checks is more like a judge applying law or a legislature making choices.
4. **Is the command "proper," even if "necessary"?**
   - A law that "violates the principle of state sovereignty" is not a "La[w] . . . proper for carrying into Execution the Commerce Clause."
   - Scalia (Printz): "[w]hat destroys the dissent's Necessary and Proper Clause argument . . . is not the Tenth Amendment but the Necessary and Proper Clause itself."
5. **Does the command move execution away from the President?** The Brady Act "effectively transfers" execution "to thousands of CLEOs in the 50 States," who act "without meaningful Presidential control." See [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
6. **Did Congress use money instead of a command?** Run the [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] test; see [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].
   - General welfare, with near-total deference.
   - An unambiguous condition, so States can "exercise their choice knowingly, cognizant of the consequences of their participation."
   - Relatedness to "the federal interest in particular national projects or programs."
   - No inducement to unconstitutional state action.
   - No coercion (element 7).
7. **Is the financial pressure coercion?** Compare the size of the stake and whether the money threatened is existing money for an old program.
   - Dole: losing 5 percent of highway funds, under half of one percent of South Dakota's budget, was "relatively mild encouragement."
   - [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB]] (7–2; Roberts, with Breyer and Kagan, plus the joint dissent): threatening all existing Medicaid funds, over 10 percent of a State's budget, to force acceptance of what is in substance a new program was "a gun to the head" and "economic dragooning that leaves the States with no real option but to acquiesce."
8. **Did Congress use a "tax" as a penalty on a reserved subject?**
   - [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey]]: "To give such magic to the word 'tax' would be to break down all constitutional limitations of the powers of Congress and completely wipe out the sovereignty of the states."
   - Bailey has never been overruled, but NFIB runs its factors (amount, scienter, collecting agency) to the opposite result. See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]].
9. **Is the burden running the other way, from a State onto the federal government?** "[T]he States have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress" ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]), because a state tax on a federal instrument is "the action of a part on the whole."
10. **Is the dispute between States rather than between a State and the Nation?**
    - [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] rejects an "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule, since "many (maybe most) state laws have the 'practical effect of controlling' extraterritorial behavior."
    - The Court points instead to "original and historical understandings of the Constitution's structure," "principles of 'sovereignty and comity,'" the Due Process Clause and the Full Faith and Credit Clause. Route to [[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]] and [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Standard, inside the rule:** categorical. "It matters not whether policymaking is involved, and no case-by-case weighing of the burdens or benefits is necessary."
- **Why no balancing:** balancing may suit "the incidental application to the States of a federal law of general applicability," but not a law whose "whole object" is "to direct the functioning of the state executive." That is why the Brady duty's temporary, modest character did not matter.
- **Not reviewed, outside the rule:** [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] rejects the "traditional governmental functions" test "as unsound in principle and unworkable in practice," and courts have "no license to employ freestanding conceptions of state sovereignty when measuring congressional authority under the Commerce Clause."
- **What fills the gap:** the political process, through state control of electoral qualifications, the Electoral College and equal Senate representation. Any judicial restraint "must be tailored to compensate for possible failings in the national political process rather than to dictate a 'sacred province of state autonomy,'" and Blackmun gives no criteria for spotting such a failing.
- **Not a political-question holding:** Garcia borrows the lack of manageable standards and something like a commitment to Congress, but it decides the merits and reserves "what affirmative limits the constitutional structure might impose on federal action affecting the States."
- **Burden of history:** with no text on point, Printz takes "historical understanding and practice," then structure, then precedent, "in that order." Whoever claims an early practice of commanding state executives carries the historical burden.
- **Burden on spending:** on the State, and heavy. A condition is not coercive "simply by reason of its success in achieving the congressional objective" (Dole), and "to hold that motive or temptation is equivalent to coercion is to plunge the law in endless difficulties" ([[Steward Machine Co. v. Davis|Steward Machine]], quoted in Dole). NFIB is the first case to find a condition coercive, and Roberts declines, as Steward Machine did, to "fix the outermost line."
- **Canon:** *Gregory v. Ashcroft* adds a clear-statement rule for statutes that intrude on core state functions.

## Exceptions

- **State courts may be conscripted.** The Supremacy Clause names "the Judges in every State," and courts "applied the law of other sovereigns all the time." The early statutes the dissent relies on (naturalization, seaworthiness, fugitive-slave certificates) all ran to state judges.
- **Conditional spending.** Scalia distinguishes "conditions upon the grant of federal funding," which is why [[South Dakota v. Dole (1987)|Dole]] could buy a drinking age Congress could not command. If the price can always be met, the anti-commandeering rule becomes a drafting rule.
- **Generally applicable laws.** *Reno v. Condon* upheld a ban on selling drivers' personal data because it regulated the State as a database owner alongside private resellers and required no state legislation or enforcement. *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) rejected anti-commandeering challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act largely because its requirements apply to private and state actors alike.
- **Ordinary preemption.** A federal law that regulates private actors and displaces contrary state law is not a direct order to a State, however completely it clears the field.
- **Consent.** The 1882 immigration statute authorized contracts with state officers designated by governors, and Wilson ran the 1917 draft with every governor's consent. O'Connor's concurrence stresses that Congress may make participation voluntary or pay for it.
- **Reserved: information-only duties.** Statutes that "require only the provision of information to the Federal Government, do not involve the precise issue before us" (Scalia). O'Connor flags ministerial reporting (missing children) as undecided, and Stevens likens the Brady Act to exactly such a reporting law.
- **Live test: 8 U.S.C. § 1373 (Note 4).** The statute forbids localities to restrict their officers from sharing immigration-status information. Under Murphy it looks like a direct order about what laws a State may pass; the information reservation cuts the other way.
- **Reserved: emergencies.** Stevens's air-raid wardens, draft, mass inoculation and terrorist threat get no direct answer; the majority says only that Congress can ask, pay, or use federal personnel.
- **Rebellion suspends membership rights.** [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] holds that during the rebellion "the rights of the State as a member, and of her people as citizens of the Union, were suspended," and that restoring them is a Guarantee Clause power that "resides in Congress." See [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]].
- **The Reconstruction Amendments add national power.** They are the one place the federalism map records an addition rather than a reservation ([[A Map of the Federalism Provisions]]).
- **Not decided: whether Garcia's reserved "affirmative limits" exist.** Blackmun names the category and gives it no content.
- **Not decided: where coercion begins.** NFIB relies on both the threat to *existing* funds and the size of the stake, and never says which is essential.
- **Not decided: whether the coercion rule protects private grant recipients.** Roberts grounds it in state sovereignty and anti-commandeering, and nothing in the opinion extends it further.

## Leading case

[[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]

- **Holding:** (5–4; Scalia) Congress may not "command the States' officers, or those of their political subdivisions, to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program." "We held in New York that Congress cannot compel the States to enact or enforce a federal regulatory program. Today we hold that Congress cannot circumvent that prohibition by conscripting the State's officers directly."
- **Driving fact:** the Brady Act's interim provisions ran to chief law enforcement officers "in their official capacities as state officers" and required them to "make a reasonable effort" to check each handgun buyer within five business days.
- **Fact held irrelevant:** the duty was temporary, modest and required no action on an ineligible buyer. Holding the burden irrelevant is what makes the rule categorical.
- **History:** the early statutes bound state *judges* "insofar as those prescriptions related to matters appropriate for the judicial power," and the absence of executive-commandeering statutes despite their "attractiveness . . . to Congress" suggests "an assumed absence of such power."
- **Structure:** citizens have "two political capacities, one state and one federal," and federal power "would be augmented immeasurably if it were able to impress into its service—and at no cost to itself—the police officers of the 50 States." Voters would blame the sheriff for a federal policy.
- **Article II:** unity of execution "would be shattered . . . if Congress could act as effectively without the President as with him."
- **Separate opinions:** O'Connor (concurring) leaves voluntary and paid participation and ministerial reporting open; Thomas (concurring) doubts the commerce power reaches intrastate handgun sales. Stevens (dissenting, with Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer) argues from text, Federalist Nos. 27 and 36, Garcia's political safeguards and emergencies, and calls the judge/executive line "empty formalistic reasoning of the highest order." Souter relies on Federalist No. 27; Breyer points to European federal systems.
- **Historical critique (Note 3):** Campbell (2013) argues that at the Founding commandeering was the States'-rights alternative to a large federal bureaucracy, so Hamilton was conceding it to the Anti-Federalists; Bellia and Clark (2020) push back. The question is whether an originalist rule should shift each time the historiography does.

## Best counter-case

[[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]

- **Distinguishing line:** If the statute regulates the State on the same terms as private actors, Garcia applies and the State's remedy is in Congress; if the statute's object is to make the State legislate or its officers administer federal law, Printz applies and the statute falls.
- **Holding:** (5–4; Blackmun) States have no judicially enforced immunity from generally applicable Commerce Clause regulation; *National League of Cities v. Usery* is overruled, and state interests are left to "procedural safeguards inherent in the structure of the federal system."
- **Driving fact:** SAMTA was regulated as an employer on the same terms as private employers and claimed immunity "only as a government."
- **Workability:** eight years of lower-court results made airports and highway authorities immune but not mental health facilities or traffic regulation; "[t]he constitutional distinction between licensing drivers and regulating traffic . . . is elusive at best."
- **Argue for Congress:** a historical test freezes the States in place, and "the States cannot serve as laboratories for social and economic experiment if they must pay an added price" for new functions (Blackmun). Madison in the First Congress: "If the power was not given, Congress could not exercise it; if given, they might exercise it, although it should interfere with the laws, or even the Constitution of the States."
- **Argue for the States:** members of Congress, once elected, "are Members of the Federal Government," so "[t]he States' role in our system of government is a matter of constitutional law, not of legislative grace" (Powell). [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch]]'s means must "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution," and the Tenth Amendment's spirit is "that the States will retain their integrity" (O'Connor).
- **The Seventeenth Amendment wedge:** direct election "removed what the framers had expected would be a check on the exercise of national power" ([[A Map of the Federalism Provisions]]). Either the People abandoned the Senate as the States' agent and courts should defer, or losing the safeguard makes judicial enforcement more necessary; Blackmun concedes the change and does not choose.
- **Both cases survive:** Stevens's Printz dissent quotes Garcia to argue that political safeguards answer commandeering too, citing the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 as proof they work. The whole case therefore turns on characterizing the statute.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not expressly delegated."** The word "expressly" is not in the Amendment. Day inserted it in [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]], Marshall had already rejected it in [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]], and [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] made the Amendment a truism.
- **"Garcia was overruled."** It never was. [[New York v. United States]] and [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]] route around [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]] by separating laws that regulate States alongside private parties from laws that command States to govern.
- **"Anti-commandeering immunizes a State from federal burdens."** A State gets no relief from a wage law or a data-sale ban that also binds private parties. The rule forbids ordering a State to govern, and it is not preemption either: forbidding States to *authorize* sports betting is a direct order (*Murphy v. NCAA*), but regulating private actors and displacing contrary state law is not.
- **"Printz forbids every federal reporting duty, so § 1373 is plainly void."** Scalia reserved information-only requirements and O'Connor flagged ministerial reporting; an answer that skips the reservation is incomplete.
- **"Commandeering ends the analysis."** Congress could not order a 21-year-old drinking age and bought one for 5 percent of highway funds. The next question is always the spending route, and [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]] puts no number on coercion: Roberts relies on both the threat to existing funds and the size of the stake, and germaneness did not decide the case.
- **"Printz rests on Article VI's judges-only text."** Printz concedes there is no text on point. Scalia uses the Supremacy Clause's reference to "the Judges in every State" only to distinguish *Testa v. Katt*; Stevens calls the implied *expressio unius* argument "implausible" and reads the Article VI oath, which binds state executive officers, the other way. See [[Supremacy and the oath - Art. VI]].
