## Rule statement

The United States must guarantee every State a republican government, protect it against invasion, and protect it against domestic violence when the State asks; enforcing those promises belongs to Congress and the President, not to the courts. [[Luther v. Borden]] holds that under the clause "it rests with Congress to decide what government is the established one in a State." [[Texas v. White]] turns the guarantee into an affirmative source of power: restoring a rebel State rests on "the obligation of the United States to guarantee to every State in the Union a republican form of government," a power that "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress." A government the President installs during a war is therefore a war measure that "must . . . be considered as provisional." Two contests remain: [[Buchanan, Address to Congress|Buchanan]] denied that any branch could act against a State at all, and Grier's dissent in [[Texas v. White]] would have left even the question whether Texas was a State to the political branches.

## Elements

1. **Is the entity claiming the guarantee a State "in this Union"?**
   - The clause runs only to States in the Union, which is why [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address|Lincoln]]'s July 4, 1861 version of the argument is circular: a State lawfully out of the Union is not a State to which the guarantee runs.
   - Whether a State is in the Union is itself contested: Grier treated it "as a political fact, not as a legal fiction," while Chase decided it as law.
2. **Which of the clause's three obligations is engaged?**
   - The guarantee of a republican form of government.
   - Protection against invasion.
   - Protection against domestic violence, but only "on Application of the Legislature, or of the Executive (when the Legislature cannot be convened)."
3. **What defect in the State's government is federal action meant to cure?**
   - In [[Texas v. White]] no government in Texas stood in constitutional relations with the Union, and the old government could not resume without new elections under an amended constitution.
   - Emancipation changed the body politic: "The new freemen necessarily became part of the people, and the people still constituted the State."
   - The casebook's working definition of a republic, from Madison in *The Federalist No. 39*, is "a government which derives all its powers directly or indirectly from the great body of the people," administered by officers holding for limited terms or during good behavior.
4. **Which branch is acting, and on what authority?**
   - **Congress** decides which government is established, and recognizes it by seating the State's senators and representatives ([[Luther v. Borden|Luther]]).
   - **The President**, under the 1795 militia act, decides which body is the State's legislature or governor before answering a call for help against domestic violence ([[Luther v. Borden|Luther]]).
   - **The President in wartime**, as commander in chief, "might institute temporary government within insurgent districts, occupied by the National forces, or take measures, in any State, for the restoration of State government faithful to the Union," but those governments are provisional and Congress may supersede them.
5. **Are the means necessary and proper, and otherwise lawful?**
   - Chase applies [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]: "a discretion in the choice of means is necessary allowed. It is essential only that the means must be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the power conferred."
   - Chase adds a limit: "no acts be done, and no authority exerted, which is either prohibited or unsanctioned by the Constitution." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
6. **Does a different clause supply the power more cleanly?**
   - Against individuals resisting federal law, Art. I, § 8, cl. 15 authorizes calling forth the militia "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections."
   - Against conquered territory, the Territories Clause of Art. IV, § 3 would supply plenary power, but only if the States had left, which [[Texas v. White]] rejects.

## Standard of review / burden

- **Not reviewed / political question:** which government is lawful is for the political branches, and Congress's recognition of a State government "is binding on every other department of the government, and could not be questioned in a judicial tribunal" ([[Luther v. Borden|Luther]], Taney, C.J.). If courts could second-guess the President's choice, "the guarantee contained in the Constitution of the United States is a guarantee of anarchy, and not of order."
- **Later applications:** the casebook reports that Guarantee Clause attacks on state initiatives and referenda (*Pacific States Telephone & Telegraph Co. v. Oregon*, 1912) and on legislative malapportionment were both held nonjusticiable. Malapportionment was later heard under the Equal Protection Clause instead (*Baker v. Carr*, 1962).
- **Tension in [[Texas v. White]]:** Chase relies on [[Luther v. Borden|Luther]] for Congress's primacy, yet decides for himself that the Reconstruction government is Texas's lawful government for purposes of the suit.
- **The dissenters' standard:** Grier would defer entirely: "I am not disposed to join in any essay to prove Texas to be a State of the Union, when Congress have decided that she is not." Swayne, joined by Miller, called the status question one "in relation to which this court is bound by the action of the legislative department."
- **What fills the gap:** congressional judgment, checked only for whether the means are necessary and proper, plus political checks on Congress and the President. The Reconstruction collision was settled by the Reconstruction Acts and [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]], not by adjudication, and [[Ex parte McCardle]] shows how little appetite the Court had to test Congress on the subject.

## Exceptions

- **States outside the Union:** the guarantee cannot by itself prove that a seceding State remains in the Union. Used that way, as in the July 4, 1861 argument, the guarantee assumes its own conclusion.
- **Wartime presidential governments:** the President's governments rest on the commander-in-chief function, last only while the war continues, and are provisional. Chase expressly reserved whether the President's Texas measures were "in all respects, warranted by the Constitution."
- **Domestic-violence branch:** federal protection against domestic violence depends on the State's application, through its legislature or, when the legislature cannot be convened, its executive.
- **Execution against individuals:** where federal law can be executed against persons, the guarantee is unnecessary. Lincoln's program was to "hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government and to collect the duties and imposts," which needs only the militia and execution powers.
- **Impossibility:** where every federal officer in a State has resigned ("We no longer have a district judge, a district attorney, or a marshal in South Carolina"), Buchanan said the Take Care duty could not be performed there. Lincoln conceded the offices point: "no attempt to force obnoxious strangers among the people." See [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
- **Not decided: what "republican" substantively requires.** The casebook calls the essentials of republican government and the limits of the federal power it justifies questions "not fully settled."
- **Not decided: how far Congress's guarantee power reaches.** The readings do not settle whether the power supports conditions on readmission or on suffrage.
- **Not decided: the expelled State.** The Lincoln notes leave open how a State that kept a lawful government but was denied its senators should be treated.

## Leading case

[[Texas v. White]]

- **Holding:** restoring a rebel State's relations with the Union is an exercise of the Guarantee Clause power, and that power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress."
- **Driving fact:** the plaintiff was a government the President had created and Congress later treated as provisional, so the Court could not decide whether Texas could sue without deciding whose power Reconstruction was.
- **Reasoning (Chase, C.J.):** suppressing the rebellion rested on "the power to suppress insurrection and carry on war"; restoring the State rested on the guarantee, "a necessary complement to the former." The pairing converts a promise into an affirmative grant.
- **Reasoning on means:** [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s test applies, subject to the limit that nothing "prohibited or unsanctioned by the Constitution" be done.
- **Quote:** "The Constitution, in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States." The guarantee protects the States' governments, so the guarantee cannot license abolishing them.
- **Consequence of the contrary view:** had secession operated, "[t]he war must have ceased to be a war for the suppression of rebellion, and must have become a war for conquest and subjugation," and Reconstruction would have run under the Territories Clause instead.
- **Separate opinions:** the vote was 5–3. Grier dissented on every point; Swayne and Miller agreed on the merits but would have dismissed because Congress had not recognized Texas.

## Best counter-case

[[Buchanan, Address to Congress]]

- **Distinguishing line:** federal force used against individuals resisting federal law, or to restore a lawful government in a State that never left, has a constitutional source; force used to coerce a State as such is what Buchanan said no clause authorizes.
- **Buchanan's position:** secession is unconstitutional, yet neither the President nor Congress may act, because a power "to coerce a State into submission" would be a power "to declare and to make war against a State," which is neither enumerated nor necessary and proper to any enumerated power.
- **Convention history:** Madison said force against a State "would look more like a declaration of war than an infliction of punishment," and the proposal was postponed.
- **The Reconstruction problem, stated in advance:** "Suppose such a war should result in the conquest of a State; how are we to govern it afterwards? Shall we hold it as a province and govern it by despotic power?" [[Texas v. White]] answers that the State never left, so Congress restores rather than governs.
- **Argue for Buchanan:** no clause authorizes war on a State as such, and a duty owed to the States is an odd source of power over them. His prudential point has constitutional weight: "our Union rests upon public opinion, and can never be cemented by the blood of its citizens shed in civil war."
- **Argue against Buchanan:** he treats every federal response as war on a State, when the Constitution's route is enforcement against individuals, backed by Art. I, § 8, cl. 15 and by the domestic-violence branch of Art. IV, § 4. His error was refusing to use the power over individuals that the Constitution does give.
- **Hypothetical:** if South Carolina seizes the Charleston customs house, collecting duties is executing federal law against individuals, not coercing a State, so even on Buchanan's theory the President could act.
- **Contemporary verdict:** in the casebook's words, Republicans ridiculed the message as saying, "in effect, that no state could secede—unless it wants to—and that the president has a duty to enforce the laws—unless somebody opposes him."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The Guarantee Clause gives individuals a judicially enforceable right to republican government."** [[Luther v. Borden|Luther]] routes enforcement to the political branches, and the Court has refused Guarantee Clause challenges to initiatives and to malapportionment on the same ground.
- **"Lincoln's Guarantee Clause argument is his strongest."** The July 4, 1861 argument ("to prevent its going out, is an indispensable means, to the end, of maintaining the guarantee") assumes the State is still in the Union. Its real value is a textual hook for force where Buchanan said none existed.
- **"[[Texas v. White]] gives the President a Reconstruction power."** Chase gives the President only a wartime, commander-in-chief power to install provisional governments; the guarantee power belongs to Congress.
- **"Chase simply followed [[Luther v. Borden|Luther]]."** Chase relies on Luther for congressional primacy while deciding Texas's status himself, and the dissenters' point is that Luther should have controlled all the way down.
- **"The two theories of Reconstruction are interchangeable."** If the States left, the South was conquered territory governed and readmitted under Art. IV, § 3; if they never left, Reconstruction restores lawful government under the guarantee. [[Texas v. White]] picks the second and makes Art. IV, § 4 the engine of the program.
- **"The guarantee only empowers Congress."** "[I]ndestructible States" and the statement that "the people of each State compose a State, having its own government" also limit what Congress may do in the guarantee's name. See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]] and [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].
