## Rule statement

Article IV § 4 obliges the United States to guarantee to every State in the Union a republican form of government, to protect each State against invasion, and to protect each against domestic violence when the State asks. [[Texas v. White]] makes the guarantee an affirmative source of federal power and not merely a promise: suppressing a rebellion rests on "the power to suppress insurrection and carry on war," while re-establishing a State's constitutional relations rests on "the obligation of the United States to guarantee to every State in the Union a republican form of government," which Chase calls "a necessary complement to the former." That power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress," so a government the President installs in an occupied district is a war measure and "must, therefore, be considered as provisional." The competing position is [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]]'s: that the Constitution gives neither the President nor Congress any power to act against a State, so the guarantee has no remedy behind it.

## Elements

1. Ask whether the entity claiming the guarantee is a State "in this Union." The clause runs to States, which is why [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]]'s July 4, 1861 version of the argument is circular: a State lawfully out of the Union is not a State to which the guarantee runs.
2. Identify which of the clause's three obligations is engaged, because they operate differently: the guarantee of a republican form, protection against invasion, and protection against domestic violence, the last conditioned on a request from the State's legislature, or from its executive when the legislature cannot be convened.
3. For the republican-form branch, identify the defect in the State's government that federal action is meant to cure. In [[Texas v. White]] the defect was that no lawful government could reconstitute itself: emancipation had changed the body politic, since "[t]he new freemen . . . [were] part of the people" and "the people still constituted the State; for States, like individuals, retain their identity, though changed to some extent in their constituent elements," and no election could be held under the old constitution.
4. Allocate the power. Congress is primary, because carrying the guarantee into effect is legislative; the President may act only while the war lasts, only in insurgent districts occupied by national forces, and only under his commander-in-chief functions, which makes his governments provisional and subject to congressional supersession.
5. Test the means as under the Necessary and Proper Clause. Chase applies [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] directly: "a discretion in the choice of means is necessary allowed. It is essential only that the means must be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the power conferred." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
6. Check whether a different clause supplies the power more cleanly before relying on the guarantee. Against individuals resisting federal law, Art. I, § 8, cl. 15 authorizes calling forth the militia "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections"; against a conquered territory, the Territories Clause of Art. IV, § 3 would supply plenary power — but only on the premise that the States left, which [[Texas v. White]] rejects.
7. Ask who decides. The enforcement question is routed to the political branches, and the fight in [[Texas v. White]] is over whether a court may decide any part of it for itself.

## Standard of review / burden

There is no judicially administrable standard, and the readings do not supply one: no case here defines what a "Republican Form of Government" substantively requires, and the clause's enforcement has been treated as belonging to the political branches since Luther v. Borden (1849), which held that which government is the lawful government of a State is for Congress and the President. That is the political-question route, and it is the answer to give first. [[Texas v. White]] both relies on Luther — it is the authority for Congress's primacy over restoration — and departs from it, because Chase decides for himself that the Reconstruction government is Texas's lawful government for purposes of the suit. Grier's dissent would apply Luther all the way down: "Is Texas one of these United States? . . . This is to be decided as a political fact, not as a legal fiction. This court is bound to know and notice the public history of the nation," and "I am not disposed to join in any essay to prove Texas to be a State of the Union, when Congress have decided that she is not"; Swayne and Miller, agreeing on the merits, treat the status question as one "in relation to which this court is bound by the action of the legislative department of the government." What fills the gap left by nonjusticiability is congressional judgment, reviewed for nothing more than whether the means chosen are necessary and proper to the guarantee, plus the political checks that operate on Congress and the President — which is why the Reconstruction collision was resolved by the Reconstruction Acts and [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]] rather than by adjudication. The casebook's framing question for the whole unit sets the burden where the readings leave it: was Lincoln bound "by his oath of office and the Take Care Clause" to put down the rebellion, "Or was President Buchanan right that secession was unconstitutional, but the President—and even Congress—could do little about it?"

## Exceptions

- The guarantee runs only to a State in the Union, so it cannot by itself establish that a seceding State remains one; used that way it assumes its own conclusion, which is the defect in the July 4, 1861 argument.
- Presidential action is a wartime exception, not a share of the guarantee power: temporary governments "within insurgent districts, occupied by the National forces" rest on the commander-in-chief function and expire with the war, and Congress treated them as provisional.
- The domestic-violence branch is not self-executing in the way the other two are; it operates on the State's application, through its legislature or, where the legislature cannot be convened, its executive.
- Where federal law can be executed against individuals, the guarantee is unnecessary: [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]]'s program was to "hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government and to collect the duties and imposts," which needs only the militia and execution powers.
- Buchanan's impossibility carve-out: where every federal officer in a State has resigned — "We no longer have a district judge, a district attorney, or a marshal in South Carolina" — the Take Care duty cannot be performed there, and Lincoln concedes the point ("no attempt to force obnoxious strangers among the people").
- Not decided in these readings: what a republican form of government substantively requires; whether any Guarantee Clause claim is ever justiciable; whether Congress's power under the clause reaches conditions on readmission and on suffrage; and how a court would treat a State that kept a lawful government but was denied its senators, which is the expelled-State hypothetical the Lincoln notes leave open.

## Leading case

[[Texas v. White]]

- Holds that restoring a rebel State's relations to the Union is an exercise of the Guarantee Clause power, and that this power "is primarily a legislative power, and resides in Congress."
- The fact that drove it is that the plaintiff was a government the President had created and Congress had later treated as provisional, so the Court could not decide whether Texas could sue without deciding whose power Reconstruction was.
- Chase pairs the two powers deliberately: suppression rests on war and insurrection, restoration on the guarantee, "a necessary complement to the former." The pairing is what converts a promise into an affirmative grant.
- Emancipation supplies the reason the clause was needed at all: the old government could not resume, because "[t]he new freemen . . . [were] part of the people" and no election could be held under the old constitution.
- The means test is [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s: "It is essential only that the means must be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the power conferred."
- The structural sentence to quote alongside it: "The Constitution, in all its provisions, looks to an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States" — the guarantee protects the States' governments, so it cannot be read as a licence to abolish them.
- Consequence of the contrary view, which is Chase's real argument: if the acts of secession had operated, "[t]he war must have ceased to be a war for the suppression of rebellion, and must have become a war for conquest and subjugation," and Reconstruction would run under the Territories Clause instead.

## Best counter-case

[[Buchanan, Address to Congress]]

- The counter-position is that the guarantee has no enforcement power behind it: secession is unconstitutional, but neither the President nor Congress may act, because a power "to coerce a State into submission" would be a power "to declare and to make war against a State," is not enumerated, and is not necessary and proper to any enumerated power.
- The distinguishing move is the Convention's rejection of coercion: Madison said force against a State "would look more like a declaration of war than an infliction of punishment," and the clause was postponed.
- The passage that makes Buchanan indispensable is the one that states the Reconstruction problem before it arose: "Suppose such a war should result in the conquest of a State; how are we to govern it afterwards? Shall we hold it as a province and govern it by despotic power?" [[Texas v. White]] is the answer — the State never left, so Congress restores rather than governs.
- Arguing for Buchanan: no clause authorizes war on a State as such; the guarantee is a duty owed to States, and duties owed to an entity are an odd source of power over it; and his prudential point is genuinely constitutional in effect — "our Union rests upon public opinion, and can never be cemented by the blood of its citizens shed in civil war."
- Arguing against Buchanan: he treats every federal response as war on a State, when the Constitution's route is enforcement against individuals, backed by Art. I, § 8, cl. 15 and by Art. IV, § 4's protection against domestic violence. His error was not his theory of the Union but his refusal to use the power over individuals that the Constitution does give.
- The hypothetical that exposes the gap: if South Carolina seizes the Charleston customs house, collecting duties is executing federal law against individuals, not coercing a State, so even on Buchanan's own theory the President could act.
- The contemporaneous verdict is worth having: the Republicans mocked the message as saying "no state could secede—unless it wants to—and that the president has a duty to enforce the laws—unless somebody opposes him."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Treating the Guarantee Clause as a source of individual rights or a judicially enforceable standard of democratic government. Nothing in these readings defines "republican form," and Luther v. Borden routes enforcement to the political branches.
- Using Lincoln's July 4, 1861 Guarantee Clause argument as his strongest. It is his weakest and his most useful: the [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] structure ("to prevent its going out, is an indispensable means, to the end, of maintaining the guarantee") assumes that the State is still in the Union, and its real value is that it supplies a textual hook for force where Buchanan said none existed.
- Reading [[Texas v. White]] as giving the President a Reconstruction power. Chase gives him only a wartime, commander-in-chief power to install temporary governments in occupied districts, expressly provisional; the guarantee power is Congress's.
- Saying Chase followed Luther v. Borden. He relies on it for congressional primacy while deciding Texas's status himself, and the dissenters' whole point is that Luther should have controlled all the way down.
- Confusing the two constitutional theories of Reconstruction. If the States left, the South was conquered territory governed under the Territories Clause of Art. IV, § 3 and readmitted under it; if they never left, Reconstruction is restoration under the guarantee. [[Texas v. White]] picks the second and thereby makes Art. IV § 4 the engine of the whole program.
- Treating the three obligations in Art. IV § 4 as one. The republican-form guarantee and protection against invasion run of their own force; protection against domestic violence depends on the State asking.
- Forgetting that the guarantee protects State governments as well as authorizing federal action. "[I]ndestructible States" and the statement that "the people of each State compose a State, having its own government" limit what Congress may do in the name of guaranteeing a republican form. See [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]] and [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].
