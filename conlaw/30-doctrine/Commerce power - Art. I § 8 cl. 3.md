## Rule statement

Congress may regulate three things under Art. I, § 8, cl. 3: the use of the channels of interstate commerce, the instrumentalities of interstate commerce and persons or things in interstate commerce even against purely intrastate threats, and activities that "substantially affect" interstate commerce ([[United States v. Lopez]]). Within the third category the activity must be economic — "the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities" — and it may then be aggregated, so that a contribution "trivial by itself" counts when "taken together with that of many others similarly situated" ([[Wickard v. Filburn]]; [[Gonzales v. Raich]]); noneconomic intrastate activity is reached only where its regulation is "an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity, in which the regulatory scheme could be undercut unless the intrastate activity were regulated" ([[United States v. Lopez]]). Two outer limits survive: the chain from activity to commerce may not be so attenuated that the Court must "pile inference upon inference," and the power "presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated," so Congress may not compel a person outside a market to enter it ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]). The doctrinal formulation is contested at its root: [[United States v. Lopez]] treats the substantial-effects category as Commerce Clause doctrine, Scalia in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] relocates it to the Necessary and Proper Clause, and Thomas would return "commerce" to founding-era trade — "selling, buying, and bartering, as well as transporting for these purposes" — which is also Jefferson's line in [[The Bank Debate]] between external commerce and commerce "between citizen and citizen."

## Elements

1. **Identify the category.** Channels (a ban on shipping goods interstate, as in [[United States v. Darby]]); instrumentalities, persons or things in commerce; or substantial effects. Categories one and two dispose of most cases without any effects inquiry, because "the shipment of manufactured goods interstate is such commerce, and the prohibition of such shipment by Congress is indubitably a regulation of the commerce" ([[United States v. Darby]]).
2. **In category three, ask whether the regulated activity is economic.** Growing a commodity for home use is ([[Wickard v. Filburn]]); cultivating and consuming a Schedule I drug is ([[Gonzales v. Raich]]); possessing a gun near a school "is in no sense an economic activity" ([[United States v. Lopez]]).
3. **Check the four Lopez factors.**
   - whether the activity is economic in character;
   - whether the statute contains a jurisdictional element ensuring "through case-by-case inquiry" a tie to commerce in each prosecution;
   - whether Congress made findings, which are not required but help where the effect is not "visible to the naked eye";
   - how attenuated the chain from the activity to interstate commerce is.
4. **If the activity is economic, aggregate it and ask whether the class substantially affects interstate commerce.** The class is drawn by the statute's own scheme, and "the de minimis character of individual instances arising under that statute is of no consequence" ([[Gonzales v. Raich]]).
5. **If the activity is not economic, ask whether it is an essential part of a larger regulation of an interstate market.** Congress must be regulating a market, and the intrastate measure must be one without which "the regulatory scheme could be undercut" — a "gaping hole" ([[Gonzales v. Raich]]).
6. **Confirm that the target is activity, not inactivity.** "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated"; being predictably a future participant is not enough, because the Clause "is not a general license to regulate an individual from cradle to grave" ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]).
7. **Run the Necessary and Proper check separately.** A measure may be "necessary" and still not "proper" if it "vests Congress with the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]), applying [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s requirement that means "consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
8. **Check the fallback powers if the commerce theory fails.** The taxing power "reaches every subject" and can burden what the commerce power cannot ([[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]]), subject to the tax/penalty line of [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]; see [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]] and [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].

## Standard of review / burden

The challenger bears the burden, and within category three the Court asks only whether "a 'rational basis' exists" for concluding that the class of activities, aggregated, substantially affects interstate commerce; it does not require proof of effects in fact ([[Gonzales v. Raich]]). Motive and purpose are not reviewable: "Whatever their motive and purpose, regulations of commerce which do not infringe some constitutional prohibition are within the plenary power conferred on Congress" ([[United States v. Darby]]), which retires the pretext inquiry [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]] had run and leaves [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s pretext reservation alive only in the taxing cases ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]). Rational-basis deference does not survive the threshold questions: whether the activity is economic, whether it is activity at all, and whether the means are "proper" are decided by the Court, and "simply because Congress may conclude that a particular activity substantially affects interstate commerce does not necessarily make it so." As against the States as States there is no separate judicially enforced immunity for "traditional governmental functions"; the protection "is one of process rather than one of result," lying in state control of electoral qualifications, the Electoral College and equal representation in the Senate ([[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]) — the closest thing in this field to a political-question answer, and expressly reserving what "affirmative limits the constitutional structure might impose." The Tenth Amendment adds nothing to the enumeration itself: it "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered" ([[United States v. Darby]]), so the whole question is whether the power was granted. On the negative side of the Clause, "extreme caution" governs before a court displaces a democratically adopted state law, and relief is given only "where the infraction is clear" ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]).

## Exceptions

- Inactivity. Congress may not "order individuals to engage in" commerce; the uninsured who buy nothing are not "active in the market" in any pertinent sense, and a person who "bought a car two years ago and may buy another in the future is not 'active in the car market'" ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]).
- Noneconomic intrastate activity standing alone, with no larger market scheme to protect and no jurisdictional element ([[United States v. Lopez]]).
- Measures that are not "proper" because they create rather than execute an enumerated power, or because they break with structural principles of state sovereignty ([[NFIB v. Sebelius]]; Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich]], reading [[Printz v. United States]]).
- Commandeering the States to govern, as distinct from regulating them alongside private parties, which [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] permits; see [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- State law never shrinks the power: it "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or nonexercise of state power" ([[United States v. Darby]]), so California's Compassionate Use Act did not carve out state-authorized medical users ([[Gonzales v. Raich]]).
- On the dormant side, there is no "almost per se" rule against state laws with extraterritorial practical effects, and a State may condition in-state sale of an ordinary good on how it was produced elsewhere absent purposeful discrimination ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]); the parallel protection for out-of-state citizens runs through Art. IV, § 2 ([[Corfield v. Coryell]]) and [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]]. See [[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]].
- Not decided: whether the post-Lopez jurisdictional hook reaching a firearm "that has moved in or that otherwise affects interstate commerce" is valid, since the Supreme Court has not taken it up.
- Not decided: whether re-enacting a noneconomic prohibition inside a comprehensive national scheme would save it — O'Connor's charge in [[Gonzales v. Raich]] that Lopez becomes "nothing more than a drafting guide" is a prediction, not a holding.
- Not decided: whether Pike balancing survives as an independent test, since no majority in [[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]] would either abandon it or apply it.

## Leading case

[[United States v. Lopez]]

- Holds the Gun-Free School Zones Act beyond the commerce power, the first federal statute struck down on that ground since the New Deal, and states the three categories that organize the modern doctrine.
- The fact that drove it is the statutory verb: § 922(q) banned simple **possession**, so the Act "neither regulates a commercial activity nor contains a requirement that the possession be connected in any way to interstate commerce."
- The government's chain ran gun to violent crime to insurance costs and deterred travel to impaired education to national productivity; accepting it would leave the Court "hard pressed to posit any activity by an individual that Congress is without power to regulate," and would reach family law and school curricula.
- No jurisdictional element, unlike the felon-in-possession statute the Court had construed in United States v. Bass, and no congressional findings.
- Rehnquist overrules nothing: [[Wickard v. Filburn]] "involved economic activity in a way that the possession of a gun in a school zone does not," and Kennedy's concurrence says the New Deal cases "are not called in question by our decision today."
- Thomas's concurrence is the original-meaning benchmark: "commerce" meant trade "in contradistinction to productive activities such as manufacturing and agriculture," and a substantial-effects gloss makes the bankruptcy, coinage, post office, patent and piracy powers surplusage — "The power we have accorded Congress has swallowed Art. I, § 8."
- Worth quoting against any effects theory: the Court must preserve a "distinction between what is truly national and what is truly local," and it declines "to pile inference upon inference."

## Best counter-case

[[Gonzales v. Raich]]

- Decided ten years after Lopez, 6–3, sustaining the Controlled Substances Act against two Californians who grew or obtained marijuana solely for their own medical use, never bought or sold and never crossed a state line — facts at least as local as Lopez's.
- The distinguishing facts are three, and each is a lever on an exam: the activity is production and consumption of a commodity, so it is "quintessentially economic"; the CSA is a comprehensive "closed regulatory system" regulating an interstate market, so the intrastate ban protects a larger scheme; and marijuana is fungible and divertible, so homegrown supply "is never more than an instant from the interstate market" (Scalia).
- Arguing for federal power: [[Wickard v. Filburn]] controls, because home-consumed supply "supplies a need of the man who grew it which would otherwise be reflected by purchases in the open market"; Congress rationally feared that exempting homegrown marijuana "would leave a gaping hole in the CSA"; and under [[United States v. Darby]] state authorization cannot shrink the federal power.
- Arguing against: the class should be drawn by "objective markers" already used in federal and state law, which makes the relevant conduct personal medical cultivation and use, "a distinct and separable subclass" (Thomas) whose exemption would not undercut the interstate ban; the government proved no effect on the national market, and "[p]iling assertion upon assertion does not . . . satisfy the substantiality test" (O'Connor); and defining economics to cover any private substitute for a market purchase — home care for daycare, gardening for the supermarket — "is to draw no line at all."
- The structural objection is the one to state out loud: letting Congress immunize local regulation "by packaging regulation of local activity in broader schemes, is tantamount to removing meaningful limits on the Commerce Clause," and makes Lopez "nothing more than a drafting guide" (O'Connor).
- The older counter-case on the other side is [[Hammer v. Dagenhart]], overruled by [[United States v. Darby]]: it drew a categorical line (production is local, and the goods were "of themselves harmless") and ran a pretext inquiry into the Act's "necessary effect." Holmes's dissent is the argument that won — "Regulation means the prohibition of something," and the states have no right in goods once "they seek to send their products across the State line."
- [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] is the counter-case going the other direction from Raich: five Justices held that the commerce power does not reach the inactive, and that a mandate is not a "proper" means even if necessary, while the payment survived as a tax. Roberts's Wickard reductio is the cleanest test of how far aggregation goes — price can be supported by raising demand, so the government's logic "would let Congress command that those not buying wheat do so."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Reciting "substantially affects" and stopping. After [[United States v. Lopez]] the first question is whether the activity is economic; the effects inquiry is only reached for economic activity or for a noneconomic measure embedded in a larger market scheme.
- Treating [[Wickard v. Filburn]] as holding that anything with an economic ripple is reachable. Filburn was growing a commodity under a statute regulating the volume of that commodity in commerce; the Court expressly noted "there is no decision of this Court that such activities may be regulated where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce," and rested on substitution plus aggregation, not on ripples.
- Saying Filburn ate all 239 bushels. The opinion says "[t]he intended disposition of the crop here involved has not been expressly stated"; the point is that the Act reached the wheat regardless of use.
- Confusing the Commerce Clause holding with the Necessary and Proper holding in [[United States v. Darby]]. The shipment ban is a channels regulation; the wage and hour rules for production "for commerce" are sustained as "appropriate means to the attainment of a legitimate end," which is [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s test in commerce clothing — and the means-end framing is what later supplies the "proper" limit.
- Citing the Tenth Amendment as an independent bar. It "states but a truism," and [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] rejects judicially defined immunity for "traditional governmental functions"; the live state-sovereignty limits are anti-commandeering and the "proper" requirement, which rest on structure.
- Arguing pretext or bad motive against a commerce statute. [[United States v. Darby]] makes motive "for the legislative judgment"; the pretext argument survives only in the taxing line ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]], where "the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions").
