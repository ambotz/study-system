## Rule statement

Congress may regulate commerce that crosses state lines, and local activity whose regulation is needed to reach that commerce, but not every activity with some economic ripple. [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]] sorts the power into three categories: channels, instrumentalities and things in commerce, and activities that "substantially affect interstate commerce." In the third category the activity must be economic — "the production, distribution, and consumption of commodities" ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]) — and may then be aggregated ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]]); noneconomic activity is reached only as "an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity" (Lopez). Nor does the power reach inactivity, because it "presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated" (Roberts, C.J., in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]). The contest runs to the root: Scalia in Raich moves the third category to the Necessary and Proper Clause, while Thomas would confine "commerce" to trade, as Jefferson did in [[The Bank Debate]] by excluding commerce "between citizen and citizen."

## Elements

1. **Does the law regulate the channels of interstate commerce?** A ban on shipping goods across state lines qualifies whatever its motive, because "the shipment of manufactured goods interstate is such commerce, and the prohibition of such shipment by Congress is indubitably a regulation of the commerce" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]).
   - Lopez also files *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* in this category.
2. **Does the law protect the instrumentalities of commerce, or persons or things moving in it?** Category two reaches intrastate threats to interstate carriers and goods, as in the *Shreveport Rate Case*, where Congress reached the intrastate rates of interstate railroads.
   - A firearm that "has moved in" interstate commerce is a category-two hook; whether past movement suffices forever is open (see Exceptions).
3. **If neither, is the regulated activity economic?**
   - Economic: growing a commodity for home use ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]]); cultivating and consuming a Schedule I drug ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]).
   - Not economic: possessing a gun near a school, which "is in no sense an economic activity" ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]]); gender-motivated violence (*United States v. Morrison*, in the Lopez notes).
   - The statutory verb often decides: a ban on the *sale* of guns near schools would very likely fall on the Wickard side of the line.
4. **Do the remaining Lopez factors support the link to commerce?**
   - Jurisdictional element: a clause ensuring "through case-by-case inquiry" that each prosecuted instance is tied to commerce.
   - Findings: not required, and helpful where the effect is not "visible to the naked eye," but in *Morrison* extensive findings "made no difference" (casebook note).
   - Attenuation: each added link in the chain (gun, crime, insurance costs, learning, national productivity) is where "pile inference upon inference" bites.
   - Traditional state concern: Kennedy's concurrence asks "whether the exercise of national power seeks to intrude upon an area of traditional state concern," such as family law or school curricula.
5. **If the activity is economic, does the class, aggregated, substantially affect interstate commerce?** The statute's own scheme draws the class, and "the de minimis character of individual instances arising under that statute is of no consequence" ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]).
   - Aggregation: a contribution "trivial by itself" counts when "taken together with that of many others similarly situated" ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]]).
   - Congress need not carve out a subclass (medical, state-authorized, homegrown) if including it was rational.
6. **If the activity is not economic, is the measure an essential part of a larger regulation of an interstate market?** Congress must be regulating a market, and exempting the local activity must threaten to leave "a gaping hole" in the scheme (Raich). With no scheme to protect, the case routes back to Lopez.
   - Lopez's formulation: "an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity, in which the regulatory scheme could be undercut unless the intrastate activity were regulated."
7. **Does the law target activity rather than inactivity?** "The power to regulate commerce presupposes the existence of commercial activity to be regulated," and predictable future participation is not enough, because the Clause "is not a general license to regulate an individual from cradle to grave" (Roberts, C.J., in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]).
8. **Is the measure a "proper" means?** A law may be necessary and still improper if it "vests Congress with the extraordinary ability to create the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]) or if it commandeers state officers ([[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]). See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
9. **If the commerce theory fails, does another power carry the law?** The taxing power "reaches every subject" and can burden what the commerce power cannot (the *License Tax Cases*, in [[Implied Limits on the Power to Tax]]), subject to the tax/penalty line of [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]. See [[Taxing power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 and direct taxes]] and [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Burden:** the challenger bears it.
- **Standard:** in category three the Court asks only whether "a 'rational basis' exists" for concluding that the class of activity, aggregated, substantially affects interstate commerce; it does not require proof of effects in fact ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]).
- **Standard — threshold questions:** whether the activity is economic, whether there is activity at all, and whether the means are "proper" are decided by the Court without deference; "[s]imply because Congress may conclude that a particular activity substantially affects interstate commerce does not necessarily make it so" (*United States v. Morrison*, quoting [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question — motive:** "Whatever their motive and purpose, regulations of commerce which do not infringe some constitutional prohibition are within the plenary power conferred on Congress by the Commerce Clause" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]). Darby retires the pretext inquiry of [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]]; [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s pretext reservation survives only in the taxing line ([[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question — States as States:** there is no judicially enforced immunity for "traditional governmental functions"; the protection "is one of process rather than one of result" ([[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]). Garcia borrows the political-question factors without declining jurisdiction, and it reserves "what affirmative limits the constitutional structure might impose."
- **What fills the gap:** the national political process — state control of electoral qualifications, the Electoral College and equal representation in the Senate (Garcia), and, as Jackson read [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] in Wickard, "effective restraints on its exercise must proceed from political rather than from judicial processes."

## Exceptions

- **Inactivity.** Congress may regulate commerce but may not "order individuals to engage in it," and a person who "bought a car two years ago and may buy another in the future is not 'active in the car market' in any pertinent sense" (Roberts, C.J., in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]). Five Justices agreed, but across two opinions, so no opinion of the Court states the rule.
- **Freestanding noneconomic regulation.** Noneconomic intrastate activity with no market scheme to protect and no jurisdictional element is outside the power ([[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]]).
- **Improper means.** A law that creates rather than executes a power, or that breaks with state sovereignty, fails the "proper" requirement even if necessary ([[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]]; Scalia's concurrence in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]], reading [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz v. United States]]).
- **Commandeering.** Congress may regulate the States alongside private employers ([[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]]) but may not command them to govern; see [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- **No state-law carve-out.** The commerce power "can neither be enlarged nor diminished by the exercise or nonexercise of state power" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]), so California's Compassionate Use Act exempted no one from the Controlled Substances Act ([[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]).
- **The negative side is a separate doctrine.** The Clause's implied limit on the States turns on discrimination and is enforced with "extreme caution" ([[National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023)|National Pork Producers Council v. Ross]]); see [[Dormant Commerce Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 3 and state discrimination]], and for discrimination against citizens [[Corfield v. Coryell (1823)|Corfield v. Coryell]] and [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]].
- **Not decided: the post-Lopez jurisdictional hook.** Amended § 922(q) reaches a firearm "that has moved in or that otherwise affects interstate commerce"; the courts of appeals have upheld it, the Supreme Court has not reviewed it, and Thomas has argued the hook "cannot be reconciled with Lopez" (*Alderman v. United States*, dissenting from denial of certiorari).
- **Not decided: re-enacting a Lopez-type ban inside a national scheme.** O'Connor's charge in [[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]] that Lopez becomes "nothing more than a drafting guide" is a prediction, not a holding; Scalia answers that the scheme route extends "only to those measures necessary to make the interstate regulation effective."
- **Not decided: whether NFIB's commerce ruling is holding or dictum.** Ginsburg says the tax holding made the commerce discussion unnecessary; Roberts says the saving construction was available only once the "most straightforward reading" failed; lower courts treat the ruling as binding in practice.

## Leading case

[[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]]

- **Holding:** the Gun-Free School Zones Act exceeds the commerce power (5–4, Rehnquist, C.J.) — the first federal statute struck down on that ground since the New Deal — and the opinion states the three categories that organize the modern doctrine.
- **Driving fact:** § 922(q) banned simple possession, so the Act "neither regulates a commercial activity nor contains a requirement that the possession be connected in any way to interstate commerce"; it had no jurisdictional element, unlike the statute in *United States v. Bass*, and no findings.
- **Reasoning — attenuation:** the government's chains (gun to violent crime to insurance costs and deterred travel; gun to impaired learning to national productivity) would leave the Court "hard pressed to posit any activity by an individual that Congress is without power to regulate," and would reach family law and school curricula. The Court must preserve a "distinction between what is truly national and what is truly local."
- **Reasoning — precedent:** Rehnquist overrules nothing. He reads [[NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp|Jones & Laughlin]], [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]] and [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]] as confirming "outer limits," says Wickard "involved economic activity in a way that the possession of a gun in a school zone does not," and takes from [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] that "[t]he enumeration presupposes something not enumerated."
- **Separate opinions — Kennedy (with O'Connor), concurring:** joins a "necessary though limited holding" and insists the New Deal cases "are not called in question by our decision today."
- **Separate opinions — Thomas, concurring:** "commerce" meant "selling, buying, and bartering, as well as transporting for these purposes," in contrast to manufacturing and agriculture. A substantial-effects gloss makes the bankruptcy, coinage, post office, patent and piracy powers surplusage: "The power we have accorded Congress has swallowed Art. I, § 8."
- **Separate opinions — Breyer (with Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg), dissenting:** would ask only whether Congress "could have had 'a rational basis'" for finding a significant cumulative effect, and says the economic/noneconomic line gives "controlling force to nomenclature," which Wickard forbade.
- **Critique — collective action:** more than forty States already banned guns near schools, so there was no interstate problem the States could not solve; the Virginia Plan would have given Congress power where "the separate States are incompetent," and the open question is whether the enumeration codifies that principle or replaces it.

## Best counter-case

[[Gonzales v. Raich (2005)|Gonzales v. Raich]]

- **Distinguishing line:** local activity is reachable when it is the production or consumption of a fungible commodity that a comprehensive federal scheme regulates in an interstate market, and not when it is freestanding noneconomic conduct outside any such scheme.
- **Result:** ten years after Lopez, the Court (6–3, Stevens, J.) sustained the Controlled Substances Act against two Californians who grew or obtained marijuana solely for their own medical use, never bought or sold it, and never crossed a state line — facts at least as local as Lopez's.
- **The three levers:** the activity is "quintessentially economic"; the CSA is a "closed regulatory system" governing an interstate market; and homegrown marijuana "is never more than an instant from the interstate market" (Scalia). Remove any lever and the case moves toward Lopez.
- **Argue for federal power:** [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]] controls, because home-consumed supply "supplies a need of the man who grew it which would otherwise be reflected by purchases in the open market"; Congress rationally feared "a gaping hole in the CSA"; and under [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] state authorization cannot shrink the power.
- **Argue against federal power:** draw the class by "objective markers" already used in federal and state law (O'Connor), making the conduct personal medical use, "a distinct and separable subclass" (Thomas) whose exemption would not undercut the interstate ban; the government proved no market effect, and "[p]iling assertion upon assertion does not . . . satisfy the substantiality test" (O'Connor).
- **Structural objection:** treating every private substitute for a market purchase as economic "is to draw no line at all," and letting Congress reach local conduct "by packaging regulation of local activity in broader schemes, is tantamount to removing meaningful limits on the Commerce Clause" (O'Connor).
- **The limit from the other side:** in [[NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)|NFIB v. Sebelius]], Roberts's Wickard reductio shows how far aggregation can go — price can be supported by raising demand, so the government's logic would let Congress "command that those not buying wheat do so" — and answers that Filburn "was at least actively engaged in the production of wheat." The joint dissent calls Wickard "the ne plus ultra of expansive Commerce Clause jurisprudence."
- **The historical counterpart:** [[Hammer v. Dagenhart (1918)|Hammer v. Dagenhart]], overruled by Darby, held the goods "of themselves harmless" and production local, following [[United States v. E.C. Knight Co|E.C. Knight]] ("Commerce succeeds to manufacture and is not a part of it"), and ran a pretext inquiry into the Act's "necessary effect." [[A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States|Schechter Poultry]] carried the "indirect effects" line into the New Deal.
- **The dissent that won:** Holmes in Hammer — "Regulation means the prohibition of something," and the States have no right in goods once "they seek to send their products across the State line."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The activity substantially affects commerce, so the law is valid."** After [[United States v. Lopez (1995)|United States v. Lopez]] the first question is whether the activity is economic; the effects inquiry is reached only for economic activity or for a noneconomic measure embedded in a market scheme.
- **"Wickard lets Congress reach anything with an economic ripple."** Filburn grew a commodity under a statute regulating the volume of that commodity in commerce, and [[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard v. Filburn]] conceded that "there is no decision of this Court that such activities may be regulated where no part of the product is intended for interstate commerce"; the holding rests on substitution plus aggregation.
- **"Filburn ate all 239 bushels."** The opinion says "[t]he intended disposition of the crop here involved has not been expressly stated"; the point is that the Act reached the excess wheat regardless of use.
- **"Darby is only a channels case."** The shipment ban is a channels regulation, but the wage and hour rules for production "for commerce" are sustained as "appropriate means to the attainment of a legitimate end" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]) — [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s test in commerce clothing, and the source of the later "proper" limit.
- **"The Tenth Amendment independently bars the law."** The Tenth Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered" ([[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]]), and [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority]] rejects immunity for "traditional governmental functions"; the live state-sovereignty limits are anti-commandeering and the "proper" requirement, which rest on structure.
- **"Congress's real motive was to regulate something local."** [[United States v. Darby (1941)|United States v. Darby]] makes motive a matter "for the legislative judgment"; the pretext argument survives only in the taxing line, where in [[Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co (1922)|Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co]] "the proof of the contrary is found on the very face of its provisions."
