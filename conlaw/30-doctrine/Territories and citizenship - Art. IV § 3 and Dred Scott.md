## Rule statement

Article IV, § 3, cl. 2 gives Congress power to "dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States," and the operative rule today is that this is a grant of legislative power over the territories, limited by the Constitution's guarantees of individual rights and not by any implied exception for a favored class of property. [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] held the opposite on both halves of this module, and both halves were repudiated by Article V: that no person of African descent whose ancestors were sold as slaves could be a "citizen" of a State or of the United States, and that Congress could not prohibit slavery in a territory because "the right of property in a slave is distinctly and expressly affirmed in the Constitution" and a law taking that property from a citizen who "came himself or brought his property into a particular Territory" "could hardly be dignified with the name of due process of law." The Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause adopts Curtis's birthright rule — "every free person born on the soil of a State, who is a citizen of that State by force of its Constitution or laws, is also a citizen of the United States" — and inverts Taney's ordering, so that national citizenship is primary and state citizenship follows residence; the Thirteenth Amendment removes the property premise of the territorial holding. What survives for examination purposes is the method: whether the Territories Clause is a grant of power to legislate, what limits the Bill of Rights imposes in a territory, and what authority a wrong constitutional decision has over the other branches.

## Elements

1. **Citizenship: ask what the law of citizenship was, not who was expected to hold it.** Curtis's chain is the model. (a) The Constitution's reference to "a natural-born citizen" "assumes that citizenship may be acquired by birth," on the public-law principle "which referred citizenship to the place of birth." (b) The naturalization power is "confined to the removal of the disabilities of foreign birth," so Congress has no power to disfranchise the native-born. (c) Persons recognized as citizens of a State at the founding became citizens of the new body — a premise Taney himself concedes. (d) Free Black men were citizens and voters in five States at the ratification of the Articles, so the class was included.
2. **Locate Congress's power over the territory, and say which clause you are using.**
   - *Territories Clause (Curtis, McLean, and modern law).* "[T]his is a grant of power to the Congress—that it is therefore necessarily a grant of power to legislate," and "[a]n enactment that slavery may or may not exist there, is a regulation respecting the territory."
   - *Implied power (Taney).* The clause covers only "the" territory held in 1787; power over later acquisitions is implied from the power to admit new States, because territory is acquired "to become a State, and not to be held as a colony," and the power to acquire "necessarily carries with it the power to preserve."
3. **Test the textual arguments against each other.** Taney's are the definite article, the pairing of territory with movable "property," the closing savings clause for existing claims, and the contrast between "needful rules and regulations" and the "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" granted over the seat of government. The answers are Curtis's (a grant to a legislature is a grant to legislate, and "it is necessarily left to the legislative discretion to determine whether a law be needful") and practice (the Northwest Ordinance re-enacted by the First Congress, and a Missouri Compromise then thirty-seven years old).
4. **Apply the individual-rights limits, which run in the territories.** Taney's own list is the doctrinal residue worth keeping: in a territory Congress could not establish a religion, abridge speech, deny the right "to keep and bear arms," or quarter soldiers. The contested step is the next one — reading the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause as a substantive bar on a class of legislation rather than as a guarantee of process.
5. **Watch for the symmetry point.** Nelson: "If Congress possesses the power, under the Constitution, to abolish slavery in a Territory, it must necessarily possess the like power to establish it." A power over the territory is a power in both directions, which is why the territorial question could not be a stable sectional compromise.
6. **For status on return, run the comity rule separately from the territorial question.** Under Strader v. Graham the returning person's status "depended on the laws of Missouri, and not of Illinois," because laws have "no inherent authority extra-territorially" and recognition is "purely from comity." Curtis keeps the two questions apart: what the territory's law did to the status, and whether the forum State recognizes it — and says a State may refuse recognition only through its statute or customary law, not on "political considerations," which are "functions of diplomatists and legislators, not of judges."
7. **Then ask what the decision binds.** Lincoln's distinction governs the aftermath: "Judicial decisions have two uses—first, to absolutely determine the case decided, and secondly, to indicate to the public how other similar cases will be decided when they arise." See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Standard of review / burden

- The Court gave complete deference on one question and none on the next, and the asymmetry is the case's best-hidden weakness: on what land belongs to the United States, "the political department of the Government shall recognize as within the limits of the United States, the judicial department is also bound to recognize"; on what Congress may do with that land, Taney reviewed de novo.
- The deference the dissenters would have applied is the ordinary rule for a legislative power: "[i]f Congress may establish a Territorial Government in the exercise of its discretion, it is a clear principle that a court cannot control that discretion" (McLean), and whether a rule is "needful" is a legislative judgment (Curtis).
- There is a jurisdictional bar before any of this. Having held the Circuit Court had no jurisdiction because Scott was not a citizen, the Court should have stopped; the modern rule is that a federal court establishes jurisdiction first and, finding none, may only dismiss (echoing [[Ex parte McCardle]]). Taney's answers are that the error was "patent on the record," that an uncorrected error "might be drawn into precedent," and — the strongest — that the territorial ruling is itself jurisdictional, since if the Missouri Compromise did not free Scott he remained a slave and so could not be a citizen.
- Taney's stated interpretive standard is fixed original meaning: the Constitution "speaks not only in the same words, but with the same meaning and intent with which it spoke when it came from the hands of its framers," and "[a]ny other rule of construction would abrogate the judicial character of this court, and make it the mere reflex of the popular opinion or passion of the day." Curtis's answer is that a standard is no protection when the history is wrong, and that once "the theoretical opinions of individuals are allowed to control its meaning," "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men."
- Outside the courts, the standard is precedential weight rather than review. Lincoln's criteria for when a decision "should control . . . the general policy of the country": unanimity, absence of "apparent partisan bias," accordance "with legal public expectation, and with the steady practice of the departments throughout our history," a basis not "in any part, based on assumed historical facts which are not really true," or else reaffirmation "through a course of years." Dred Scott failed every one.

## Exceptions

- **Fugitives.** The Missouri Compromise's own proviso allowed reclamation of fugitives, and the Fugitive Slave Clause would have governed a person who fled into free territory rather than being taken there — the standard fact-variation that removes the free-soil claim entirely.
- **A judgment rather than mere residence.** Had Illinois adjudicated the status before the return, Full Faith and Credit would have posed a different and much stronger question than comity does; Missouri's freedom to apply its own law runs to another State's *law*, not to its *judgment*.
- **What the political branches recognize as United States territory** is not reviewable; the courts follow the political department on the extent of the national domain.
- **Overseas possessions.** Taney's premise that territory "is acquired to become a State, and not to be held as a colony" was set aside for overseas possessions in the Insular Cases, so the modern law of the territories does not rest on the New States Clause route he built.
- **The acquisition question itself was never settled.** Jefferson's own doubts about the Louisiana Purchase are the enumerated-powers problem Taney answers by implication from the New States Clause — see [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
- **The judgment binds even where the opinion does not.** Lincoln accepted that Scott himself remained a slave by the judgment — "We do not propose that when Dred Scott has been decided to be a slave by the court, we, as a mob, will decide him to be free" — while denying the decision's force "as a political rule." The departmentalist carve-out is about the rule, not the decree.
- **Congress acted on that carve-out.** In 1862 Congress banned slavery in all the territories and Lincoln signed the bill, doing exactly what Dred Scott said Congress could not do; in 1870 the Senate seated Hiram Revels despite Article I, § 3's requirement of nine years' citizenship, which required treating the citizenship holding as never having been law (or the Citizenship Clause as declaratory of a citizenship that already existed).

## Leading case

[[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]

- Holds (7–2) that descendants of imported slaves, "whether emancipated or not," were "not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word 'citizens' in the Constitution," so Scott could not invoke diversity jurisdiction; and, going on after finding no jurisdiction, that the Missouri Compromise's prohibition of slavery "is not warranted by the Constitution, and is therefore void."
- The fact that drove the citizenship holding is the shape of the plea in abatement: it rested only on Scott's African descent and his ancestors' enslavement, so, as Curtis says, "[i]f any such person can be a citizen, this plaintiff has the right to the judgment of the court that he is so." The question could not be narrowed.
- The fact that drove the territorial holding is Fort Snelling: Emerson held Scott where the Compromise prohibited slavery, which put the statute's validity in issue, and consented to Scott's marriage there, which Curtis treats as "the most effectual abandonment of the legal rights of a master over his slave."
- The fact that drove the Illinois holding is the Missouri Supreme Court's 1852 reversal of its own twenty-eight years of precedent; on Nelson's reasoning, a Missouri court following its old cases would have freed Scott.
- The territorial holding does less work than it appears to. Taney finds a congressional power over acquired territory anyway, implied from the New States Clause, so the case turns on the due process limit, not on the Territories Clause.
- The due process move is the one to be able to attack: the clause permits deprivations of property *with* due process, and Taney converts it into a substantive bar on a category of legislation. Curtis's Cuba hypothetical is the cleanest refutation — if Congress may free slaves brought into the United States under its commerce power without violating the Fifth Amendment, "how can a similar regulation respecting a Territory violate the fifth amendment?"
- Taney names the remedy the country used: "If any of its provisions are deemed unjust, there is a mode prescribed in the instrument itself by which it may be amended." See [[Amendment process - Art. V]].
- The sentence worth quoting against the method, from Curtis: "Political reasons have not the requisite certainty to afford rules of juridical interpretation. They are different in different men. They are different in the same men at different times."

## Best counter-case

[[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]

- The reading that cuts the other way is not another case but the political branches' answer: Lincoln's Springfield speech of 1857 and the 1858 debates, in which he accepts the judgment against Scott and denies the decision's authority "as a political rule, which shall be binding on the voter to vote for nobody who thinks it wrong, which shall be binding on the members of Congress or the President to favor no measure that does not actually concur with the principles of that decision."
- The distinguishing fact is the one Lincoln keeps pressing: nobody proposed to free Scott. "[W]ho resists it? Who has, in spite of the decision, declared Dred Scott free, and resisted the authority of his master over him?" The program was "we shall do what we can to have it to over-rule this" — opposing a precedent, not disobeying a decree.
- **Arguing Lincoln's side:** judicial review does not entail judicial supremacy, since Marbury's logic is only that a court deciding a case must prefer the Constitution to a statute; the two uses of a decision come apart, and the second is a question of precedential weight; and his criteria for a settled decision are neutral and would bind him to a decision he disliked. Note how much he concedes — a decision "fully settled" should control "the general policy of the country, subject to be disturbed only by amendments of the Constitution," and "[m]ore than this would be revolution."
- **Arguing Douglas's side:** the decision of "the highest judicial tribunal on earth" "becomes the law of the land, binding on you, on me, and on every other good citizen whether we like it or not"; "[w]hat are important courts worth unless their decisions are binding on all good citizens?"; and the consistency challenge that is his best point — "I want to know whether he is not bound to a decision which is contrary to his opinions just as much as to one in accordance with his opinions." Douglas's weakest moves are equating criticism with mob rule ("Will he appeal to a mob?") and assuming the conclusion when he says "a Senator has no right to interfere with the decision of judicial tribunals."
- **The fact that flips it:** move from opposing the rule to blocking the decree, and Lincoln's own principle condemns you — which is how a departmentalist distinguishes Faubus in [[Cooper v. Aaron]] without adopting Douglas.
- **Where the argument actually landed:** Congress banned territorial slavery in 1862 and the President signed, Fessenden voting "on what I understand to be the law of right"; the Senate seated Revels in 1870; and the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments then settled both holdings formally. Douglas's "I know of but one mode of reversing judicial decisions, and that is by appealing from the inferior to the superior court" is answered by the other modes the Constitution supplies: elections, appointments, new cases, and Article V.
- Runner-up counter-authority within the case itself: Curtis's dissent, which beats Taney on Taney's own originalist ground and refuses to reach the merits at all — "A great question of constitutional law, deeply affecting the peace and welfare of the country, is not, in my opinion, a fit subject to be thus reached."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Treating any part of [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] as live authority.** The citizenship holding is reversed by the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause and the property premise by the Thirteenth. Note that the Citizenship Clause does more than overrule a holding: Taney's premise was that a State cannot make a national citizen, and the clause inverts that, making national citizenship primary and state citizenship follow from residence.
- **Saying the Missouri Compromise holding was "just dictum" without arguing it.** Each part purports to be part of the opinion of the Court and a majority joined it; Taney's strongest answer is that the territorial ruling is an alternative jurisdictional ground, since a slave could not be a citizen. The dictum answer is Curtis's — nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" into a judgment of dismissal. Note the parallel problem in [[Marbury v. Madison]], which decided the right and the remedy before finding no jurisdiction; the difference to argue is that Marbury's surplus invalidated nothing.
- **Saying Taney held that Congress has no power over the territories.** He held the Territories Clause reached only the land held in 1787 and then found the power anyway, implied from the New States Clause, so the holding that voided the statute is the due process one. Remember Nelson's symmetry while you are there: a power to exclude slavery from a territory is equally a power to establish it.
- **Treating "no rights which the white man was bound to respect" as an aside or as Taney's own view stated in passing.** It is Taney's account of founding-era opinion, offered as proof of what "citizens" meant, and so it is the premise of the citizenship holding — and Curtis's evidence shows it was false as history.
- **Concluding that originalism produced the decision.** Both opinions claim the 1787 understanding. Taney reasons from expected applications and from the framers' conduct, against general words he concedes "would seem to embrace the whole human family"; Curtis reasons from the legal meaning of "natural-born citizen" and "naturalization" and from who was in fact a citizen and voter in 1787, and wins on Taney's own conceded premise. See [[Modes of constitutional argument]].
- **Conflating Lincoln's departmentalism with defiance of a judgment.** He accepted the decree as to Scott; the question whether the executive may refuse a judgment arises only later, in [[Ex parte Merryman]]. The companion error in a fact pattern is collapsing the two status questions: what the territory's law did to the person, and whether the forum State recognizes it, are separate, and Taney and Nelson resolve the case on the second alone.
