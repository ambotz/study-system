## Rule statement

Congress governs federal territory, and on the better reading the Territories Clause is itself a grant of legislative power, including power to exclude slavery. The clause gives Congress power to "dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States." [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]] held that no descendant of imported slaves could be a "citizen," and that Congress could not bar slavery in a territory because "the right of property in a slave is distinctly and expressly affirmed in the Constitution." Article V reversed both holdings: the Thirteenth Amendment removed the property premise, and the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause adopted Curtis's birthright rule and made national citizenship primary. The live contests are Taney's originalism against Curtis's on the meaning of "citizen," the Territories Clause as a grant of power against Taney's implied-power route, and what authority a wrong decision holds over the other branches.

## Elements

1. **Who is a citizen: what did the law of citizenship provide, rather than who was expected to hold it?**
   - Curtis: the Constitution's reference to "a natural-born citizen" "assumes that citizenship may be acquired by birth," on the public-law principle "which referred citizenship to the place of birth."
   - Curtis: the naturalization power is "confined to the removal of the disabilities of foreign birth," so Congress has no power to disfranchise the native-born.
   - Taney concedes that everyone recognized as a state citizen at the founding became a citizen of the new national body.
   - Curtis shows that free Black men were citizens, and voters, in five States at the ratification of the Articles, so Taney's own concession answers the question.
   - The casebook reads Taney's citizenship holding as driven by Article IV: he could not accept free Black citizens claiming privileges and immunities in other States. See [[Privileges and immunities - Art. IV § 2 and state discrimination]].
2. **Where does Congress's power to acquire the territory come from?**
   - Jefferson doubted that any enumerated power allowed the Louisiana Purchase and thought an amendment was needed: "let us not make it a blank paper by construction."
   - The casebook's alternative sources are the treaty power plus appropriations, or acquisition as a "great substantive and independent power."
   - Jefferson went ahead anyway, later defending action beyond the written law on grounds of necessity. See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
3. **Which clause gives Congress power to govern the territory?**
   - **Territories Clause (Curtis, McLean):** "[T]his is a grant of power to the Congress—that it is therefore necessarily a grant of power to legislate," and "[a]n enactment that slavery may or may not exist there, is a regulation respecting the territory."
   - **Implied power (Taney):** the clause covers only the territory held in 1787; power over later acquisitions is implied from the New States Clause, because territory is acquired "to become a State, and not to be held as a colony," and the power to acquire "necessarily carries with it the power to preserve."
4. **Which textual arguments win?**
   - Taney's arguments: the definite article ("the" territory); the pairing of territory with movable "property"; the closing savings clause for existing claims; and the contrast between "needful rules and regulations" and "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" over the seat of government.
   - Curtis's answer: a grant to a legislature is a grant to legislate, and "it is necessarily left to the legislative discretion to determine whether a law be needful."
   - The answer from practice: the Northwest Ordinance, re-enacted by the First Congress, and a Missouri Compromise then thirty-seven years old.
5. **Which individual-rights limits bind Congress in a territory?**
   - Taney's list is the residue worth keeping: in a territory Congress could not establish a religion, abridge speech, deny the right "to keep and bear arms," or quarter soldiers.
   - The contested step is reading the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause as a substantive bar on a class of legislation rather than a guarantee of process.
6. **Does the power run in both directions?**
   - Nelson: "If Congress possesses the power, under the Constitution, to abolish slavery in a Territory, it must necessarily possess the like power to establish it." A territorial power could therefore never be a stable sectional compromise.
7. **What happens to a person's status on return to a State?**
   - Under *Strader v. Graham* the returning person's status "depended on the laws of Missouri, and not of Illinois," because laws have "no inherent authority extra-territorially" and recognition is "purely from comity."
   - Curtis separates two questions: what the territory's law did to the person's status, and whether the forum State recognizes that status. A State may refuse recognition through its own statute or customary law, but not on "political considerations," which are "functions of diplomatists and legislators, not of judges."
8. **What does the decision bind?**
   - Lincoln: "Judicial decisions have two uses—first, to absolutely determine the case decided, and secondly, to indicate to the public how other similar cases will be decided when they arise." See [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Deference on the extent of the territory:** Taney defers entirely: "whatever the political department of the Government shall recognize as within the limits of the United States, the judicial department is also bound to recognize."
- **No deference on the use of the territory:** on what Congress may do there, Taney reviews de novo. The asymmetry is the opinion's best-hidden weakness.
- **The dissenters' standard:** "[i]f Congress may establish a Territorial Government in the exercise of its discretion, it is a clear principle that a court cannot control that discretion" (McLean), and whether a rule is "needful" is a legislative judgment (Curtis).
- **Jurisdiction first:** having held that Scott was not a citizen, the Court arguably had to stop. The modern rule is that a federal court finding no jurisdiction may only dismiss (*Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, echoing [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]]).
- **Taney's answers on jurisdiction:** the error was "patent on the record"; an uncorrected error "might be drawn into precedent"; and, most strongly, the territorial ruling is itself jurisdictional, since if the Compromise did not free Scott he remained a slave and could not be a citizen.
- **Taney's interpretive standard:** the Constitution "speaks not only in the same words, but with the same meaning and intent with which it spoke when it came from the hands of its framers," and any other rule would make the Court "the mere reflex of the popular opinion or passion of the day."
- **Curtis's reply:** once "the theoretical opinions of individuals are allowed to control its meaning," "we have no longer a Constitution; we are under the government of individual men."
- **Weight outside the courts:** Lincoln's test for when a decision should control national policy is unanimity, no "apparent partisan bias," accord "with legal public expectation, and with the steady practice of the departments throughout our history," and no basis in "assumed historical facts which are not really true," or else reaffirmation "through a course of years." Dred Scott failed every criterion.

## Exceptions

- **Fugitives:** the Missouri Compromise itself provided that a fugitive escaping into the territory "may be lawfully reclaimed." A person who fled into free territory, rather than being taken there, has no free-soil claim.
- **A judgment rather than mere residence:** had Illinois adjudicated Scott free before his return, the Full Faith and Credit Clause would have posed a stronger question than comity does (casebook Note 2c). Missouri's freedom to apply its own law runs to another State's law, not to another State's judgment.
- **Recognition of territory:** what the political branches recognize as United States territory is not reviewable.
- **Overseas possessions:** the Insular Cases (1901) held that the Constitution does not apply in all its particulars to territories populated "by alien races," setting aside Taney's premise that territory is acquired "to become a State, and not to be held as a colony."
- **The judgment as against the opinion:** Lincoln accepted that Scott himself remained a slave by the judgment ("We do not propose that when Dred Scott has been decided to be a slave by the court, we, as a mob, will decide him to be free") while denying the decision's force "as a political rule."
- **The political branches acted on that line:** in 1862 Congress banned slavery in all the territories and Lincoln signed the bill. In 1870 the Senate seated Hiram Revels despite Article I, § 3's nine-year citizenship rule, which required either treating the citizenship holding as never having been law or treating the Citizenship Clause as declaratory of existing citizenship.

## Leading case

[[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]

- **Holding on citizenship:** 7–2 (Taney, C.J.), descendants of imported slaves, "whether emancipated or not," were "not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word 'citizens' in the Constitution," so Scott could not invoke diversity jurisdiction.
- **Holding on the territories:** going on after finding no jurisdiction, the Court held that the Missouri Compromise's prohibition of slavery "is not warranted by the Constitution, and is therefore void."
- **Driving fact, citizenship:** the plea in abatement rested only on Scott's African descent and his ancestors' enslavement, so, as Curtis says, "[i]f any such person can be a citizen, this plaintiff has the right to the judgment of the court that he is so."
- **Driving fact, territories:** Emerson held Scott at Fort Snelling, where the Compromise prohibited slavery, and consented to his marriage there, which Curtis treats as conclusive: "there can be no more effectual abandonment of the legal rights of a master over his slave."
- **Driving fact, Illinois:** the Missouri Supreme Court's 1852 reversal of twenty-eight years of its own precedent; a Missouri court following its old cases would have freed Scott.
- **Reasoning (Taney, C.J.):** Taney implied a congressional power over acquired territory from the New States Clause anyway, so the statute fell on the due process limit, not on the Territories Clause.
- **The due process move:** a law taking property from a citizen who "came himself or brought his property into a particular Territory" "could hardly be dignified with the name of due process of law." The casebook asks, "Did you catch the sleight-of-hand?" Curtis's Cuba hypothetical refutes it: if Congress may free slaves brought from Cuba under the commerce power, "how can a similar regulation respecting a Territory violate the fifth amendment?"
- **Quote (Curtis, dissenting):** "Political reasons have not the requisite certainty to afford rules of juridical interpretation. They are different in different men. They are different in the same men at different times."

## Best counter-case

[[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]

- **Distinguishing line:** a political branch may refuse to treat a decision's reasoning as a rule for its own votes and legislation, but may not block execution of the judgment between the parties.
- **Lincoln's position:** he accepts the judgment against Scott and opposes the decision "as a political rule, which shall be binding on the voter to vote for nobody who thinks it wrong, which shall be binding on the members of Congress or the President to favor no measure that does not actually concur with the principles of that decision."
- **The fact Lincoln presses:** nobody proposed to free Scott: "Who has, in spite of the decision, declared Dred Scott free, and resisted the authority of his master over him?" The program was "we shall do what we can to have it to over-rule this."
- **Argue for Lincoln:** judicial review does not entail judicial supremacy, since [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]'s logic only requires a court deciding a case to prefer the Constitution. His criteria for a settled decision are neutral, and he concedes a lot: a settled decision should control "the general policy of the country, subject to be disturbed only by amendments," and "[m]ore than this would be revolution."
- **Argue for Douglas:** the decision of "the highest judicial tribunal on earth" "becomes the law of the land, binding on you, on me, and on every other good citizen whether we like it or not." His best point is consistency: "I want to know whether he is not bound to a decision which is contrary to his opinions just as much as to one in accordance with his opinions."
- **Douglas's weak points:** he equates criticism with mob rule ("Will he appeal to a mob?") and assumes his conclusion in saying "a Senator has no right to interfere with the decision of judicial tribunals."
- **The fact that flips it:** move from opposing the rule to blocking the decree, and Lincoln's own principle condemns the actor. That is how a departmentalist distinguishes Governor Faubus in [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] without adopting Douglas's view.
- **Where the argument landed:** Congress banned territorial slavery in 1862, with Fessenden voting "on what I understand to be the law of right"; the Senate seated Revels in 1870; and the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments reversed both holdings formally. Douglas knew "but one mode of reversing judicial decisions," appeal; the other modes are elections, appointments, new cases and Article V. See [[Amendment process - Art. V]].

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Some part of [[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott]] is still good law."** The Thirteenth Amendment removed the property premise and the Citizenship Clause reversed the citizenship holding. The Citizenship Clause also inverts Taney's premise that a State cannot make a national citizen: national citizenship is now primary, and state citizenship follows residence.
- **"The Missouri Compromise holding was just dictum."** That answer has to be argued. Each part "purports to be part of the Opinion of the Court," and Taney's best reply is that the territorial ruling is an alternative jurisdictional ground. Curtis's answer is that nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" a judgment of dismissal. [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] also decided the merits before finding no jurisdiction; the difference is that Marbury's extra discussion invalidated no statute.
- **"Taney held that Congress has no power over the territories."** Taney confined the Territories Clause to 1787 land and then found the power anyway, implied from the New States Clause. The holding that voided the statute is the due process holding.
- **"'No rights which the white man was bound to respect' is Taney's aside."** The phrase is Taney's account of founding-era opinion, offered as proof of what "citizens" meant, so it is the premise of the citizenship holding. Curtis's evidence shows it was false as history.
- **"Originalism produced the decision."** Both opinions claim the 1787 understanding. Taney reasons from expected applications against general words he concedes "would seem to embrace the whole human family"; Curtis reasons from the legal meaning of "natural-born citizen" and from who actually voted in 1787, and wins on Taney's own concession. See [[Modes of constitutional argument]].
- **"Lincoln's departmentalism meant defying the judgment."** Lincoln accepted the decree as to Scott; whether the executive may disregard a judgment arises only later, in [[Ex parte Merryman (1861)|Ex parte Merryman]]. The companion error in a fact pattern is collapsing the two status questions: what the territory's law did to the person, and whether the forum State recognizes it.
