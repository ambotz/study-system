## Rule statement

The Constitution is supreme written law, ordained by "We the People," which binds every officer who exercises power and can be changed only through Article V. Six themes recur across the whole document and supply the premises for every structural argument in the course: a written constitution, republicanism and popular sovereignty, separation of powers, federalism, rights, and the Constitution's imperfect congruence with justice. Whether the document's age defeats its authority is the dead-hand problem, posed by Jefferson's "The earth belongs always to the living generation" and answered by Madison's warning that a government "so often revised" would be "too mutable to retain those prejudices in its favor which antiquity inspires." The operative position is that the Constitution binds; the contest is over which justification for that — acceptance, inheritance, or precommitment — is correct, because each carries a different theory of how to read the text.

## Elements

1. **A written constitution.** Article VI makes it "the supreme Law of the Land," which is the foundation of judicial review and of the principle that all government is constrained by law. St. George Tucker: "the constitution is not an 'ideal thing,' but a real existence: it can be produced in a visible form." The contrast with England is the whole point — there, written documents are "islands of text in a sea of unwritten tradition"; here, the text is "a great continent" with "scattered lakes and ponds and rivers of tradition."
2. **Republicanism and popular sovereignty.** "We the People" locates sovereignty in neither the States nor the national government but in the people, which is why ratification by conventions matters and why the voting amendments (XV, XVII, XIX, XXIII, XXIV, XXVI) are extensions of an original principle rather than departures from it. The principle was present but imperfect at the founding, tainted by the accommodation of slavery.
3. **Separation of powers.** The division is functional — legislative, executive, judicial — and not the English mixed regime of king, lords and commons; Montesquieu is the source. The Anti-Federalist objection was that checks and balances intermingle the powers Montesquieu said to keep apart, and Madison's answer in The Federalist Nos. 47 through 51 is institutional: different electorates, staggered terms, and a "will" of its own for each branch. See [[Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances]].
4. **Federalism.** Supremacy for valid national action, but limited, enumerated national powers. The case for the center is collective-action and free-rider problems, economies of scale, and protection of minorities against States; the case for the locality is varied preferences, competition, experimentation, and closer popular control. Federalism "tends to lead to some form of judicial review," because someone must umpire. See [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].
5. **Rights.** A few appear in the original text (Art. I, §§ 9 and 10) and most in the amendments; the Bill of Rights runs against the national government and the Reconstruction Amendments against the States, a "Second Founding." Those amendments also expanded national power, through the enforcement clauses of XIII–XV, the income tax (XVI) and direct election of senators (XVII).
6. **The imperfect congruence of the Constitution with justice.** The document "does not forbid all bad things or require all good ones," and it permitted slavery; nearly a third of the Convention's delegates left or refused to sign. Washington called it "the best that could be obtained at this time," with "a constitutional door . . . op[e]ned for amendment," and Spencer Roane warned that treating the Constitution as perfect makes it "a nose of wax."
7. **The dead-hand question**, which sits beneath every interpretive argument: why should a document ratified in 1789 and 1791 bind anyone now? Four answers, each with an interpretive consequence:
   - Jeffersonian: each generation starts anew, and "every constitution, . . . and every law, naturally expires at the end of 19 years." The consequence is present judgment.
   - Acceptance: every generation is free to reject the Constitution and ours, like each earlier one, has accepted it. The consequence is the meaning that was accepted.
   - Inheritance: if we use the institutions the Constitution creates, we take its limits with them — "the children who inherit a house must accept its mortgage and its covenants." The consequence is the meaning that was inherited.
   - Precommitment: individuals and nations bind their future selves, as shareholders bind later shareholders and contracts bind tomorrow; if we are a nation and not a collection of people at different moments, we can make commitments. The consequence is fidelity to the commitment as made.
8. **Carry the chosen answer into method.** Acceptance and inheritance point toward original meaning; the Jeffersonian answer points toward present judgment. That is the link to [[Modes of constitutional argument]], and it is what is actually being fought over when [[Gundy v. United States]] and the removal cases argue about founding-era evidence.

## Standard of review / burden

No standard of review exists here, because none of this is a test a court applies; these are the premises from which tests are built, and a module that recites them as doctrine has misread them. What fills the gap is a set of structural commitments the document enforces on itself. Article VI supplies the operative legal rule — federal law is supreme, state judges are bound "notwithstanding" contrary state law, and federal *and* state officers swear an oath "to support this Constitution" — and Article V supplies the only lawful route to change, deliberately hard, requiring proposal by two-thirds of both Houses or by a convention called by two-thirds of the States and ratification by three-fourths. The burden that matters is argumentative rather than evidentiary: the party invoking a theme must show that the text and structure carry it, since Federalist 39's own scorecard refuses a single characterization — "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both," federal in its foundation, partly federal and partly national in the sources of its powers, national in their operation, federal in their extent, and neither wholly one nor the other in the amendment process. Where a structural premise runs out, the question is not which branch reviews it but which branch decides, and the course's recurring answer is that the political branches decide first and a court reviews only where a case presents the question.

## Exceptions

- Article V's two entrenched provisos: equal suffrage in the Senate, which no State may lose without its consent, and the slave-trade clause before 1808. Popular sovereignty and the amendment route both stop at those.
- Tradition and practice fill the spaces the text leaves; the casebook's image of "lakes and ponds and rivers of tradition" concedes that the continent of text is not continuous, which is the opening for liquidation and historical-gloss arguments throughout the course.
- The founding's own legality problem: the Convention's fifth decision was ratification by nine States under Article VII, "contrary to the Articles' unanimity rule," so the Constitution's own adoption was either an unlawful amendment, a revolution, or States withdrawing from a treaty. A theory of constitutional authority that cannot accommodate that fact is incomplete.
- Unenumerated rights: the Ninth Amendment protects them and the Tenth confirms limited, enumerated federal power, so the written-constitution theme does not entail that everything protected is written.
- The sixth theme is itself an exception to the other five: the Constitution's congruence with justice is imperfect by design and by compromise, and the three-fifths and fugitive slave clauses are, in the casebook's phrase, "the great tragedy in the work of the framers."
- Not decided, and left as the reading's closing question: who should fix the imperfections, and how — the amending power, the political branches, or the courts.

## Leading case

[[Constitutional Themes]]

- Supplies the six recurring themes that frame the course, and pairs them with The Federalist No. 39's refusal to classify the government as either national or federal.
- The fact that drives it is the Convention's own mixed result: the Great Compromise, a separately chosen single executive, and the slavery clauses mean the design cannot be described by a single principle, so Madison scores it provision by provision.
- Theme 3 is the one that matters most for the rest of the course: separation of powers here is functional rather than the mixed regime, which is why the vesting clauses and not the composition of the branches carry the argument.
- Theme 4 states the umpire point — federalism "tends to lead to some form of judicial review" — which connects the structural material to [[Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause]].
- The sentence worth quoting is Tucker's: the constitution "is not an 'ideal thing,' but a real existence: it can be produced in a visible form."
- The sixth theme carries the reading's warning and its interpretive payoff: if the Constitution is assumed perfect, the interpreter will make it match whatever he thinks perfection is, and it becomes Roane's "nose of wax."
- Read with [[Before the Constitution]] for why these were live choices: the Articles gave Congress no tax power and no commerce power, required nine of thirteen States for significant measures and unanimity to amend, and left Marshall's "corps of ambassadors" who "could, by their own power, execute nothing."

## Best counter-case

[[The Dead-Hand Problem]]

- Jefferson to Madison, September 6, 1789, cuts against the whole premise that the document binds: "The earth belongs always to the living generation," no generation may bind the next, and "every constitution, . . . and every law, naturally expires at the end of 19 years."
- The distinguishing move is Madison's reply of February 4, 1790, which answers on consequences rather than on authority: frequent revision would bring "pernicious factions," and a government "so often revised" would be "too mutable to retain those prejudices in its favor which antiquity inspires."
- Arguing for Jefferson: the case for starting again grows as the founding recedes; the ratifying generation is dead; Article V's difficulty makes the consent of the present generation formal rather than real; and the sixth theme concedes that what binds us is a compromised document.
- Arguing against Jefferson: the acceptance answer says each generation has been free to reject the Constitution and has not; the inheritance answer says anyone using the institutions it creates takes its limits with them; the precommitment answer says a nation that persists through time can bind its future self, as contracts and corporate charters do.
- The exam payoff is the link to method, not the philosophy. Whichever answer is given fixes what kind of argument counts: acceptance and inheritance point to the meaning accepted or inherited, the Jeffersonian answer to present judgment.
- Article V is the Constitution's own answer to the problem, which is why the amendment material belongs next to it rather than in a separate corner of the course; see [[Amendment process - Art. V]].
- Use this reading to show both halves of what Baude tests: the doctrine takes the Constitution's authority as given, and the dead-hand section is the place where the course asks whether that assumption is earned.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Treating the Constitution as congruent with justice and then reading rights into it to close the gap. The casebook's sixth theme says the opposite, and Roane's "nose of wax" names the failure mode exactly.
- Quoting The Federalist No. 39 for the proposition that the government is national. Madison's scorecard is deliberately split — "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both" — and each of its five categories gets a different answer.
- Describing separation of powers as a mixed regime of king, lords and commons. The American division is functional, and John Adams's preference for an elected mixed regime was the road not taken.
- Saying that popular sovereignty means the States or the national government is sovereign. "We the People" means neither is; that premise is what [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] uses against Maryland and what the secession readings fight over.
- Reading Art. I, § 1 and Art. II, § 1 as parallel. Article I vests "All legislative Powers **herein granted**"; Article II vests "The executive Power" with no such limiting phrase, and the whole vesting-clause debate turns on that difference.
- Assuming the original Constitution had no rights provisions because the Bill of Rights came later. Article I, §§ 9 and 10 carry several, including the Suspension, Bill of Attainder and Ex Post Facto Clauses.
- Using the four dead-hand answers interchangeably. They are not variations on one thought: acceptance rests on present consent, inheritance on the use of the institutions, precommitment on national identity over time, and each points toward a different interpretive method.
- Forgetting that the founding itself broke the rules it replaced. Article VII's nine-State ratification violated the Articles' unanimity requirement, which is why the legality of the founding is a question the casebook presses rather than an oddity to pass over.
