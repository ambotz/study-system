## Rule statement

The Constitution is supreme written law made by the people, it binds every officer who exercises power, and it can be changed only through Article V. Article VI makes it "the supreme Law of the Land," and the Preamble grounds it in "We the People." Six themes recur and supply the premises for structural argument: a written constitution, popular sovereignty, separation of powers, federalism, rights, and the Constitution's imperfect congruence with justice. Beneath them sits the dead-hand problem: Jefferson's "The earth belongs always to the living generation" against Madison's warning that a government "so often revised" would be "too mutable." The contest is over why the document binds (acceptance, inheritance, or precommitment, against the Jeffersonian denial), because each answer implies a different way to read it.

## Elements

1. **Is the argument grounded in the written text?**
   - Article VI makes the text "supreme Law," which is the foundation of judicial review and of the principle that all government is constrained by law ([[Constitutional Themes]]).
   - St. George Tucker's contrast: elsewhere, "[w]hatever the government or any branch of it had once done, it was inferred they had a right to do again"; here, "the constitution is not an 'ideal thing,' but a real existence: it can be produced in a visible form."
   - In England, written documents are "islands of text in a sea of unwritten tradition"; here, the text is "a great continent" with "scattered lakes and ponds and rivers of tradition" ([[Before the Constitution]]).
   - What falls outside the text, and how far practice may fill it, routes to [[Modes of constitutional argument]].
2. **Does it respect popular sovereignty?**
   - "We the People" makes neither the states nor the national government sovereign; the people are.
   - The people act through conventions and representative institutions, which is why ratification by conventions matters.
   - The voting amendments (XV, XVII, XIX, XXIII, XXIV, XXVI and XXVII) extend an original principle rather than depart from it.
   - The principle was present but imperfect at the founding, tainted by the accommodation of slavery.
3. **Does it respect the functional separation of powers?**
   - The division is by function (legislative, executive, judicial), not the English mixed regime of king, lords and commons; Montesquieu is the source.
   - The Anti-Federalists objected that checks and balances intermingle the powers Montesquieu said to keep apart; John Adams preferred an elected mixed regime.
   - Madison's answer in The Federalist Nos. 47–51 is institutional: different electorates, staggered terms, a "will" of its own for each branch, and vital powers such as war-making divided between branches.
   - See [[Separation of powers - vesting clauses and checks and balances]].
4. **Does it respect federalism?**
   - Valid national action is supreme, but national powers are limited and enumerated.
   - For the center: collective-action and free-rider problems (the navy example), economies of scale, and protection of minorities against states.
   - For the locality: varied preferences, competition, experimentation, and closer popular control.
   - Written federalism "tends to lead to some form of judicial review," because someone must umpire; see [[Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause]] and [[Nature of the Union - compact theory, nationalism and secession]].
5. **Which rights provision, and against which government?**
   - A few rights sit in the original text (Art. I, §§ 9 and 10); most are in the amendments.
   - The Bill of Rights runs against the national government; the Reconstruction Amendments run against the states, a "Second Founding."
   - The amendments also expanded national power: the enforcement clauses of XIII–XV, the income tax (XVI), and direct election of senators (XVII).
6. **Does the argument assume the Constitution is just?**
   - The document "does not forbid all bad things" or require all good ones, and it permitted slavery; the three-fifths and fugitive slave clauses were "the great tragedy in the work of the framers."
   - Almost a third of the Convention's delegates left or refused to sign. Washington called it "the best that could be obtained at this time," with "a constitutional door . . . op[e]ned for amendment."
   - Spencer Roane warned that treating the Constitution as perfect makes it "a nose of wax," and pointed to "the ample provisions it contains for its amendment" as proof its framers thought it imperfect.
7. **Why does a document ratified in 1789 and 1791 bind now?** Four answers ([[The Dead-Hand Problem]]):
   - Jeffersonian: each generation starts anew, and "every constitution, . . . and every law, naturally expires at the end of 19 years." The case for starting again grows as the founding recedes.
   - Acceptance: each generation is free to reject the Constitution, and ours, like every earlier one, has accepted it.
   - Inheritance: using the Constitution's institutions means taking its limits; "the children who inherit a house must accept its mortgage and its covenants."
   - Precommitment: individuals and nations bind their future selves, as shareholders bind later shareholders and contracts bind tomorrow; if we are a nation and not a collection of people at different moments, we can make commitments.
8. **Carry the chosen answer into method.**
   - The casebook lists the four answers without mapping them to methods.
   - A workable mapping: inheritance points to the meaning inherited, precommitment to the commitment as made, and the Jeffersonian answer to present judgment.
   - Acceptance is ambiguous, since what our generation accepted may be the original meaning or the Constitution as currently understood.
   - This is the link to [[Modes of constitutional argument]], and it is what [[Gundy v. United States (2019)|Gundy]] and the removal cases are really fighting about when they argue over founding-era evidence.

## Standard of review / burden

- **Not a test:** No court applies these themes as a standard; they are the premises from which tests are built.
- **What fills the gap (supremacy):** Article VI supplies the operative rule. Federal law is supreme, state judges are bound "notwithstanding" contrary state law, and federal *and* state officers swear an oath "to support this Constitution" ([[A Map of the Constitution]]).
- **What fills the gap (change):** Article V is the only lawful route to constitutional change, and it is deliberately hard: proposal by two-thirds of both houses or by a convention called on two-thirds of the states, then ratification by three-fourths. See [[Amendment process - Art. V]].
- **Burden:** A party invoking a theme must show that the text and structure carry it. The Federalist No. 39 refuses a single label: the Constitution is "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both."
- **Federalist 39's scorecard:** federal in its foundation; partly federal and partly national in the sources of its powers; national in their operation; federal in their extent; neither wholly one nor the other in the amendment process.

## Exceptions

- **Article V's entrenched provisos.** Equal suffrage in the Senate cannot be taken from a state without its consent, and the slave-trade clause was protected until 1808. The amendment route, and with it popular sovereignty, stops at those limits.
- **Tradition in the gaps.** The "lakes and ponds and rivers of tradition" concede that the continent of text is not continuous. That concession is the opening for liquidation and historical-gloss arguments throughout the course.
- **The founding's own legality problem.** The Convention's fifth decision was ratification by nine states under Article VII, contrary to the Articles' unanimity rule. The adoption was an unlawful amendment, a revolution, or states withdrawing from a treaty, and a theory of constitutional authority must account for that ([[Before the Constitution]]).
- **Unenumerated rights.** The Ninth Amendment protects them, and the Tenth confirms limited, enumerated federal power, so the written-constitution theme does not entail that everything protected is written.
- **Imperfection as a limit on the other five themes.** The sixth theme concedes that the Constitution's congruence with justice is imperfect by design and by compromise.
- **Not decided:** who should fix the imperfections, and how: judges, elected officials, or the people through Article V.

## Leading case

[[Constitutional Themes]]

- **Holding:** Six themes recur throughout the Constitution and frame the course; they follow The Federalist No. 39's refusal to classify the government as either national or federal.
- **Driving fact:** The Convention's mixed result (the Great Compromise, a separately chosen single executive, and the slavery clauses) means the design cannot be described by a single principle, so Madison scores it provision by provision.
- **Separation of powers:** The division is functional rather than a mixed regime, which is why the vesting clauses, not the social composition of the branches, carry the argument in later classes.
- **Federalism:** Written federalism "tends to lead to some form of judicial review," which connects the structural material to [[Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause]].
- **Quote:** Tucker: the constitution "is not an 'ideal thing,' but a real existence: it can be produced in a visible form."
- **Imperfection:** If the Constitution is assumed perfect, the interpreter will make it match whatever he thinks perfection is, and it becomes Roane's "nose of wax."
- **Background:** Read with [[Before the Constitution]]. The Articles gave Congress no tax power and no commerce power, required nine of thirteen states for significant measures and unanimity to amend, and left Marshall's "corps of ambassadors" who "could, by their own power, execute nothing."

## Best counter-case

[[The Dead-Hand Problem]]

- **Distinguishing line:** An argument that assumes the Constitution binds and asks what it means runs on the six themes; an argument that asks why a 1789 text binds at all must pick a dead-hand answer, and that answer decides which method of interpretation counts.
- **Jefferson to Madison, September 6, 1789:** "The earth belongs always to the living generation"; no generation may bind the next, and "every constitution, . . . and every law, naturally expires at the end of 19 years."
- **Madison's reply, February 4, 1790:** Madison answers on consequences rather than authority. Frequent revision would bring "pernicious factions," and a government "so often revised" would be "too mutable to retain those prejudices in its favor which antiquity inspires."
- **Argue for Jefferson:** The ratifying generation is dead; Article V's difficulty makes the present generation's consent formal rather than real; and the sixth theme concedes that what binds us is a compromised document.
- **Argue against Jefferson:** Each generation has been free to reject the Constitution and has not; anyone using its institutions takes its limits with them; and a nation that persists through time can bind its future self, as contracts and corporate charters do.
- **Link to Article V:** Article V is the Constitution's own answer to the dead hand, which is why the amendment material belongs beside this reading; see [[Amendment process - Art. V]].
- **Both halves of the exam:** The doctrine takes the Constitution's authority as given; the dead-hand question asks whether that assumption is earned.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"The Constitution is just, so read rights into it to close any gap."** The sixth theme says the opposite, and Roane's "nose of wax" names the failure mode.
- **"Federalist 39 shows the government is national."** Madison's scorecard is deliberately split, "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both," and each of its five categories gets a different answer.
- **"Popular sovereignty means the states (or the nation) are sovereign."** "We the People" means neither is. That premise is what [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] uses against Maryland and what the secession readings fight over.
- **"Article I, § 1 and Article II, § 1 are parallel."** Article I vests "All legislative Powers **herein granted**"; Article II vests "The executive Power" with no limiting phrase, and the vesting-clause debate turns on that difference ([[The Constitution of the United States]]).
- **"The original Constitution had no rights provisions."** Article I, §§ 9 and 10 carry several, including the Suspension, Bill of Attainder and Ex Post Facto Clauses.
- **"The four dead-hand answers are interchangeable."** Acceptance rests on present consent, inheritance on the use of the institutions, and precommitment on national identity over time; each points toward a different method, and the Jeffersonian answer rejects the premise that the document binds.
