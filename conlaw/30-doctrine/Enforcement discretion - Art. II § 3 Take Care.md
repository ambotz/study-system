## Rule statement

Article II, § 3 directs that the President "shall take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed," and the Convention "unanimously rejected a proposal to give the president a suspending power," so the executive may neither set a statute aside wholesale nor dispense individuals from it in advance. Within that duty the executive holds broad discretion over whom to prosecute and how to spend finite enforcement resources, and a court may neither coerce nor review a particular charging decision, because "the affixing or withholding of the signature is a matter of executive discretion which cannot be coerced or reviewed by the courts" ([[United States v. Cox]]). The discretion runs out where Congress has directed enforcement and prescribed its means and the executive adopts "a general policy which is in effect an abdication of its statutory duty" ([[Adams v. Richardson]]). The same clause also operates as a grant: in [[In re Neagle]] the duty reaches "the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself," supplying authority to act where Congress has enacted nothing. The contest is whether "take Care" is a power or a duty — doctrine treats it as the source of prosecutorial discretion, while the original-meaning objection is that "faithfully" imports a fiduciary obligation and that category-wide forbearance is the seventeenth-century suspending power under a new name.

## Elements

1. **Identify the law the executive is charged with executing.** A statute is the ordinary case; under [[In re Neagle]] "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument" also counts, which makes the executive's own constitutional reading part of the content of the duty.
2. **Characterize the congressional instruction, because it fixes the Youngstown category.**
   - Silence, or a bare authorization to act: category two, the posture of [[United States v. Cox]].
   - An express command that the agency "is authorized and directed to effectuate" the statute, with the enforcement means specified: category three, the posture of [[Adams v. Richardson]].
   - An affirmative funding restriction disobeyed by the executive: category three, the posture of [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] on § 2262.
3. **Characterize the form of the non-enforcement.**
   - A single decision not to charge a particular person for past conduct — the paradigm of lawful discretion.
   - A published framework that sets priorities but "provide[s] a general framework for exercising enforcement discretion in individual cases" — lawful under [[The Thompson Memo]]'s fourth principle only if the case-by-case discretion to deny is real.
   - A categorical policy that "forecloses the exercise of case-by-case discretion," which "poses 'special risks.'"
4. **Ask whether the forbearance is retrospective or prospective.** Declining to prosecute past conduct excuses no one in advance; "a decision to openly tolerate an undocumented alien's continued presence in the United States for a fixed period" is an assurance about future conduct and begins to resemble a dispensation.
5. **Ask whether benefits travel with the forbearance.** Work authorization and tolling of unlawful presence under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(9) must rest on "independent and more specific statutory authority rooted in the text of the INA," not on the Take Care Clause.
6. **Test the stated justification.**
   - Resources and priorities — "factors which are peculiarly within [the enforcing agency's] expertise" — is the strongest ground, and the whole Thompson framework leans on the gap between 11.3 million undocumented aliens and capacity to remove "fewer than 400,000 such aliens each year."
   - Fit with congressional policy: the executive may not "under the guise of exercising enforcement discretion, attempt to effectively rewrite the laws to match its policy preferences," and decisions must be "consonant with, rather than contrary to, the congressional policy."
   - A constitutional objection to the statute is a distinct and higher-order ground, because the President "is required to act in accordance with the laws—including the Constitution, which takes precedence over other forms of law."
7. **Run the abdication check.** A policy "so extreme as to amount to an abdication of its statutory responsibilities" is "ordinarily incompatible with the constitutional obligation to faithfully execute the laws," and is the one ground on which a court will order the executive to enforce.
8. **Ask whether the executive is simultaneously spending against Congress's instruction.** [[Adams v. Richardson]] treated HEW's continued payments to segregated schools as independently fatal: the agency was "actively supplying segregated institutions with federal funds, contrary to the expressed purposes of Congress," which converts the problem into one under [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].

## Standard of review / burden

- There is no standard of review for the paradigm case, because individual charging decisions are not reviewable at all: "the courts are not to interfere with the free exercise of the discretionary powers of the attorneys of the United States in their control over criminal prosecutions" ([[United States v. Cox]]), and the discretion "may well depend upon matters of policy wholly apart from any question of probable cause."
- Where the claim proceeds under the Administrative Procedure Act, the committed-to-agency-discretion exception is "a narrow one," applicable only where there is "no law to apply" (Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe, as applied in [[Adams v. Richardson]]); a statute that both directs enforcement and prescribes its means supplies the law to apply and defeats the exception. Heckler v. Chaney then flips the default for civil enforcement — an agency's refusal to act is "generally committed to an agency's absolute discretion" — but reserves the abdication case in its footnote 4.
- The challenger's burden is to show a *general policy* of non-enforcement rather than a series of individual judgments; a functioning program with occasional passes stays on the Cox side of the line.
- Article III supplies a second route to non-review. In United States v. Texas (2023) the Court held that States lacked standing to challenge DHS's enforcement priorities, because "the Executive Branch possesses authority to decide how to prioritize and how aggressively to pursue legal actions" and such decisions are "ordinarily unsuitable for judicial review"; the abdication argument was again reserved, not rejected. Cross-reference [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- The political-question route runs through [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]], which argues that "the whole subject-matter is political and not judicial" and that "no court or judge can take cognizance of the political acts of the President," relying on Luther v. Borden for judicial deference to the executive's determination of insurrection.
- Criminal liability is now a third gap. [[Trump v. United States]] holds that the Executive has "exclusive authority and absolute discretion" over which crimes to investigate and prosecute, placing that conduct in the "conclusive and preclusive" tier where immunity is absolute; for other official acts "[i]t is ultimately the Government's burden to rebut the presumption of immunity."
- What fills the gap where review is absent: the electorate, once the disagreement is disclosed in open court (Brown's concurrence in Cox); Congress, by legislating against the practice, which [[The Thompson Memo]] says is how the line has actually been policed; the appropriations power, since Lincoln performed the Take Care duty only "unless my rightful masters, the American people, shall withhold the requisite means"; and impeachment, which [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] names as the sole remedy because the President is "responsible before the high court of impeachment, and before no other human tribunal."

## Exceptions

- **The abdication exception.** A consciously and expressly adopted general policy of non-enforcement of a mandatory statute is reviewable and enjoinable; "[a] consistent failure to do so is a dereliction of duty reviewable in the courts" ([[Adams v. Richardson]]).
- **Mandatory statutes that also prescribe the means.** Where Congress says the agency "is authorized and directed to effectuate" the prohibition and lists the enforcement tools, voluntary compliance is "a preliminary step" and not a substitute; the discretion is over sequencing, not over whether to act.
- **Affirmative payment to the violators.** Even accepting that resources make universal enforcement impossible, "it is quite another to say HEW may affirmatively continue to channel federal funds to defaulting schools."
- **Constitutional objections to the statute.** [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] announced that "the executive branch will construe section 2262 not to abrogate these Presidential prerogatives," a refusal to be bound grounded in the Vesting, Opinions and Take Care Clauses; [[The Thompson Memo]] sets this ground apart from policy non-enforcement in its "But see" to the 1994 OLC opinion.
- **Impossibility of execution.** [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]] argued the duty lapses where the machinery is gone: "We no longer have a district judge, a district attorney, or a marshal in South Carolina." [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]] accepted the practical concession — "there will be no attempt to force obnoxious strangers among the people" — while still holding federal property and collecting duties.
- **Take Care as a grant rather than a limit.** [[In re Neagle]] permits protective executive action with no statute behind it, because otherwise the government would be "without the means of self-protection"; the mails hypothetical shows the power is not confined to judges.
- **Not decided: whether the abdication rule reaches criminal non-enforcement.** [[Adams v. Richardson]] is a funding case, and if its reasoning is limited to funding it says nothing about the Cox line.
- **Not decided: whether the Supreme Court accepts the abdication exception at all.** It has twice reserved it and never applied it.
- **Not decided: prospective class-wide deferred action.** The Fifth Circuit's injunction against DAPA was affirmed by an equally divided Court, without opinion or precedential effect.
- **Congress may not close the gap by conscripting state officers.** [[Printz v. United States]] holds that transferring execution to "thousands of CLEOs in the 50 States," who act "without meaningful Presidential control," shatters the unity of execution the Take Care Clause presupposes; see [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].

## Leading case

[[United States v. Cox]]

- Holds that a federal indictment is invalid without the government attorney's signature and that signing or withholding it is unreviewable, "as an incident of the constitutional separation of powers," with the Attorney General acting as "the hand of the President in taking care that the laws of the United States in legal proceedings and in the prosecution of offenses, be faithfully executed."
- The driving fact is that the grand jury voted to indict and the U.S. Attorney refused on the express instruction of Acting Attorney General Katzenbach: the conflict is between a constitutional body that wants a prosecution and the executive branch that will not bring one.
- The second driving fact is the identity of the targets — two Black witnesses for the United States in a voting-rights suit — which supplies Wisdom's federalism rationale that discretion must lie with an official "insulated from local prejudices and parochial pressures" rather than with "a majority of twenty-three members of a jury chosen from the Southern District of Mississippi."
- The judgment reversed only because Judge Cox's order was conjunctive (draft *and* sign); a different 4–3 majority held the U.S. Attorney could be compelled to draft, with Brown the swing vote on both questions.
- Jones supplies the formalist argument (prosecution is an executive function, so the judiciary may not exercise it); Wisdom supplies the functionalist and historical one, conceding that the discretion "would have evolved without the doctrine" of separation of powers.
- The dissenters read the same clause the other way: "one of the most fundamental and important of the laws so to be faithfully executed" is the Fifth Amendment's grand-jury requirement, so the Take Care duty cuts *for* the grand jury — and they reserve judicial power to require "a showing of good faith, and a statement of some rational basis for dismissal."
- The sentence worth quoting is Brown's: "The power not to initiate is indeed awesome. But it has to reside somewhere." Wisdom's is the answer to the corruption objection: "I am not aware that we have had more lawless Attorneys General than lawless juries."

## Best counter-case

[[Adams v. Richardson]]

- Holds that non-enforcement is reviewable where the agency has "consciously and expressly adopted a general policy which is in effect an abdication of its statutory duty," and rejects the claim that the prosecutorial-discretion cases confer "absolute discretion."
- The distinguishing facts are three, and the court ranks them: Title VI "authorized and directed" enforcement and specified the means; the challenge was to a general policy rather than to particular decisions (introduced with "More significantly"); and HEW was "actively supplying segregated institutions with federal funds, contrary to the expressed purposes of Congress."
- The concession that makes the case dangerous for the executive is narrow and deliberate: the court accepts the resource premise for prosecutors — "It is one thing to say the Justice Department lacks the resources necessary to locate and prosecute every civil rights violator" — and refuses to extend it to a funding program.
- Argue the executive side by stripping the grounds one at a time: make the statute permissive and ground one disappears; make the policy a working program with individual passes and ground two disappears, moving the case back toward [[United States v. Cox]]; remove the payments and ground three disappears and the case says little beyond the purse.
- Argue the congressional side by denying that the three grounds are severable: a mandatory command plus a category-wide refusal is a suspension in substance, which is the one thing the Take Care Clause was written to deny, and a resource excuse is available for every statute ever enacted.
- Note the posture asymmetry for the exam: Adams was litigated under the APA, not the Constitution, and the constitutional implication — that Article II does not let the executive ignore a specific congressional enforcement mandate — is drawn "at least implicitly."
- [[The Thompson Memo]] is the executive's own attempt to live with both cases: principles one and four are Cox, principle three is Adams, and the memo's bet is that a class-wide program with genuine case-by-case denial stays on Cox's side. Its own three concessions about deferred action — toleration rather than declination, attached benefits, and invitation to apply — are the best brief against it.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Citing [[United States v. Cox]] for a single holding. There were two different 4–3 majorities: signing cannot be compelled, drafting can, and the order was reversed only because it required both. Getting the split wrong is the first cold-call trap.
- Treating Heckler v. Chaney as constitutional law. It is an APA presumption about reviewability, not a holding that Article II confers absolute non-enforcement power, and its footnote 4 preserves the Adams exception rather than burying it.
- Overstating [[Adams v. Richardson]]. It is a court of appeals decision on a funding program; the Supreme Court has repeatedly declined either to adopt or to reject the abdication exception, and a student who says "the Court has held that wholesale non-enforcement is unconstitutional" has invented a holding.
- Calling ordinary prosecutorial discretion a dispensing power. A dispensation excuses an individual prospectively from obeying the law; declining to prosecute past conduct excuses no one in advance. The dispensation objection bites against prospective, class-wide assurances of forbearance, not against Cox.
- Reading [[The Thompson Memo]]'s four principles as limits without testing the resource premise. The framework constrains the executive only as far as the resource gap is real; an agency declining to collect a tax it could easily collect fails principle one, and a category-wide exemption fails principle four.
- Reading [[Trump v. United States]] as blessing non-enforcement. It holds that core prosecution and removal decisions cannot be *criminally punished*, not that any particular non-enforcement policy is lawful; the remaining check it contemplates is impeachment.
- Forgetting that [[In re Neagle]] runs the clause the other way. Cox and Adams read "take Care" as describing a duty owed with respect to statutes Congress passed; Neagle reads it as a source of authority where Congress passed nothing, which makes the executive's own view of its constitutional obligations part of the discretion.
- Treating a signing statement as a line-item veto. A signing statement changes nothing in the law and a court remains free to disagree, unlike the cancellation in [[Clinton v. City of New York]]; the objection to [[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]] is the suspending-power objection, not [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
- Assuming the Take Care duty is self-funding. [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]] qualifies it twice, including by Congress's control of the means, and an unconstitutional funding restriction is not itself an appropriation.
