## Rule statement

The decision to take the nation into war belongs to Congress under the Declare War Clause; the President as Commander in Chief conducts a war once begun and may use force without prior authorization to repel attacks on the United States, its territories or its armed forces. [[The Prize Cases]] state both halves: the President "has no power to initiate or declare a war," but when war is made upon the nation by invasion or organized rebellion he is "bound to accept the challenge without waiting for any special legislative authority," and his determination that a state of war exists binds the courts. The War Powers Resolution codifies that division in § 2(c) and adds a 60-day clock in § 5(b), but every President since the veto in [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]] has denied that a statute can contract a constitutional power. The live contest is between the Convention's formulation in [[Madison's Notes on the War Power]] — Sherman's "[t]he executive shd be able to repel and not to commence war" — and the executive's claim in [[Modern Applications of the War Power]] that "limited hostilities" serving "important national interests" are not constitutional "war" at all.

## Elements

1. Characterize the act: initiating hostilities, conducting a war already begun, or terminating one. The allocation is different at each stage, and most exam errors come from applying the initiation rule to a conduct question.
2. For initiation, ask first whether Congress has decided. Congressional authority takes three forms, and the form matters less than the fact of a congressional decision.
   - A formal declaration, as in the Declaration of War Against Japan of December 8, 1941, which recognizes the war, authorizes "the entire naval and military forces," and pledges "all of the resources of the country" ([[Declarations of War]]).
   - A specific statutory authorization: the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, the 2001 AUMF, which declares itself "[c]onsistent with section 8(a)(1) of the War Powers Resolution." There is no constitutional difference from a declaration as to the power to initiate; the differences are scope, legal incidents, specificity and politics.
   - Nothing else. Appropriations do not authorize — WPR § 8(a)(1) forbids inferring authority from "any provision contained in an appropriation Act" — and U.N. Security Council approval is not a congressional decision, as the Korea episode shows.
3. If Congress has not decided, ask whether the use of force falls in the repel-attacks carve-out that the substitution of "declare" for "make" was meant to preserve.
   - An attack on U.S. territory already under way: squarely within it.
   - An attack on U.S. armed forces abroad: within its logic, and WPR § 2(c)(3) agrees.
   - An attack believed imminent: within it only if "repel" includes preempt, which the Convention debate does not settle.
   - A strike to punish or deter after the attack has ended: no longer repelling, and outside [[The Prize Cases]].
4. If neither authorization nor attack, the only remaining claim is Article II standing alone, tested by the OLC two-part framework: whether the President "could reasonably determine that the action serves important national interests," and whether "the 'anticipated nature, scope and duration'" of the operation would fall short of "war under the Constitution."
5. Run the facts through the Youngstown frame, which supplies the analytic grid for the whole field: express or implied authorization is category one, congressional silence or absence is category two, and a statutory prohibition — the 60-day clock after it runs, a funding cutoff — is category three, where the President must claim a conclusive and preclusive command power. See [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]].
6. For conduct of a war lawfully under way, the Commander in Chief directs operations and may take enemy property and persons as war measures, but Congress retains express textual powers over the same subject: "make Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water," "make Rules for the Government and Regulation of the land and naval Forces," and the purse. Where Congress has prescribed the method, the presidential order yields (Little v. Barreme; Brown v. United States).
7. Check the law-of-war limit on conduct. [[Lincoln, Order of Retaliation]] rests on "[t]he law of nations and the usages and customs of war as carried on by civilized powers," not on the Constitution, and the order is conditional and one-for-one because reprisal is a device to coerce compliance rather than to punish.
8. For termination, there is no Terminate War Clause; Ludecke v. Watkins names three modes — "treaty or legislation or Presidential proclamation" — and holds that "[w]ar does not cease with a cease-fire order." Repeal of an authorization alone did not end Vietnam; the funding cutoff did. See [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].

## Standard of review / burden

There is no ordinary standard of review, because the courts defer on the facts and often refuse the case. On whether hostilities amount to war, [[The Prize Cases]] hold that the question "is to be decided by him," the President, and that "this Court must be governed by the decisions and acts of the political department," with the blockade proclamation "official and conclusive evidence" of the state of war; Ludecke v. Watkins supplies the mirror image at the other end, that termination "is a political act." That deference runs to the factual predicate, not to the President's own view of the scope of his powers, and the two must be kept apart. The political-question route is contested rather than settled: Dellums v. Bush (1990) held a Gulf War challenge nonjusticiable only for ripeness, because the Constitution supplies a clear standard requiring congressional authorization, while Ange v. Bush (1990) held the same dispute a political question outright; Orlando v. Laird (2d Cir. 1971) found the Vietnam challenge justiciable and then upheld the war because "[t]he Congress and the Executive have taken mutual and joint action." Ripeness is the real barrier, since a court cannot decide that Congress has refused before Congress votes, and Congress's authorization of Desert Storm mooted the question. What fills the gap is interbranch practice rather than adjudication: the WPR's reporting requirement, the appropriations power, and the executive's own precedents — which is why the fight is over whether practice has "liquidate[d]" the meaning of the Clause in Madison's sense, a claim weakened by the fact that Presidents report "consistent with" rather than "pursuant to" the Resolution and Congress passed it over a veto.

## Exceptions

- Repelling sudden attacks: the carve-out the "declare" amendment was adopted to preserve, and the one uncontested unilateral power.
- Attack on the armed forces abroad, treated as sufficient by both [[The Prize Cases]] and WPR § 2(c)(3), even though the blockade case itself involved attack at home.
- Rescue and protection of citizens: the strongest of the unauthorized episodes in [[Declarations of War]] is the 1980 Iran raid, because the objective was rescue rather than defeat of an enemy.
- Insurrection and civil war: the Militia Acts of 1795 and 1807 authorize force against invasion and insurrection in advance, and Grier holds that Congress "cannot declare war against a State," so a declaration cannot be the test in a domestic rebellion.
- Ratification after the fact: Congress's 1861 acts approved Lincoln's measures "as if" taken under "previous express authority," which Grier uses as a fallback and Nelson refuses to apply retroactively to captures already made.
- Covert action under the Intelligence Authorization Act of 1991, permitted on a written presidential finding within 48 hours and reported to the intelligence committees, which supplies a statutory framework without resolving whether covert lethal force is war.
- The WPR's own disclaimers: § 8(c)(2) denies that the Resolution grants any authority, so it cannot be read as a licence for sixty days of war, and § 5(c)'s concurrent-resolution removal device is invalid after [[INS v. Chadha]], leaving severability open.
- Not decided: whether "repel" reaches anticipatory self-defense; whether the 2001 AUMF reaches groups with no connection to September 11; whether the 60-day clock binds a President who claims an independent Article II power; whether the Declare War Clause, unlike ordinary legislative power, requires Congress itself to name the enemy rather than delegating that choice by "he determines" (see [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]).

## Leading case

[[The Prize Cases]]

- Holds that a civil war "is never solemnly declared; it becomes such by its accidents," so the President could institute a blockade of rebel ports "which neutrals are bound to regard" before Congress acted.
- The driving fact is that the South attacked Fort Sumter: the war was "thrust" upon the government, and Grier's premise that the President "has no power to initiate or declare a war" would have cut the other way had Lincoln begun it.
- Congress was out of session and had neither authorized nor forbidden the blockade, which places the case in category two at worst and category one once the Acts of 1795 and 1807 and the 1861 ratification are counted; it is never category three.
- Enemy character at sea follows territory, not loyalty: liability to capture "does not in any manner depend on the personal allegiance of the owner," and residents of rebel territory "are none the less enemies because they are traitors."
- The sentence to quote: "The President was bound to meet it in the shape it presented itself, without waiting for Congress to baptize it with a name; and no name given to it by him or them could change the fact."
- Nelson's dissent, with Taney, Catron and Clifford, is the formalist pole: a state of war is a legal status, not a fact, and the Militia Acts could not confer "the power of declaring war against a State of this Union, or of deciding that war existed," a power that "cannot be delegated or surrendered to the Executive." Captures before the Act of July 13, 1861 are "illegal and void."

## Best counter-case

[[Modern Applications of the War Power]]

- OLC's position is that the President, "as Commander in Chief and Chief Executive, has the constitutional authority to deploy the military to protect American persons and interests without seeking prior authorization from Congress," conceding only that Congress decides "whether to commit the Nation to a sustained, full-scale conflict with another Nation."
- The distinguishing move is definitional rather than doctrinal: the Syria and Soleimani strikes are said not to be "war" because of their anticipated nature, scope and duration — no ground troops, a specific target, an operation of a few hours — so the Declare War Clause is never engaged.
- Arguing for the executive: two centuries of limited unilateral force, "quite literally running from the halls of Montezuma to the shores of Tripoli and beyond," with Congress funding the military and rarely objecting, is Frankfurter's historical gloss applied to war; Goldsmith's "That is our system: One person decides" describes the practice accurately, and Bates's premise in [[Bates, Opinion on the Suspension of Habeas Corpus]] that "the whole subject-matter is political and not judicial" keeps a court out of it.
- Arguing against: gloss requires a practice the other branch has never questioned, and Congress questioned this one in the War Powers Resolution, passed over a veto; liquidation settles ambiguous text, and OLC's own concession about sustained conflict shows the text is not ambiguous about who decides; many of OLC's precedents are OLC opinions, which are evidence of the executive's claims and not of congressional acquiescence.
- The pressure points on the test itself: nothing in the Convention's sense of war turns on the means, since a naval blockade was the belligerent act in [[The Prize Cases]]; and a test keyed to "anticipated" duration makes the constitutional line depend on the President's own prediction, when "[a] brief and tidy operation can grow into something big."
- The best synthesis to offer: practice has plausibly liquidated a narrow category of brief, limited strikes and rescues, and has not liquidated unilateral initiation of sustained hostilities; a months-long air campaign triggers OLC's own reservation for "prolonged and substantial engagements" and starts the 60-day clock.
- The statutory escape route is worth flagging on any modern hypothetical: the Soleimani strike was also defended under the 2002 Iraq AUMF, which "would sidestep the constitutional question" by moving the case to category one — and the NDAA for Fiscal Year 2026 repealed the 1991 and 2002 Iraq authorizations.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Reading [[The Prize Cases]] as authority for unilateral presidential war. Grier says the opposite in terms — "He has no power to initiate or declare a war" — and the holding is a power to respond to force already used against the nation.
- Treating a formal declaration as constitutionally required. The Convention placed the decision in Congress, not a form of words, and only five wars have been formally declared; a specific statutory authorization is Congress's decision and suffices for initiation.
- Treating continued appropriations as authorization. WPR § 8(a)(1) forbids the inference, the Kosovo supplemental is the standard trap fact, and the deeper point is that the Declare War Clause would do no work beyond the Appropriations Clause if funding counted.
- Writing that Congress can stop a war by concurrent resolution under WPR § 5(c). After [[INS v. Chadha]] a removal resolution must be presented to the President, which is why the 2020 Iran resolution was vetoed; Congress's real power is a two-thirds majority in each House or a funding cutoff, and repeal alone left Nixon fighting in Vietnam.
- Over-reading [[The Emancipation Proclamation]] as proof that the Commander in Chief may seize property generally. It was expressly "a fit and necessary war measure," it freed no one in the loyal border States or in areas already under Union control, and its territorial limits are what make the war-measure theory work; the 1862 statutes also put it nearer category one than its Commander in Chief recital suggests.
- Treating the Commander in Chief power over the conduct of war as exclusive. The Captures Clause and the Rules for the Government and Regulation of the Forces are express congressional powers over the same subject, which is why a statute banning the reprisal ordered in [[Lincoln, Order of Retaliation]] would be strong and the President's counterclaim weak.
- Saying that Lincoln defied a court order to release Merryman. In [[Ex parte Merryman]] Taney issued a writ and a contempt attachment, both resisted, and then ordered only that the record be filed and sent to the President; precision about what was adjudged is what the judgment-supremacy question turns on.
- Confusing the two no-coercion arguments in the secession readings. [[Buchanan, Address to Congress]] says no enumerated power authorizes war on a State; [[Jefferson Davis, Farewell Address to the Senate]] says "there are no laws of the United States to be executed within the limits of a seceded State." Davis's is parasitic on the validity of secession, and [[Lincoln, First Inaugural Address]] answers both by reframing coercion as execution of the laws against individuals.
