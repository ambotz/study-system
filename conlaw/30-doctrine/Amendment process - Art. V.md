## Rule statement

Article V supplies two routes to a proposal — two-thirds of both houses, or a convention called by Congress on the application of two-thirds of the state legislatures — and two routes to ratification, by three-fourths of the state legislatures or by conventions in three-fourths of the states, "whichever method Congress chooses in the particular case" ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]). An amendment so ratified is "valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of th[e] Constitution," and it is not presented to the President (Hollingsworth v. Virginia). The operative doctrine on timing is [[Dillon v. Gloss]]: Article V implies that ratification must come "within some reasonable time after the proposal," and Congress may fix a definite period "as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification." The contest is that this doctrine has almost no enforcer and a serious rival — *Coleman v. Miller* treated Dillon's contemporaneity reasoning as dictum and left promulgation to Congress, while the executive branch concluded in 1992 that Article V "contains no time limits for ratification" and certified the Twenty-seventh Amendment 203 years after its proposal ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]).

## Elements

1. **Proposal.** Two-thirds of both houses, or a convention called by Congress on the application of two-thirds of the states. The convention route has never been used; it exists "to circumvent Congress if its members were too resistant to constitutional change," and whether it can be limited to named topics, and who would enforce the limit, are open ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
2. **Designation of the mode of ratification.** Congress chooses legislatures or conventions. The original Constitution used conventions to "circumvent the state legislatures, which jealously guarded their own powers" ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
3. **Ratification by three-fourths of the states.** Count the denominator and the numerator: whether seceded states count is the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendment problem, and the Twenty-seventh crossed the line with Michigan on May 7, 1992 as the thirty-eighth of fifty.
4. **Timing.** Ask three separate questions. Is there any implicit limit at all? If Congress imposed one, was it in the amendment's text (the Eighteenth, Twentieth, Twenty-first and Twenty-second) or in a separate joint resolution (the ERA)? And may a deadline be imposed after proposal, extended, or the proposal recalled ([[The Riddles of Article V]], [[The Equal Rights Amendment]])?
5. **Rescission.** After the three-fourths threshold is reached, no: "the scale-tipping ratification locks the amendment in place," or a state could secede from part of the Constitution. Before the threshold, open — Idaho's ERA rescission was upheld in *Idaho v. Freeman* and then vacated as moot, and Ohio's and New Jersey's rescissions as to the Fourteenth Amendment were never resolved ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
6. **Certification.** The Archivist certifies under 1 U.S.C. § 106b. On the OLC's reading this is a duty, not a discretion, and it does not wait on Congress, because Article V lets Congress propose, designate the mode and call a convention and "[n]othing in Article V suggests that Congress has any further role" ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
7. **The two entrenched provisos.** No amendment before 1808 affecting the first and fourth clauses of Art. I, § 9, and no state deprived of equal suffrage in the Senate without its consent. Whether an amendment may entrench further, and whether the provisos themselves may be amended away, are unresolved ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
8. **No presentment.** An amendment does not go to the President ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]); note the awkwardness that the 1978 ERA extension resolution failed to get two-thirds and was signed by President Carter instead ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).

## Standard of review / burden

There is effectively no judicial standard, and that is the point of the class. [[Dillon v. Gloss]] is "[t]he only judicial authority in the chapter," and what it actually held is narrow: Congress may set a deadline, and seven years is reasonable. *Coleman v. Miller* then held that whether an amendment had "lost its vitality through lapse of time" was for Congress when the time came to promulgate, suggesting "either that Congress possesses this power or that its acceptance of a state ratification is a 'political question'," without a controlling majority ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]). The gap is filled by the political branches acting on their own constitutional judgment: Archivist Wilson had already decided to certify the Twenty-seventh Amendment, on OLC advice, before Congress voted to accept it, and the OLC read Coleman as resting on "a political question rationale" that "has no bearing on the actions of the Archivist, an officer of one of the political branches." The burden that matters in practice is therefore a practical one — whoever wants an amendment recognised must persuade the certifying officer, as the states suing the Archivist over the ERA are trying to do ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]). The OLC's methodological argument is the closest thing to a standard of review in the field: an amendment procedure "must provide a clear rule that is capable of mechanical application, without any need to inquire into the timeliness or substantive validity of the consensus achieved," so "any interpretation that would introduce confusion must be disfavored. . . . The alternative to procedural formalism is uncertainty and litigation." Claims that particular amendments are substantively invalid were "raised in, and rejected by, *Leser v. Garnett*," and a suit over the allegedly forgotten 1792 ratification of the House-size amendment was disposed of on standing and political-question grounds in *LaVergne v. Bryson* without reaching the merits ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).

## Exceptions

- **No presentment to the President.** Settled since Hollingsworth v. Virginia; this is the one Article V ambiguity with a clean judicial answer ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
- **Congress's power to fix a deadline.** [[Dillon v. Gloss]] allows it as "a matter of detail which Congress may determine as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification," which is a carve-out from the bare text rather than a reading of it.
- **The two entrenched provisos.** Article V's own exceptions, and the only express time rule the Framers wrote — which is the OLC's best textual argument that no general limit was intended: "[i]f the Framers had contemplated some terminus of the period for ratification of amendments generally, they would have so stated."
- **Rescission after the threshold.** Barred, on the shared assumption that the scale-tipping ratification locks the amendment in place ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- **Not decided: rescission before the threshold.** "Is ratification like a light-switch that a state can turn on and then off again?"
- **Not decided: whether Congress may reject a ratification that has crossed three-fourths.** Neither the executive nor Congress has recognised the ERA; the pending suit asks a court to force the Archivist's hand, and whether that is "something the courts may do" is open ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- **Not decided: whether a convention may be limited to named topics.** Charles Black read "a Convention for proposing Amendments" as one free to propose what it likes; Rappaport argues the other way; the casebook's harder question is "who would enforce it?" ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
- **Amendment outside Article V.** Ackerman's "constitutional moments" and Amar's national-referendum route are both claims that Article V is not exclusive; the casebook's rejoinder is "Why go to the bother of listing the prescribed methods of amendment if they are not the prescribed methods of amendment?"
- **Still-pending unratified proposals.** The 1789 apportionment amendment, the 1810 titles-of-nobility amendment, the 1861 Corwin Amendment and the 1920s Child Labor Amendment all remain outstanding on the no-deadline view; Corwin, "[h]ad it been ratified at the time," "would have become the Thirteenth Amendment."

## Leading case

[[Dillon v. Gloss]]

- Holds that "the fair inference or implication from Article V is that the ratification must be within some reasonable time after the proposal," and that seven years was reasonable.
- The fact that drove it: the proposing resolution for the Eighteenth Amendment declared the Amendment "inoperative unless ratified within seven years." Without that clause Dillon has no argument, and the Court has no occasion to ask where a time limit could come from.
- Concedes at the outset that Article V says nothing about time, "neither that it shall be unlimited nor that it shall be fixed by Congress," and reaches its rule by implication: "what is reasonably implied is as much a part of it as what is expressed."
- Three grounds, and you should be able to name all three: proposal and ratification are "not treated as unrelated acts but as succeeding steps in a single endeavor"; amendments are proposed only when "deemed to be a necessity," implying prompt disposition; and ratification "is but the expression of the approbation of the people" and so must be "sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of States to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- The consequence that made the contrary reading "quite untenable": four proposals from 1789, 1810 and 1861 were still pending in 1921, and their old ratifications could be completed "by representatives of generations now largely forgotten." One of those four became the Twenty-seventh Amendment seventy-one years later.
- The internal tension worth naming: the Court derives a timing requirement from structure and purpose while conceding the text is silent, then hands the content of that requirement to Congress, so the principle and the institution applying it come apart. *Coleman* widened the gap and the OLC drove through it.
- The sentence worth quoting is Jameson's, adopted by the Court: an amendment not ratified "early while that sentiment may fairly be supposed to exist" is "regarded as waived, and not again to be voted upon, unless a second time proposed by Congress."

## Best counter-case

[[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]

- The anchor is a document, not a case, and that is the assignment: an executive-branch opinion telling the Supreme Court it was wrong, acted on by the Archivist, and never tested in court.
- Its holding: "By a straightforward reading of Article V, the amendment is now 'valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of th[e] Constitution,'" and the Archivist was required to certify it under 1 U.S.C. § 106b without waiting for Congress.
- The distinguishing fact from [[Dillon v. Gloss]] is the one the OLC presses hardest: Dillon's issue "was Congress's authority to limit the period for ratification, not a State's authority to ratify a long-dormant proposed amendment," and Hughes himself called the contemporaneity discussion "merely a dictum" in *Coleman*.
- Argue the OLC side, point for point against Dillon's three grounds. "Single endeavor" "simply assumes its conclusion that the process is to be short rather than lengthy." Proposal-on-necessity confuses two things, because "necessity is not the same as emergency," and the states ratifying in the 1990s "evidently consider it to be just as necessary today." And "[c]onsensus does not demand contemporaneity," since lasting consensus "may just as well be served by a process that allows for extended deliberation in the various states."
- Argue the Dillon side: Article V states its numbers precisely and its drafters knew how to write a time rule when they wanted one, but a Constitution amended by ratifications two centuries apart is not ratified by any identifiable people; the objection is the one Note 2 puts plainly — "isn't Dillon right that there is something fishy about adding an amendment without a real consensus . . . ? But isn't the O.L.C. opinion right that Article V does not say that?"
- The second, structural half of the opinion is the departmentalism payoff: giving one branch "ultimate control over the Constitution's very content would be to repudiate the American approach in favor of a return to parliamentary supremacy," and if congressional promulgation were required "the executive branch would have illegally certified every amendment except the Fourteenth." Read it with [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- The asymmetry to have ready: the Twenty-seventh Amendment had no deadline and ripened after 203 years; the ERA had a deadline, missed it, and is conventionally dead — so if the OLC is right that Article V contains no time limit, "the only thing killing the ERA is a congressional resolution that Article V never authorized" ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- The closing line: "The Congressional Pay Amendment — rather, the Twenty-Seventh Amendment — although well aged, is not stale."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Stating Dillon's dictum as its holding.** [[Dillon v. Gloss]] held that Congress may set a deadline and that seven years is reasonable. It did not hold that any particular ratification came too late, and the contemporaneity language is reasoning. Getting this wrong makes the OLC's attack unintelligible.
- **Assuming the Twenty-seventh Amendment was judicially validated.** There has never been a judicial decision on it "because Congress has not yet attempted to violate it" ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]). The casebook twice calls it the "(alleged?)" ratification.
- **Assuming Congress must accept a ratification.** Congress did vote to accept the Twenty-seventh Amendment, but the Archivist had already certified, and the OLC's position is that Congress has no promulgation role at all. "What if Congress had voted not to accept it?" is the live question ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
- **Running the ERA arithmetic carelessly.** Thirty-five states ratified before the original deadline and five attempted to rescind, so the count is 30 or 35, "both short of the 38 states needed"; the three late ratifications (Nevada 2017, Illinois 2018, Virginia 2020) reach 38 only if the deadline is ignored and rescissions are disregarded ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- **Treating a presidential statement as a ratification.** Biden's January 2025 statement calling the ERA "the law of the land" changed nothing, because the Archivist did not certify or publish and the certified text did not change. The certifying officer's refusal, not the President's view, determines the published text.
- **Ignoring where the deadline was written.** The ERA's limit was in a separate joint resolution, not in the amendment's text, which is the only reason the extension argument gets off the ground; the Eighteenth, Twentieth, Twenty-first and Twenty-second put the limit in the text ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]], [[The Riddles of Article V]]).
- **Assuming a state that has rejected an amendment is finished with it.** Several Southern states ratified the Fourteenth Amendment after rejecting it, and the reverse question — rescission before the threshold — has never been resolved.
- **Forgetting how the Fourteenth Amendment was ratified.** Congress replaced Southern governments and conditioned readmission on ratification, which is "remarkably close" to the hypothetical the casebook poses as obviously improper; Amar's answer is that those governments were not "republican" under Art. IV, § 4, which moves the question to [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]] rather than to Article V.
- **Treating Article V as a sideshow.** Pozen and Schmidt's claim is that "the overwhelming majority of recognized amendments had significant arguable legal infirmities at the time of their adoption," and the final question of the chapter is whether a hard-to-amend Constitution justifies updating by interpretation or proves the opposite — which is the hinge between this module and [[Modes of constitutional argument]] and [[Constitutional themes and the dead hand]].
