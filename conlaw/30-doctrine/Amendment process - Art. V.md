## Rule statement

An amendment becomes part of the Constitution when Congress or a convention proposes it and three-fourths of the states ratify it by the mode Congress chooses; the President plays no part. Ratification runs through state legislatures or state conventions, "whichever method Congress chooses in the particular case" ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]). On timing, [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] holds that ratification must come "within some reasonable time after the proposal," and that Congress may fix a definite period. The Hughes opinion in [[Coleman v. Miller]] called Dillon's reasoning dictum and left lapse of time to Congress, without a controlling majority. The live contest is Dillon's implied-consensus reading against the executive branch's conclusion that Article V "contains no time limits for ratification," on which the Twenty-seventh Amendment was certified 203 years after proposal ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]).

## Elements

1. **Was the amendment validly proposed?**
   - Congressional route: two-thirds of both houses.
   - Convention route: Congress "shall call" a convention on the application of two-thirds of the state legislatures. The route has never been used; the framers meant it "to circumvent Congress if its members were too resistant to constitutional change" ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
   - Whether a convention can be limited to named topics, and who would enforce the limit, is open; see Exceptions.
2. **Did Congress designate the mode of ratification?**
   - Congress chooses state legislatures or state conventions.
   - The original Constitution used conventions to "circumvent the state legislatures, which jealously guarded their own powers" ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
3. **Did three-fourths of the states ratify?**
   - Count the numerator and the denominator separately.
   - Denominator: whether purportedly seceded states count is the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendment problem; on Lincoln's theory that no state could leave, they must be counted ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]], Note 6).
   - Numerator: the Twenty-seventh crossed the line when Michigan ratified on May 7, 1992, as the thirty-eighth of fifty states.
   - Whether coerced ratifications count (the Southern states on the Fourteenth) routes partly to [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]]; see Common trap.
4. **Was ratification timely?** Ask three separate questions.
   - Is there any implied time limit at all? [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] says a "reasonable time"; the [[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment|OLC]] says none.
   - If Congress imposed a deadline, where did it put it? In the amendment's text (the Eighteenth, Twentieth, Twenty-first and Twenty-second) or in a separate joint resolution ([[The Equal Rights Amendment|the ERA]])?
   - May Congress impose a deadline after proposal, extend one, or recall a proposal outright ([[The Riddles of Article V]])?
5. **Did any ratifying state validly rescind?**
   - After the three-fourths threshold: no. "[T]he scale-tipping ratification locks the amendment in place," or a state could "secede" from part of the Constitution.
   - Before the threshold: open. Idaho's ERA rescission was upheld in *Idaho v. Freeman* and then vacated as moot; Ohio's and New Jersey's attempts on the Fourteenth Amendment were never resolved ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
   - The mirror case is settled in practice: several Southern states ratified the Fourteenth Amendment after first rejecting it.
6. **Who certifies the result?**
   - The Archivist certifies under 1 U.S.C. § 106b.
   - On the OLC's reading, certification is a duty, not a discretion, and does not wait for Congress: Article V lets Congress propose, designate the mode and call a convention, and "[n]othing in Article V suggests that Congress has any further role" ([[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
7. **Does the amendment touch an entrenched proviso?**
   - No amendment before 1808 could affect the first and fourth clauses of Art. I, § 9 (the slave-trade and capitation clauses).
   - No state may be deprived of equal suffrage in the Senate without its consent.
   - Whether an amendment may entrench itself further, as the Corwin Amendment purported to, and whether the Senate proviso can be amended away first, are unresolved ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
8. **Presentment is not an element.**
   - An amendment is not presented to the President (*Hollingsworth v. Virginia*, 1798).
   - The awkward exception in practice: the 1978 ERA extension resolution did not get two-thirds and was signed by President Carter instead ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).

## Standard of review / burden

- **Standard:** [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] is the only judicial authority in the chapter, and what it held is narrow: Congress may set a ratification deadline, and seven years is reasonable. The contemporaneity requirement is reasoning, not a holding that any ratification came too late.
- **Not reviewed / political question:** In [[Coleman v. Miller]], Chief Justice Hughes (joined by Stone and Reed) refused to decide whether the Child Labor Amendment had "lost its vitality through lapse of time," leaving the question to Congress "when . . . the time arrives for the promulgation of the adoption of the amendment"; Black and three others concurred separately. The casebook reads Coleman as "suggesting, without a controlling majority opinion, either that Congress possesses this power or that its acceptance of a state ratification is a 'political question'" ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
- **Not reviewed / lower courts:** Suits over the allegedly forgotten 1792 Connecticut ratification of the House-size amendment failed on standing and political-question grounds without reaching the merits (*LaVergne v. Bryson*, 3d Cir. 2012).
- **Substantive-validity claims:** Claims that an amendment is out of keeping with the Constitution's "spirit" and so cannot be adopted were "raised in, and rejected by, *Leser v. Garnett*" (1922) ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
- **What fills the gap:** The political branches act on their own constitutional judgment. Archivist Wilson decided to certify the Twenty-seventh Amendment on OLC advice before Congress voted to accept it. The OLC read Coleman as resting on "a political question rationale" that "has no bearing on the actions of the Archivist, an officer of one of the political branches."
- **The OLC's method as a quasi-standard:** An amendment procedure "must provide a clear rule that is capable of mechanical application, without any need to inquire into the timeliness or substantive validity of the consensus achieved," so "any interpretation that would introduce confusion must be disfavored. . . . The alternative to procedural formalism is uncertainty and litigation."
- **Burden in practice:** Whoever wants an amendment recognised must persuade the certifying officer. The states' mandamus suit against the Archivist over the ERA, which the casebook calls pending, ended without a merits ruling when the D.C. Circuit held they had not shown a clear right to relief (*Illinois v. Ferriero*, 2023) ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).

## Exceptions

- **No presentment.** Settled since *Hollingsworth v. Virginia* (1798); the one Article V ambiguity with a clean judicial answer ([[The Congressional Pay Amendment]]).
- **Congressional deadlines.** [[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]] allows Congress to fix a period as "a matter of detail which Congress may determine as an incident of its power to designate the mode of ratification." The power rests on implication; Article V's text says nothing about it.
- **The two entrenched provisos.** They are Article V's own exceptions, and the 1808 proviso is the only express time rule the framers wrote. That is the OLC's best textual argument against an implied general limit: "[i]f the Framers had contemplated some terminus of the period for ratification of amendments generally, they would have so stated."
- **Rescission after the threshold.** Barred, on the shared premise that the scale-tipping ratification locks the amendment in place ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- **Not decided: rescission before the threshold.** "Is ratification like a light-switch that a state can turn on and then off again?"
- **Not decided: whether the political branches may refuse a ratification that has crossed three-fourths.** Neither the executive branch nor Congress has recognised the ERA, and whether a court may force the Archivist's hand is open ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- **Not decided: limited conventions.** Charles Black read "a Convention for proposing Amendments" as one free to propose "such amendments as that convention decides to propose"; Rappaport argues text and original meaning allow a limited convention; the casebook's harder question is "who would enforce it?" ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
- **Amendment outside Article V.** Ackerman's "constitutional moments" (the Founding, Reconstruction, the New Deal) and Amar's national-referendum route both deny that Article V is exclusive. The casebook's rejoinder: "Why go to the bother of listing the prescribed methods of amendment if they are not the prescribed methods of amendment?"
- **Still-pending proposals.** On the no-deadline view, the 1789 House-size amendment, the 1810 titles-of-nobility amendment, the 1861 Corwin Amendment and the 1920s Child Labor Amendment all remain open. Corwin, "[h]ad it been ratified at the time, . . . would have become the Thirteenth Amendment."

## Leading case

[[Dillon v. Gloss (1921)|Dillon v. Gloss]]

- **Holding:** "[T]he fair inference or implication from Article V is that the ratification must be within some reasonable time after the proposal," and Congress may fix that period in advance; seven years was reasonable.
- **Driving fact:** The Eighteenth Amendment's proposing resolution declared the Amendment "inoperative unless ratified within seven years." Without that clause Dillon had no argument, and the Court had no occasion to ask where a time limit could come from.
- **Starting point:** Article V says nothing about time, "neither that it shall be unlimited nor that it shall be fixed by Congress," so the rule comes from implication: "what is reasonably implied is as much a part of it as what is expressed."
- **Reasoning (Van Devanter, J.), three grounds:** proposal and ratification are "succeeding steps in a single endeavor"; amendments are proposed only when "deemed to be a necessity," implying prompt disposition; and ratification expresses "the approbation of the people," so it must be "sufficiently contemporaneous in that number of States to reflect the will of the people in all sections at relatively the same period."
- **Consequence the Court would not accept:** Four proposals from 1789, 1810 and 1861 were still pending, and their old ratifications could be completed despite coming from "generations now largely forgotten"; the Court called that view "quite untenable." One of the four became the Twenty-seventh Amendment seventy-one years later.
- **Internal tension:** The Court derives a timing requirement from structure and purpose while conceding the text is silent, then hands the content of that requirement to Congress. Coleman widened the gap between principle and enforcer, and the OLC drove through it.
- **Quote:** Jameson, adopted by the Court: an amendment not ratified "early while that sentiment may fairly be supposed to exist" "ought to be regarded as waived, and not again to be voted upon, unless a second time proposed by Congress."

## Best counter-case

[[OLC Opinion on the Congressional Pay Amendment]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Where Congress fixed a deadline, Dillon upholds it; where Congress fixed none, Dillon's contemporaneity language is dictum, and the certifying officer applies the OLC's rule that Article V contains no time limit.
- **What it is:** An executive-branch opinion telling the Supreme Court it was wrong, acted on by the Archivist, and never tested in court. The casebook's Note 1: "This is departmentalism in action."
- **Holding:** "By a straightforward reading of Article V, the amendment is now 'valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of th[e] Constitution,'" and the Archivist was required to certify it under 1 U.S.C. § 106b without waiting for Congress.
- **Why Dillon does not bind:** Dillon's issue "was Congress's authority to limit the period for ratification, not a State's authority to ratify a long-dormant proposed amendment," and Hughes himself called the contemporaneity discussion "merely a dictum" in [[Coleman v. Miller|Coleman]].
- **Argue for the OLC:** "Single endeavor" "simply assumes its conclusion that the process is to be short rather than lengthy." "[N]ecessity is not the same as emergency," and states ratifying in the 1980s and 1990s evidently thought the amendment just as necessary. "Consensus does not demand contemporaneity," because lasting consensus "may just as well be served by a process that allows for extended deliberation."
- **Argue for Dillon:** A Constitution amended by ratifications two centuries apart is not ratified by any identifiable people. Note 2 states the tension: "Isn't Dillon right that there is something fishy about adding an amendment without a real consensus . . . ? But isn't the O.L.C. opinion right that Article V does not say that?"
- **Structural half (Part II):** Giving one branch "ultimate control over the Constitution's very content would be to repudiate the American approach in favor of a return to parliamentary supremacy." If congressional promulgation were required, "the executive branch would have illegally certified every amendment except the Fourteenth." Read with [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
- **The asymmetry:** The Twenty-seventh had no deadline and ripened after 203 years; the ERA had a deadline, missed it, and is dead on the conventional view. If the OLC is right, the only thing killing the ERA is a congressional resolution that Article V never authorized ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Dillon held that a late ratification is invalid."** It held only that Congress may set a deadline and that seven years is reasonable. The contemporaneity language is reasoning, and missing that makes the OLC's attack unintelligible.
- **"Coleman gave Congress the final say."** Coleman had no controlling majority; the Hughes opinion spoke for three Justices and only "suggest[ed]" congressional power or a political question. The OLC reads it as a rule about courts, not about which political branch decides.
- **"A court or Congress validated the Twenty-seventh Amendment."** No court has ruled on it, "because Congress has not yet attempted to violate it." Congress voted 414–3 and 99–0 to accept it, but the Archivist had already decided to certify, and the OLC denies Congress any promulgation role; "What if Congress had voted not to accept it?" is the live question ([[The Riddles of Article V]]).
- **"The ERA has 38 states."** It has 38 only if the deadline is ignored and the five rescissions are disregarded; within the deadline the count is 30 or 35, "both short of the 38 states needed." President Biden's January 2025 statement calling the ERA "the law of the land" changed nothing, because the Archivist did not certify or publish it ([[The Equal Rights Amendment]]).
- **"The Fourteenth Amendment's ratification raises only an Article V question."** Congress replaced Southern governments and conditioned readmission on ratification, which is "remarkably close" to the hypothetical the casebook treats as obviously improper. Amar's answer, that those governments were not "republican," moves the question to [[Republican government - Art. IV § 4 Guarantee Clause]].
- **"Article V is a sideshow."** Pozen and Schmidt argue that "the overwhelming majority of recognized amendments had significant arguable legal infirmities at the time of their adoption," and Article V's difficulty frames the interpretive question in [[Modes of constitutional argument]] and [[Constitutional themes and the dead hand]].
