## Rule statement

Article III fixes very little about the federal courts and leaves the rest to Congress: whether inferior courts exist at all, what business they hear, how many Justices sit, and what classes of case reach the Supreme Court on appeal. The operative rule is [[Ex parte McCardle]]'s — the Court's appellate jurisdiction is "conferred by the Constitution" but only "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make," a repeal reaching pending and argued appeals is "hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception," and "[w]e are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature." Two things Congress cannot do: enlarge the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond the Article III list ([[Marbury v. Madison]]), and use a jurisdictional form to prescribe how a pending cause must be decided (*United States v. Klein*, 1872). The contest is whether McCardle's language survives the case it did not face — a statute closing every federal route to a federal constitutional claim — which is the "essential functions" objection (Hart) against the text-first reading Chase actually gave.

## Elements

1. **Inferior courts: creation, abolition and reorganization.** "Congress have constitutional authority to establish from time to time such inferior tribunals as they may think proper; and to transfer a cause from one such tribunal to another," and "there are no words in the constitution to prohibit or restrain the exercise of legislative power" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]). The Madisonian Compromise is the premise: Article III leaves it to Congress whether to create lower courts at all, and until 1875 they had no general federal-question jurisdiction ([[A Map of Article III]]).
2. **Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction: the Exceptions Clause.** Ask (a) does the statute withdraw a class of cases, (b) does it reach pending cases by its terms, and (c) does any other statutory route survive.
   - Pending cases: "When an act of the legislature is repealed, it must be considered, except as to transactions past and closed, as if it never existed," and the only remaining function is "announcing the fact and dismissing the cause" ([[Ex parte McCardle]]).
   - Surviving routes: the 1868 repeal "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised," and *Ex parte Yerger* (1869) used the 1789 route months later. McCardle closed one door and left another open, which is why it is weaker authority for total stripping than its language sounds.
3. **Original jurisdiction: a ceiling, not a floor.** Congress may not add to the cases listed in Art. III, § 2, cl. 2, because the distribution would otherwise be "mere surplusage" and "form without substance"; "in one class of cases its jurisdiction is original, and not appellate; in the other it is appellate, and not original" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]). Note that the Exceptions Clause attaches only to the appellate half.
4. **Size of the Court.** Article III is silent; the number is statutory and has changed repeatedly. Congress's undoubted power is what forces the objection in the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] onto purpose rather than power.
5. **Individual judges: impeachment.** Hamilton's answer in The Federalist No. 81 to the fear of judicial usurpation was that impeachment "is alone a complete security" ([[A Map of Article III]]). [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] is where the Senate declined to use it against rulings: a majority voted guilty on three of eight articles, none reached two-thirds, and the highest vote (19–15) came on Article VIII, the political harangue to a Baltimore grand jury, not on any ruling.
6. **The limits, stated as questions to run in order.** Does the statute remove a class of cases (valid) or dictate an outcome (invalid)? Does it leave any forum for the federal claim? Does it reach final judgments already entered (it may not)? Does it raise a due process or Suspension Clause problem for a detained claimant?

## Standard of review / burden

There is no judicial standard, because the two questions that matter are ones courts refuse to ask. Motive is off limits: "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature. We can only examine into its power under the Constitution; and the power to make exceptions to the appellate jurisdiction of this court is given by express words" ([[Ex parte McCardle]]). Constitutionality in the teeth of a long practice is answered by the practice itself: "practice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer," and "the question is at rest, and ought not now to be disturbed" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]). What fills the gap is political. The Senate supplies what a court cannot: the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] condemns the 1937 bill precisely for its "frank acknowledgment that neither speed nor 'new blood' in the judiciary is the object of this legislation," but "a change in the decisions of the Court" — the very inquiry McCardle forecloses. Impeachment's threshold is two-thirds of the Senate, and [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] turned on at least six of the President's own party joining the nine Federalists. Where Congress does act, the burden on a litigant is only to find a surviving door: jurisdictional repeals are read to reach only what they name. This module hands off the "who decides" question to [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]] and the case-or-controversy floor to [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].

## Exceptions

- Congress may not prescribe the rule of decision under a jurisdictional label. Chase drew the line himself in *Klein*: "If it simply denied the right of appeal in a particular class of cases, there could be no doubt that it must be regarded as an exercise of the power of Congress," but directing courts how to treat a pardon was "to prescribe a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way." One-sided stripping — removing only the cases where the claimant would win — is the Klein side of the line.
- Congress may not add to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction ([[Marbury v. Madison]]); the holding has never been disturbed.
- "[T]ransactions past and closed" are untouched by a jurisdictional repeal, and Congress may not reopen final judgments (*Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm*).
- The "not both" position: some argue Congress may limit the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction or the lower courts' existence and jurisdiction, but not both, because some federal court must have the last word ([[A Map of Article III]]). On the competing "menu" view, state courts decide finally and may give different final answers in different States.
- Not decided: whether Congress may strip every federal court of jurisdiction over a class of federal constitutional claims. McCardle never faced it, because the 1789 route survived and *Yerger* used it; *Felker v. Turpin* (1996) upheld AEDPA's habeas restrictions partly for the same reason.
- Not decided: whether Congress may abolish a court in order to remove its life-tenured judges. [[Stuart v. Laird]] sustained the 1802 Repeal Act without mentioning good-behaviour tenure, the Appointments Clause or the Compensation Clause, and no displaced 1801 circuit judge was a party.
- Not enforceable in court: the norm against packing and the norm against impeaching judges for their rulings. Both are practice, policed by Congress alone.

## Leading case

[[Ex parte McCardle]]

- Holds that the Exceptions Clause let Congress repeal the Supreme Court's habeas appellate jurisdiction under the 1867 Act, that the repeal reached a case already argued, and that the motive behind it is not a judicial question; appeal "DISMISSED FOR WANT OF JURISDICTION."
- The fact that drove it: the repeal expressly reached "the exercise of any such jurisdiction by said Supreme Court, on appeals which have been, or may hereafter be taken," which left no room to read it as prospective only.
- The second fact that drove it, and the one students miss: the repeal touched only the 1867 route, so the 1789 habeas-with-certiorari route and the original habeas power survived, and the opinion says so.
- Chase concedes the premise of the objection — the jurisdiction is "not derived from acts of Congress" and is "strictly speaking, conferred by the Constitution" — and defeats it with the rest of the same sentence.
- The sentence worth quoting: "It is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception." Pair it with "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law" and with "judicial duty is not less fitly performed by declining ungranted jurisdiction than in exercising firmly that which the Constitution and the laws confer."
- Decided against the background of military Reconstruction: the repeal passed over President Johnson's veto on March 27, 1868, while his impeachment trial was under way ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]), and everyone knew Congress feared a merits ruling.

## Best counter-case

[[Senate Report on Court Packing]]

- Cuts the other way not by denying the power but by attacking its exercise: the committee reported S. 1392 "with the recommendation that it do not pass," calling it "a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle."
- The distinguishing fact is that the objection is to purpose, and purpose is exactly what [[Ex parte McCardle]] says no court will examine. The report can make the argument because the Senate is not a court: it relies on the President's own fireside chat for "the frank acknowledgment that neither speed nor 'new blood' in the judiciary is the object of this legislation."
- The principle stated: "This amounts to nothing more than the declaration that when the Court stands in the way of a legislative enactment, the Congress may reverse the ruling by enlarging the Court," which "would subjugate the courts to the will of Congress and the president and thereby destroy the independence of the judiciary."
- The reciprocity argument, which is why a Democratic committee voted 10–8 against a Democratic President: "If we yield to temptation now to lay the lash upon the Court, we are only teaching others how to apply it to ourselves and to the people."
- Arguing that the norm is constitutional: structure — a Court enlarged to change its answers is not independent in the sense Article III tenure and salary protections presuppose ([[The Federalist No. 78]]) — plus the report's own words, "abandonment of constitutional principle."
- Arguing that the norm is merely political: the size has been set by statute seven times, several changes (1801–02, 1866, 1869) had partisan motives, nothing in Article III fixes nine, and the report's own remedy is "constitutional amendment fixing definite terms," which concedes that a statute would do it.
- The same split runs through [[Stuart v. Laird]] (the 1802 restructuring sustained without inquiry into purpose) and [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (the Senate declining to remove for rulings): in all three the power is Congress's and the restraint is Congress's.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Reading [[Ex parte McCardle]] as holding that Congress may cut off all Supreme Court review. It repealed one statutory route, said so expressly, and *Ex parte Yerger* took jurisdiction months later through the route it reserved. Congress had not in fact stopped Supreme Court review of military detention.
- Treating jurisdiction stripping as overruling the Court. It removes the occasion for a ruling; it does not change the Court's view of the Constitution or disturb judgments already entered.
- Saying [[Stuart v. Laird]] upheld the abolition of the 1801 judgeships, or treating it as a general practice-fixes-meaning case. The opinion never mentions good-behaviour tenure, the Appointments Clause or the Compensation Clause; it decides a transfer-of-causes question and a circuit-riding question, sustains the Repeal Act by silence, and rests the circuit-riding holding entirely on twelve years of practice with no textual analysis.
- Overstating [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]. The Senate never voted on whether impeachable offences must be indictable; the acquittal is a precedent of restraint, and later practice (Pickering, removed for drunkenness and misconduct on the bench) has not required a crime.
- Treating the nine-Justice Court as constitutionally fixed. It is statutory, has ranged from six to ten, and the only barrier to changing it is the political one the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] supplies.
- Forgetting that the Exceptions Clause attaches only to appellate jurisdiction. A hypothetical that has Congress "excepting" a case from the Court's original jurisdiction, or adding one to it, is testing [[Marbury v. Madison]], not McCardle.
