## Rule statement

Article III fixes little about the federal courts and leaves the rest to Congress: whether inferior courts exist, what they hear, how many Justices sit, and which cases reach the Supreme Court on appeal. The operative rule is [[Ex parte McCardle]]'s: appellate jurisdiction exists "with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make"; a repeal reaching argued appeals is valid ("hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception"); and "[w]e are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature." Congress may not enlarge the Court's original jurisdiction ([[Marbury v. Madison]]). The contest is whether McCardle survives the case it did not face — a statute closing every federal route to a constitutional claim — where Hart's "essential functions" view and the "not both" position oppose Chase's text-first reading.

## Elements

1. **Does the statute create, abolish or reorganize inferior courts, or move causes among them?** Valid.
   - "Congress have constitutional authority to establish from time to time such inferior tribunals as they may think proper; and to transfer a cause from one such tribunal to another," and "there are no words in the constitution to prohibit or restrain the exercise of legislative power" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]).
   - The Madisonian Compromise is the premise: Article III leaves it to Congress whether to create lower courts at all, and until 1875 they had no general federal-question jurisdiction ([[A Map of Article III]]).
   - Consequence: many federal questions are decided in state courts, reviewable by the Supreme Court under [[Martin v. Hunter's Lessee]].
2. **Does the statute withdraw a class of cases from the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction?** Valid under the Exceptions Clause; then ask whether it reaches pending cases and whether another route survives.
   - Pending cases: a repealed statute is treated, "except as to transactions past and closed, as if it never existed," and the only remaining function is "announcing the fact and dismissing the cause" ([[Ex parte McCardle]]).
   - Surviving routes: the 1868 repeal "does not affect the jurisdiction which was previously exercised," so the 1789 habeas route and the original habeas power remained. McCardle closed one door and said another was open.
3. **Does the statute add to or subtract from the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction?** The original list is a ceiling, not a floor.
   - Otherwise the distribution would be "mere surplusage" and "form without substance"; "in one class of cases its jurisdiction is original, and not appellate; in the other it is appellate, and not original" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]).
   - The Exceptions Clause attaches only to the appellate half, so a hypothetical about original jurisdiction is testing Marbury, not McCardle.
4. **Does the statute change the size of the Court?** Within the power.
   - Article III is silent; the number is statutory and has ranged from six to ten ([[A Map of Article III]]).
   - Because the power is undoubted, the objection in the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] has to be to purpose, not power.
5. **Is Congress removing an individual judge?** Only by impeachment.
   - Hamilton (Federalist No. 81) answered the fear of judicial usurpation with impeachment: "This is alone a complete security" ([[A Map of Article III]]).
   - [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] is where the Senate declined to use impeachment against rulings: a majority voted guilty on three of eight articles, none reached two-thirds, and the highest vote (19–15) came on Article VIII, the political harangue to a Baltimore grand jury, not on any ruling.
6. **Does the statute cross one of the outer limits?** Run these in order.
   - Does it remove a class of cases (valid) or dictate the outcome of a pending cause (invalid)?
   - Does it leave any forum, federal or state, for the federal claim?
   - Does it reach "transactions past and closed," such as final judgments (it may not)?
   - Does it leave a detained claimant without a forum, raising a due process or Suspension Clause problem ([[Suspension of habeas corpus - Art. I § 9 cl. 2]])?

## Standard of review / burden

- **Not reviewed — motive:** "We are not at liberty to inquire into the motives of the legislature. We can only examine into its power under the Constitution; and the power to make exceptions to the appellate jurisdiction of this court is given by express words" ([[Ex parte McCardle]]).
- **Not reviewed — long practice:** "[P]ractice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer," and "the question is at rest, and ought not now to be disturbed" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]).
- **Burden on a litigant:** Only to find a surviving door. Jurisdictional repeals are read to reach only what they name ([[Ex parte McCardle]]).
- **What fills the gap — the Senate:** The [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] condemns the 1937 bill for "the frank acknowledgment that neither speed nor 'new blood' in the judiciary is the object of this legislation," but "a change in the decisions of the Court" — the very inquiry McCardle forecloses to courts.
- **What fills the gap — impeachment:** Conviction needs two-thirds of the Senate, and [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] failed because at least six Jeffersonian Republicans joined the nine Federalists.
- **Hand-offs:** The "who decides" question goes to [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]]; the case-or-controversy floor to [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].

## Exceptions

- **No rule of decision under a jurisdictional label (outside the assigned readings):** In *United States v. Klein* (1872), Chase, distinguishing [[Ex parte McCardle]], contrasted a statute that "simply denied the right of appeal in a particular class of cases" with one that would "prescribe a rule for the decision of a cause in a particular way." One-sided stripping — removing only the cases the claimant would win — falls on the Klein side.
- **Original jurisdiction:** Congress may not add to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction ([[Marbury v. Madison]]); the holding remains the law.
- **Transactions past and closed:** A jurisdictional repeal leaves closed transactions untouched ([[Ex parte McCardle]]). The modern rule that Congress may not reopen final judgments (*Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm*) is outside the assigned readings.
- **The "not both" position:** Congress may limit the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction, or the lower courts' existence and jurisdiction, but not both, because some federal court must have the last word. On the competing "menu" view, state courts decide finally and may give different "final" answers in different States ([[A Map of Article III]]).
- **Not decided:** Whether Congress may strip every federal court of jurisdiction over a class of federal constitutional claims. McCardle never faced it because the 1789 route survived. Outside the assigned readings, *Ex parte Yerger* (1869) used that route months later, and *Felker v. Turpin* (1996) upheld AEDPA's habeas limits partly because original habeas remained.
- **Not decided:** Whether Congress may abolish a court in order to remove its life-tenured judges. [[Stuart v. Laird]] sustained the 1802 Repeal Act without mentioning good-behaviour tenure, the Appointments Clause or the Compensation Clause, and no displaced 1801 circuit judge was a party.
- **Not enforceable in court:** The norms against packing the Court and against impeaching judges for their rulings. Both are practice, policed by Congress alone.

## Leading case

[[Ex parte McCardle]]

- **Holding:** The Exceptions Clause let Congress repeal the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over habeas appeals under the 1867 Act; the repeal reached a case already argued; motive is not a judicial question. Appeal "DISMISSED FOR WANT OF JURISDICTION" (8–0; Chase, C.J.).
- **Driving fact:** The repeal expressly reached jurisdiction "on appeals which have been, or may hereafter be taken," leaving no room to read it as prospective only.
- **Second driving fact:** The repeal touched only the 1867 route; the 1789 habeas-with-certiorari route and the original habeas power survived, and the opinion says so.
- **Reasoning (Chase, C.J.):** The jurisdiction is "not derived from acts of Congress" and is "strictly speaking, conferred by the Constitution" — but conferred with the exceptions Congress makes, so the premise of the objection defeats it.
- **Quote:** "It is hardly possible to imagine a plainer instance of positive exception." Pair it with "Jurisdiction is power to declare the law" and "judicial duty is not less fitly performed by declining ungranted jurisdiction than in exercising firmly that which the Constitution and the laws confer."
- **Context:** The repeal passed over President Johnson's veto on March 27, 1868, during his impeachment trial ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]), and everyone knew Congress feared a merits ruling against military Reconstruction.

## Best counter-case

[[Senate Report on Court Packing]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Where Congress uses an undoubted power over the courts to change their answers rather than their workload, no court will stop it, and the objection must be made — and enforced — in Congress.
- **Verdict:** The committee reported S. 1392 "with the recommendation that it do not pass," calling it "a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle."
- **The objection is to purpose:** Purpose is exactly what [[Ex parte McCardle]] says no court will examine. The Senate could make the argument because it is not a court, relying on the President's own "frank acknowledgment that neither speed nor 'new blood' in the judiciary is the object of this legislation."
- **Principle stated:** "[W]hen the Court stands in the way of a legislative enactment, the Congress may reverse the ruling by enlarging the Court," which "would subjugate the courts to the will of Congress and the president and thereby destroy the independence of the judiciary."
- **Reciprocity:** A Democratic committee voted 10–8 against a Democratic President: "If we yield to temptation now to lay the lash upon the Court, we are only teaching others how to apply it to ourselves and to the people."
- **Argue that the norm is constitutional:** A Court enlarged to change its answers is not independent in the sense Article III tenure and salary protections presuppose ([[The Federalist No. 78]]); the report itself calls packing an "abandonment of constitutional principle."
- **Argue that the norm is merely political:** The size has changed by statute seven times; several changes (1801–02, 1866, 1869) had partisan motives; nothing in Article III fixes nine; and the report's own remedy is "constitutional amendment fixing definite terms," which concedes that ordinary legislation could otherwise do it.
- **Same pattern elsewhere:** [[Stuart v. Laird]] (the 1802 restructuring sustained without inquiry into purpose) and [[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]] (the Senate declining to remove for rulings). In all three the power is Congress's and the restraint is Congress's.
- **What the Constitution requires:** One original-meaning reading takes "Exceptions . . . and . . . Regulations" to concern fact review and housekeeping, not the removal of constitutional questions; [[The Federalist No. 78]] and [[Brutus No. 11]] both assumed the Court would pass on the constitutionality of laws.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"McCardle holds that Congress may cut off all Supreme Court review."** McCardle repealed one statutory route and said so expressly. Outside the assigned readings, *Ex parte Yerger* took jurisdiction months later through the reserved route, so Congress had not in fact stopped Supreme Court review of military detention.
- **"Jurisdiction stripping overrules the Court."** Stripping removes the occasion for a ruling; it does not change the Court's view of the Constitution or disturb judgments already entered.
- **"[[Stuart v. Laird]] upheld abolishing the 1801 judgeships."** The opinion never mentions good-behaviour tenure, the Appointments Clause or the Compensation Clause. It decides a transfer-of-causes question and a circuit-riding question, sustains the Repeal Act by silence, and rests the circuit-riding holding entirely on twelve years of practice.
- **"The Chase acquittal decided that impeachable offences must be indictable."** The Senate never voted on that principle. The acquittal is a precedent of restraint, and later practice (Pickering, removed for drunkenness and misconduct on the bench) has not required a crime ([[The Impeachment of Justice Chase]]).
- **"Nine Justices is constitutionally fixed."** The number is statutory, has ranged from six to ten, and the only barrier to changing it is the political one the [[Senate Report on Court Packing]] supplies.
- **"The Exceptions Clause reaches original jurisdiction."** It attaches only to appellate jurisdiction. Congress "excepting" a case from the Court's original jurisdiction, or adding one to it, is a [[Marbury v. Madison]] problem.
