## Rule statement

Congress may spend for "the general Welfare" on objects outside its other enumerated powers, and it may attach conditions to the money that it could not impose by direct regulation, subject to four limits and one caveat: the spending must serve the general welfare, the condition must be stated unambiguously, it must be related to the federal interest in the particular national project or program, and it must not induce the State to do something independently unconstitutional — and the inducement must not be "so coercive as to pass the point at which 'pressure turns into compulsion.'" The Hamiltonian reading of clause 1 is the operative law: the spending power "is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution," and "the constitutional limitations on Congress when exercising its spending power are less exacting than those on its authority to regulate directly." The contest is the founding-era one, and it is still live in two forms: Madison's position that "general Welfare" states the purpose of the taxing power and is "limited and explained by the particular enumeration subjoined," so Congress may spend only in aid of an enumerated power; and the prior textual doubt the casebook raises, that clause 1 grants a power "To lay and collect Taxes" and may confer no independent spending power at all, in which case the source is the Necessary and Proper Clause (Madison's result by another route) or Article IV's power to dispose of federal property.

## Elements

1. **Identify the exercise as spending rather than regulating.** A condition that "go[es] beyond specifying how the money should be spent" is, on O'Connor's view in [[South Dakota v. Dole]], "not a condition, but a regulation, which is valid only if it falls within one of Congress' delegated regulatory powers." The majority rejects that line, but it is the first question to raise, and it is the one that decides a conditional-spending hypothetical if the examiner wants the doctrine tested rather than recited.
2. **General welfare.** The expenditure must be "in pursuit of 'the general welfare,'" but "courts should defer substantially to the judgment of Congress." Hamilton's own qualification — that the object "be General and not local; its operation extending in fact, or by possibility, throughout the Union, and not being confined to a particular spot" ([[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]]) — survives as a stated limit and has never been enforced.
3. **Unambiguous notice.** Spending legislation is "much in the nature of a contract," so Congress must state the condition clearly enough to enable "the States to exercise their choice knowingly, cognizant of the consequences of their participation." A condition inferred from a program's purposes fails; a condition written into the statute does not.
4. **Germaneness.** The condition must not be "unrelated 'to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs.'" Two competing versions:
   - *Shared purpose (majority).* A drinking age is germane to highway money because safe interstate travel is "one of the main purposes for which highway funds are expended."
   - *Marginal effect (O'Connor).* The condition must make the funded program work better; "the efficacy, the marginal impact, of the highway spending is the same whether or not there is a 21-year-old drinking age."
5. **No independent constitutional bar.** The limit means only "that the power may not be used to induce the States to engage in activities that would themselves be unconstitutional" — "invidiously discriminatory state action or the infliction of cruel and unusual punishment" — and is expressly "not . . . a prohibition on the indirect achievement of objectives which Congress is not empowered to achieve directly."
6. **Coercion.** Ask three questions in order, from [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]:
   - *Does the condition put existing, independent grants at risk, or only the new money?* Conditions on the use of the new funds are the ordinary case; threatening "other significant independent grants" is "a means of pressuring the States to accept policy changes."
   - *Is the change in substance a new program or a modification of the existing one?* Congress's own label is "irrelevant" if the change "is not properly viewed as a modification of the existing Medicaid program."
   - *How large is the sum, measured against the State's budget?* Five percent of highway funds — under half of one percent of South Dakota's budget — is "relatively mild encouragement"; all federal Medicaid funds, over ten percent of a State's overall budget, "is a gun to the head."

## Standard of review / burden

- The challenger bears the burden throughout, and on the first limit the review is nearly nominal: "the concept of welfare or the opposite is shaped by Congress," and the line between general and particular welfare exists but "[t]he discretion, however, is not confided to the courts." Madison predicted exactly this in the Bonus Bill veto, arguing that the broad reading would exclude "the judicial authority of the United States from its participation in guarding the boundary between the legislative powers of the General and the State Governments, inasmuch as questions relating to the general welfare, being questions of policy and expediency, are unsusceptible of judicial cognizance and decision."
- The notice limit runs on a contract standard rather than a deference standard: the question is whether the State could know what it was agreeing to when it took the money, which is a reading question for the court.
- Coercion is reviewed as a question of degree with no stated threshold. The Court refuses to "fix the outermost line," saying only that "wherever that line may be, this statute is surely beyond it," and it refuses to infer coercion from a condition's effectiveness: a grant is not "unconstitutional simply by reason of its success in achieving the congressional objective."
- The Court will not treat temptation as compulsion, because doing so "is to plunge the law in endless difficulties" and ends in "the acceptance of a philosophical determinism by which choice becomes impossible"; the State always retains "the 'simple expedient' of not yielding."
- The track record is the best statement of the standard. Butler in 1936 was the last decision striking a statute as beyond the Spending Clause until the Medicaid holding in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] — one invalidation in seventy-six years.
- The remedy where coercion is found is narrow: bar the threat, not the program. The Secretary "cannot apply § 1396c to withdraw existing Medicaid funds for failure to comply with the requirements set out in the expansion," and the rest of the statute stands.

## Exceptions

- **Conditions on how the money itself is spent.** Always valid, and conceded by every opinion: Congress appropriating money to train state police may require the money be used to train state police, and "[w]hen Congress appropriates money to build a highway, it is entitled to insist that the highway be a safe one."
- **New grants with conditions attached.** Expressly preserved in [[NFIB v. Sebelius]]: nothing forbids Congress from offering funds and requiring that States accepting them comply with the conditions on their use; the defect was the threat to the pre-existing grant.
- **Genuine modifications of an existing program.** Congress reserves the right to amend its grant programs, and States have "no entitlement to receive any Medicaid funds," so an amendment that does not work "a basic change in the nature" of the program may be enforced against all of its funds.
- **Independent constitutional bars that actually bite.** The Bill of Attainder Clause is the worked example: [[United States v. Lovett]] is Dole's fourth limit doing real work, where Congress used an appropriations condition against three named individuals.
- **Spending is not regulating.** Hamilton's own concession is a limit still available: "A power to appropriate money with this latitude which is granted too in express terms would not carry a power to do any other thing, not authorised in the constitution, either expressly or by fair implication" ([[Hamilton, Report on Manufactures]]). Hamilton assumed appropriation and command were different things; conditional grants are the hole in that assumption.
- **The narrow reading, if it ever returns.** Madison and Jefferson in [[The Bank Debate]] both read "general Welfare" as stating the purpose of the taxing power rather than granting a separate one, on the ground that the broad reading "would render nugatory the enumeration of particular powers"; on that view every appropriation must be traced to an enumerated power, and conditional-grant doctrine collapses into the Necessary and Proper Clause.
- **Not decided: the source of the spending power.** The clause grants a power "To lay and collect Taxes" and "reads like a limitation on the purposes for which taxes may be imposed"; whether the spending power comes from the general-welfare language, from the Necessary and Proper Clause (which would leave "no free-standing power to spend"), or from Article IV, § 3's power to dispose of federal property is unresolved, and the choice decides how much of the doctrine survives.
- **Not decided: the outer line of coercion**, the effect of a percentage between 5 and 100, whether the coercion rule protects private grant recipients (it is grounded in state sovereignty and anti-commandeering, so probably not), and whether Congress could legislate a national drinking age directly under the Twenty-first Amendment.

## Leading case

[[South Dakota v. Dole]]

- Holds that 23 U.S.C. § 158, which directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold five percent of specified highway funds from States allowing alcohol purchase or public possession under age 21, is a valid conditional exercise of the spending power (7–2).
- The fact that drove the result is the size of the withholding: five percent of certain highway grants is "relatively mild encouragement," and "the argument as to coercion is shown to be more rhetoric than fact." Change the number and the case changes.
- The second driving fact is South Dakota's concession that a uniform drinking age was not "unrelated to a national concern in the absence of the Twenty-first Amendment," which the majority treats as disposing of germaneness and O'Connor says was no concession at all.
- The Court redefines the fourth limit so that it asks whether the *State's compliance* would be unconstitutional, not whether *Congress's direct regulation* would be — which is what lets Congress buy what the Twenty-first Amendment may forbid it to command.
- The Court expressly reserves whether Congress could "legislate directly a national minimum drinking age," so the case decides the price of the statute, not Congress's power to write it.
- O'Connor's dissent supplies the counter-rule and the best exam language: § 158 "is a regulation determining who shall be able to drink liquor," and the majority's rule would let "the Congress . . . effectively regulate almost any area of a State's social, political, or economic life on the theory that use of the interstate transportation system is somehow enhanced."
- She also supplies the over- and under-inclusiveness attack: the condition "stops teenagers from drinking even when they are not about to drive on interstate highways," while eighty-four percent of alcohol-related highway fatalities involve people over 21.
- The sentence worth quoting is Rehnquist's caveat, because it is the only doctrinal hook a State ever gets: an inducement "might be so coercive as to pass the point at which 'pressure turns into compulsion.'"

## Best counter-case

[[NFIB v. Sebelius]]

- The Medicaid holding (7–2 on coercion; 5–4 on remedy) is the only time the Court has found a spending condition coercive: threatening a State's existing Medicaid funds to force acceptance of the expansion is "economic dragooning that leaves the States with no real option but to acquiesce."
- The distinguishing facts are two and both are needed. **Size:** Dole risked under half of one percent of South Dakota's budget; Medicaid is over twenty percent of the average State's budget, with federal money covering 50 to 83 percent of it, so the threat reached over ten percent of the State's whole budget. **Structure:** the threat reached *existing* grants for refusing a program Roberts treats as *new*, where Dole's condition attached to new money.
- **Arguing for the State (coercion):** spending legislation is "much in the nature of a contract" and is legitimate only if the State "voluntarily and knowingly accepts the terms"; the coercion limit follows from anti-commandeering, because "the Constitution simply does not give Congress the authority to require the States to regulate," whether directly or by an inducement no State can refuse; and the States "must either accept a basic change in the nature of Medicaid, or risk losing all Medicaid funding," which is "a gun to the head."
- **Arguing for the United States:** Medicaid is "a single program with a constant aim," Congress expressly reserved the power to amend it, the States have "no entitlement to receive any Medicaid funds," and unlike Dole the condition relates solely to the funded program and induces nothing Congress could not do itself, since it "could operate its own health-care program for poor persons." Add Steward Machine's warning that equating temptation with coercion "plunge[s] the law in endless difficulties."
- **The facts that flip it:** offer the expansion as a separate new grant and the coercion disappears entirely; make the change a modest amendment to existing Medicaid — an added covered service, a small eligibility adjustment — and the reserved power to amend carries it; drop the threatened amount to five percent of existing Medicaid funds and the case looks like Dole again. Roberts relies on both size and the new-program characterization and never says which is indispensable.
- **The weak joint in the holding** is the new-versus-old distinction: Roberts says Congress's styling of the expansion as an amendment is "irrelevant," Ginsburg says treating them as two programs "begs the question," and no test is offered for telling a modification from a new program other than a "basic change in the nature" of the old one.
- **Note what the case does not do:** it does not touch germaneness (expanding Medicaid to more poor adults is germane to Medicaid), does not disturb Dole's four limits, and does not extend to private grant recipients, because the rationale is state sovereignty.
- Runner-up counter-authority for the original-meaning half of the question: [[Madison, Veto Message on the Bonus Bill]], with the 55–34 vote against disaster relief in [[The Savannah Fire Debate]] as the founding-era application.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Stating Dole's fourth limit as "Congress may not do indirectly what it cannot do directly."** That is precisely the reading the Court rejected. The limit forbids only conditions that would induce the State to act unconstitutionally; the whole point of the case is that Congress may buy what it may not command.
- **Treating the general-welfare limit as a real screen.** It is the limit Madison predicted no court would enforce, and no court has: "The discretion, however, is not confided to the courts." An answer that spends a paragraph on general welfare and a sentence on germaneness has the weighting backwards — and the germaneness question is the one that overlaps [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]], since the majority's shared-purpose test is more permissive than even [[McCulloch v. Maryland]]'s generous standard for means.
- **Reciting four limits and forgetting coercion.** Coercion sits outside the four as a caveat drawn from Steward Machine, and it is the only one that has ever invalidated a statute. Count five things, not four.
- **Reading [[NFIB v. Sebelius]] as a holding about percentages.** Roberts gives a comparison, not a threshold, and expressly declines to "fix the outermost line." An answer that asserts a number invents doctrine; the argument runs on the size *and* the existing-versus-new distinction together.
- **Citing Butler for a limited spending power.** Butler adopted Hamilton's broad reading in the same opinion that struck the statute down, and the restrictive half — that Congress may not indirectly accomplish by taxing and spending what it cannot regulate directly — is exactly what Dole repudiates. Both Rehnquist and O'Connor cite Butler, for opposite propositions.
- **Assuming the founding-era record belongs to one side.** Conditional grants are the workaround to anti-commandeering, so that rule is a drafting rule wherever the price can be met ([[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]]); the First Congress funded every lighthouse "from Maine to Georgia" while rejecting a national university, and "[i]n each case the scope of the spending power was debated, but not resolved"; Madison's own practice ran against his theory, with the codfish subsidy recharacterized as tax relief and St. Domingo relief as repayment of the French debt; and Hamilton's stated limit that the object be "General and not local" makes relief to one burned city, and most earmarks, hard to defend on his own terms.
