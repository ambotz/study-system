## Rule statement

Article VI does three things in three unnumbered clauses: it carries the Confederation's debts forward, it makes "[t]his Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties made, or which shall be made, under the Authority of the United States" the supreme law of the land binding "the Judges in every State," and it binds federal and state officers alike by oath "to support this Constitution" while forbidding any religious test for federal office. The operative consequences are a priority rule and a personal obligation: in a conflict, federal law wins "any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding," and a federal officer doing what federal law requires cannot be punished by a State for doing it ([[In re Neagle]]). The contested question is how far "Laws of the United States" reaches beyond enacted text — Neagle says "any obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument . . . is a 'law,'" while the clause's own "in Pursuance thereof" language suggests law made by a procedure, which is Lamar's reading and the reason executive agreements sit uneasily in the same sentence ([[A Note on Executive Agreements]]).

## Elements

1. **Identify which of the three supreme-law categories the federal thing falls into, because they are qualified differently.** The Constitution is supreme without qualification; statutes only if "made in Pursuance thereof"; treaties if made "under the Authority of the United States," language drafted to cover treaties predating the Constitution and therefore not meaning "in pursuance of" it.
2. **Ask whether the federal instrument is on the list at all.** Executive agreements are not named. They "may have legal status as a matter of international law, but their status as domestic law is trickier" ([[A Note on Executive Agreements]]).
3. **Separate the priority question from the scope question.** "[A]ny Thing . . . to the Contrary notwithstanding" tells a judge which law wins; it does not say how large the field of federal law is. Every preemption fight is therefore a fight about the scope of the federal statute or of the enumerated power behind it, which is why [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] spends its energy on the Necessary and Proper Clause before it reaches Maryland's tax.
4. **Identify who is bound, by name and by omission.** The Supremacy Clause names state judges and only state judges. The oath clause names "[t]he Senators and Representatives . . . the Members of the several State Legislatures, and all executive and judicial Officers, both of the United States and of the several States." The President is omitted because Article II, § 1 gives him his own oath ("preserve, protect and defend").
5. **For supremacy applied to executive action, run the Neagle chain and name the weak link at each stage.** Rev. Stat. § 753 (habeas for an act done "in pursuance of a law of the United States"); Rev. Stat. § 788 (marshals have the powers sheriffs have under state law, the one statutory link, and it borrows state law for its content); the Attorney General's letter, which is not law at all; and the Article II, § 3 duty, which is the thing in dispute.
6. **Ask what "the Laws" in the Take Care Clause includes.** Miller's question answers itself in the opinion's structure: "Is this duty limited to the enforcement of acts of congress or of treaties of the United States according to their express terms; or does it include the rights, duties, and obligations growing out of the constitution itself, our international relations, and all the protection implied by the nature of the government under the constitution?"
7. **For the oath, state the object and then the unanswered question.** The officer swears to the Constitution, not to Congress and not to the Court; the clause does not say whose reading he is sworn to, which is the whole of [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].
8. **For the religious-test clause, check the three limits.** Federal office only; every "Office or public Trust"; and written as a permanent prohibition ("no religious Test shall ever be required"), paired in the same sentence with the oath.

## Standard of review / burden

For a federal officer held by a State, the question on habeas is whether the act was federally authorized, not whether a statute commanded it, and the federal court may resolve necessity itself on the habeas record rather than sending it to a state jury: the circuit court took testimony, found that Neagle "did justifiably interfere by shooting Terry while in the act of assaulting Mr. Justice Field," and the Supreme Court affirmed the discharge ([[In re Neagle]]). Lamar's dissent puts the burden the other way — a federal defence is a defence, not an exemption, "[w]hether his defense is sufficient or not is for the court which tries him to determine," and the remedy is writ of error after trial — and the answer is institutional rather than textual: an officer who must stand trial in a hostile state court for doing his duty is deterred from doing it, so the protection must run to the process and not only to the judgment. For ordinary supremacy there is no special standard; the litigated question is the scope of the federal power or statute. For the oath there is no standard at all, because the clause names no arbiter; what fills the gap is each officer's own judgment, checked politically — see [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]] and, for the executive side of the same clause, [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]]. For executive agreements the practical "standard" is durability rather than review: an instrument that binds only as long as one President wants it to is not law in the Article VI sense, and the Cotton letter's prediction that a successor "could revoke such an agreement with the stroke of a pen" proved true.

## Exceptions

- Executive agreements are outside the clause's enumeration, and the easy case must be separated from the hard one: an agreement in which the President promises only to exercise powers he already holds is lawful; whether he may promise "to do things that otherwise lie outside of unilateral presidential authority — such as preempting state law" is the open question ([[A Note on Executive Agreements]]).
- An agreement that executes a statute displaces state law more easily, because the Supremacy Clause does cover statutes; the Litvinov line (*Belmont*, *Pink*) is the high-water mark, and *Medellín v. Texas* refused to let the President use a non-self-executing treaty to override Texas criminal procedure.
- The clause binds state judges and says nothing about state executives. [[Printz v. United States]] builds the anti-commandeering rule on the omission: Congress may direct state courts but not commandeer state executive officers — see [[State sovereignty and anti-commandeering - Tenth Amendment]].
- The religious-test clause reaches only offices "under the Authority of the United States," not state office.
- The oath clause does not reach the President, whose oath is prescribed separately in Article II, § 1.
- Neagle says nothing about constitutional limits on the means. A federal officer who conducts a warrantless search to protect a judge has authority to act under Neagle and no answer to an individual right; a State's criminal law is the only thing the case displaces.
- Not decided: what "Law of the United States" means when the clause's own vocabulary is used against Miller. Lamar's objection — habeas itself is a creature of enacted law, and "[t]he power to award the writ . . . must be given by written law" — was never answered, and the "in Pursuance thereof" asymmetry is the strongest text on his side.

## Leading case

[[In re Neagle]]

- Holds that a deputy marshal guarding a Justice under no statute acted "in pursuance of a law of the United States" for habeas purposes, and that California could not hold him for trial on a murder charge for the killing.
- The fact that drove it: there was no statute. "It is not supposed that any special act of Congress exists which authorizes the marshals or deputy marshals of the United States in express terms to accompany the judges." The absence of enacted authority is the premise of the holding, not an obstacle to it.
- The definitional sentence worth quoting: "[A]ny obligation fairly and properly inferrible from that instrument, or any duty of the marshal to be derived from the general scope of his duties under the laws of the United States, is a 'law,' within the meaning of this phrase."
- The federal-peace move, which supplies the officer: "That there is a peace of the United States; that a man assaulting a judge of the United States while in the discharge of his duties violates that peace," combined with Rev. Stat. § 788, makes the marshal stand to the peace of the United States as the sheriff stands to the peace of the State.
- The consequence argument: on the contrary view the government would be "without the means of self-protection," and the mails hypothetical shows the power does not stop at judges.
- The dissent's frame, which is the better exam frame: Lamar restates the question as whether Neagle "shall in this ex parte proceeding be discharged and delivered from any trial or further inquiry in any court, state or federal," and answers that "[n]o officer or employe of the United States is placed by his position, or the services he is called to perform, above responsibility to the legal tribunals of the country."
- Where it sits doctrinally: Neagle reads Article II, § 3 as a source of power where Congress has legislated nothing, while [[United States v. Cox]] and [[Adams v. Richardson]] read the same clause as describing a duty owed with respect to statutes Congress passed.

## Best counter-case

[[A Note on Executive Agreements]]

- Cuts against any inference that whatever the executive does is supreme law, and it does so from the clause itself: the Supremacy Clause names the Constitution, laws made in pursuance of it, and treaties — and stops. "(Look at the text of the Supremacy Clause in Article VI. Do you see why?)"
- The distinguishing fact is what the instrument is asked to do. Neagle asked only that federal authority defeat a state prosecution of a federal officer for performing a federal duty; an executive agreement asks that a presidential instrument operate as domestic law against a State, which is the thing the enumeration omits.
- The worked example is the JCPOA: never submitted as a treaty, and under the Iran Nuclear Agreement Review Act it "could go into effect as an executive agreement with the support of one third of one of the two Houses of Congress."
- Reversibility is the structural point. Forty-one senators warned that "[t]he next president could revoke such an agreement with the stroke of a pen"; the casebook's verdict is "That turned out to be true," and Trump withdrew "relying on the same unilateral foreign affairs power as President Obama had."
- Arguing for the broad reading: the government needs "the means of self-protection," the executive must be able to act in fields Congress has left empty, and the *Belmont*–*Pink* line held that a recognition settlement displaced state law.
- Arguing for the narrow reading: the clause's own vocabulary makes supreme law the product of a procedure, *Medellín* refused to let a presidential instrument override state criminal procedure, and an instrument that lasts only as long as one President is not law in the Article VI sense.
- The exam formulation to carry: the Supremacy Clause's asymmetry between statutes "made in Pursuance thereof" and treaties "made . . . under the Authority of the United States" is also the strongest textual argument for judicial review in [[Marbury v. Madison]], because some organ must decide which statutes qualify — so the same sentence that powers [[Judicial review - Art. III and the Supremacy Clause]] is the sentence that embarrasses Neagle.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Treating the Supremacy Clause as a source of federal power. It is a priority rule addressed to state judges; it decides which law wins in a conflict and says nothing about how far federal power reaches, which is why the work in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] is done by the Necessary and Proper Clause.
- Quoting the oath clause as binding the President. Article VI's list omits him; his oath is Article II, § 1's "preserve, protect and defend," and the Constitution swears everyone twice over on purpose.
- Assuming "Laws of the United States" means the same thing in Article VI and in Rev. Stat. § 753. [[In re Neagle]] gives the statutory phrase a reading that the constitutional phrase's "in Pursuance thereof" language resists, and the mismatch is the case's soft spot.
- Reading [[In re Neagle]] as holding that Neagle was innocent. The holding is that he need not be tried at all in a state court; the Court resolved necessity on a habeas record, which is what the dissent objected to.
- Saying executive agreements are "supreme law of the land like treaties." They are not named in the clause, their domestic status is unsettled, and *Medellín* cuts against the assumption.
- Reading [[Printz v. United States]] as flowing from the Tenth Amendment alone. Its textual engine is Article VI's omission: the clause names state judges and says nothing about state executives.
