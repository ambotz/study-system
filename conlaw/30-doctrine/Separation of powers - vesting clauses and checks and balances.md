## Rule statement

Each branch may exercise only the power the Constitution gives it, and one branch cannot take over another's power even with that branch's consent. Art. I, § 1 vests "[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress; Art. II, § 1 vests "[t]he executive Power" in a President; Art. III, § 1 vests "[t]he judicial Power of the United States" in the courts. Where the Constitution shares a power, the shared procedure is the only lawful route ([[INS v. Chadha]], [[Clinton v. City of New York]]). Because paper boundaries are mere "parchment barriers," each branch gets "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others" (The Federalist Nos. 48 and 51, in [[A Map of Article I]]). The contest is formalism, which asks who holds the power (Burger in Chadha; Black in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]), against functionalism, which asks whether one branch gains at another's expense (White in Chadha; Jackson in Youngstown). Beneath both lies the original-meaning question whether Article II's omission of "herein granted" vests a residual executive power.

## Elements

1. **Is the act legislative, executive or judicial in character and effect, whatever its label or author?**
   - Action is legislative when it "alter[s] the legal rights, duties, and relations of persons . . . outside the Legislative Branch" ([[INS v. Chadha]]).
   - A statutory label does not control: the FEC was described as a legislative-branch body and still exercised executive power ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
   - "Quasi legislative" and "quasi judicial" are not categories: an agency executing a statute against private parties "exercises executive power — no ifs, ands, or quasis about it" ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]).
2. **Which vesting clause, and which specific clause, carries the power?**
   - Article I: only the powers "herein granted," listed mostly in § 8.
   - Article III: "the judicial Power," followed by the list of cases and controversies.
   - Article II: "the executive Power," followed by "a seemingly random assortment" of specific powers ([[Introduction to Article II]]).
   - An unlisted power such as removal must come from the Vesting Clause or from nowhere; see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].
3. **Does the text share the power, and was the shared procedure followed?**
   - Lawmaking: passage by both Houses and presentment (Art. I, § 7); see [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
   - Principal officers: presidential nomination plus Senate consent (Art. II, § 2, cl. 2); see [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].
   - Impeachment: the House impeaches and the Senate tries, two-thirds to convict; see [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].
   - War: Congress declares, the President commands; see [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief]].
   - Spending: no money leaves the Treasury without an appropriation (Art. I, § 9, cl. 7); see [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]].
4. **If one House acted alone, does an express exception authorize the solo action?**
   - Only four one-House actions carry the force of law: House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation, Senate treaty ratification ([[INS v. Chadha]]).
   - Where the Framers meant one House to act alone "they did so in explicit, unambiguous terms."
5. **Did the affected branch consent? Consent does not cure.**
   - Congress cannot keep a legislative veto that Presidents accepted "as the price for a broad delegation of authority" (White, dissenting in [[INS v. Chadha]]).
   - Congress cannot give the President a power to cancel enacted provisions, even by a statute he signed ([[Clinton v. City of New York]]).
   - Congress cannot appoint officers, even officers who police its own members' elections ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
6. **Where the text is silent or the power concurrent, where does the act fall in Jackson's scheme?**
   - Presidential power "fluctuate[s], depending upon [its] disjunction or conjunction with those of Congress" ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]).
   - Maximum with authorization; "zone of twilight" in silence; "lowest ebb" against Congress's will, where only a "conclusive and preclusive" power survives.
   - Full analysis in [[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]].
7. **Does practice settle the allocation?**
   - Frankfurter's test: practice glosses "executive Power" only if "systematic, unbroken," "long pursued to the knowledge of the Congress and never before questioned" ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]).
   - Widespread practice does not save Congress's inventions: [[INS v. Chadha]] invalidated the legislative veto despite 295 veto-type provisions in 196 statutes.
   - [[Trump v. Slaughter]] rejects any "practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power": "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere."
   - Early practice can still "liquidate[] & settle[]" meaning, which is how [[Trump v. Slaughter]] uses [[The Decision of 1789]].
8. **Which branch gains, and at whose expense?**
   - Checks and balances are "a self-executing safeguard against the encroachment or aggrandizement of one branch at the expense of the other" ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
   - Ask whether the acting branch took another branch's power or defended its own.

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption:** "When the Executive acts, he presumptively acts in an executive or administrative capacity"; when one House acts, it acts in its legislative sphere ([[INS v. Chadha]]).
- **Standard:** No tiers of scrutiny. Once the act is classified as belonging to another branch, the inquiry ends: "policy arguments supporting even useful 'political inventions' are subject to the demands of the Constitution," and "the Framers ranked other values higher than efficiency" ([[INS v. Chadha]]).
- **Burden:** A President acting against Congress's expressed will must show a "conclusive and preclusive" power ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]).
- **Burden:** Congress cannot use the Necessary and Proper Clause to do what another clause "by clear implication prohibits" ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** The structural claim itself is justiciable: [[INS v. Chadha]] rejected the political-question objection, and a House suing as a House does not make the case "a non-justiciable 'political' dispute" ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** The decision not to prosecute "cannot be coerced or reviewed by the courts" ([[United States v. Cox]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Courts answer only inside a case. The Justices refused to advise Washington, in part because the same questions might return to them "as judges of a court in the last resort" ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Who sues decides whether a court hears the claim. A State or taxpayer asserting usurped powers raises "abstract questions of political power" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]); the House as an institution may sue over spending with no appropriation ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]). See [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- **What fills the gap:** Congress checks the President with the purse (it can end a war by cutting funds), refusal to confirm or ratify, and impeachment ([[An Introduction to the Separation of Powers]]).
- **What fills the gap:** The Senate's two-thirds requirement is what separates impeachment from a no-confidence vote ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]).
- **What fills the gap:** Congress checks the courts by altering their size, jurisdiction or budget, by proposing amendments that overturn decisions (XI, XIV, XVI, XXVI), and by impeaching judges.
- **What fills the gap:** The President checks Congress with the veto, enforcement priorities and foreign affairs, and checks the courts through appointments and control over which issues reach court.
- **What fills the gap:** The courts' one check is holding the other branches' acts unlawful, "a check that constrains every action those branches can take." The judiciary has "neither Force nor Will, but merely judgment," and depends on "the executive arm" to make its judgments effective ([[The Federalist No. 78]]).

## Exceptions

- **The four express one-House powers.** House impeachment, Senate trial, Senate confirmation and Senate treaty ratification operate without bicameralism or presentment ([[INS v. Chadha]]).
- **Each House's internal governance.** Under Art. I, § 5 each House judges its members' elections, sets its own rules, and punishes or expels members ([[A Map of Article I]]); each may appoint its own officers "to assist in the legislative processes" without offending the Appointments Clause ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
- **Bodies exercising no executive power.** A commission that only investigates and reports to Congress may be staffed by Congress ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]). What survives of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]] after [[Trump v. Slaughter]] is only the "observation" that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the removal rule.
- **The Federal Reserve.** Reserved in Part III-B of [[Trump v. Slaughter]] "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States." [[Trump v. Cook]] then called the Governors' for-cause protection "consistent with the Constitution," but on a stay application, after the Government conceded the point, with statutory process as the ground of decision; the Article II holding may be dictum.
- **Non-Article III judges and purely adjudicatory bodies.** Expressly reserved in [[Trump v. Slaughter]]; the one case applying Humphrey's, *Wiener v. United States*, involved "a solely adjudicatory body."
- **Unreviewable enforcement discretion.** A U.S. Attorney's refusal to sign an indictment cannot be compelled by a court ([[United States v. Cox]]); see [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]].
- **Not decided: a short, self-terminating emergency seizure.** Frankfurter reserved a seizure that was "short, explicitly temporary" and ended automatically unless Congress approved ([[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]).
- **Not decided: whether Congress may deport or otherwise burden named individuals by statute.** [[INS v. Chadha]] says the result "could have been achieved, if at all, only by legislation." The "if at all" points to [[United States v. Lovett]], Powell's adjudication rationale, and [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]].
- **Not decided: how far the Executive can bind itself.** [[United States v. Nixon]] held the Executive bound by its Special Prosecutor regulation "[s]o long as this regulation remains in force," which sits uneasily with the unitary-executive premise of [[Trump v. Slaughter]]; see [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]].

## Leading case

[[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer]]

- **Holding:** 6–3, Black for the Court. The President's power "must stem either from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself," and seizing steel mills to avert a strike is "a job for the Nation's lawmakers, not for its military authorities."
- **Driving fact:** Congress had considered emergency seizure in the 1947 Taft-Hartley debates and voted it down. Frankfurter reads that as clear as if Congress had said "You may not seize"; a refusal, not mere silence, puts the case at the lowest ebb.
- **Reasoning (Black):** The order "directs that a presidential policy be executed in a manner prescribed by the President," which is lawmaking. The Take Care Clause "refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker"; the President's part in lawmaking is recommending and vetoing.
- **Reasoning (Jackson):** Jackson's concurrence became the law because it alone supplies a framework for other cases. The Vesting Clause allocates the powers "thereafter stated" and is not "a grant in bulk of all conceivable executive power"; otherwise the Framers would not have listed "several specific items, including some trifling ones."
- **Reasoning (Jackson, emergencies):** The Framers "knew what emergencies were" and wrote one emergency provision, the Suspension Clause, in Article I. Emergency powers "would tend to kindle emergencies"; the casebook's note adds that Weimar's emergency clause "helped bring Adolf Hitler to power."
- **Separate opinions:** Frankfurter supplies the historical-gloss test and finds three seizures in 1941 insufficient. Douglas argues that a seizure is a taking, only Congress can pay, so only Congress can authorize it. Clark relies on *Little v. Barreme*: where Congress has prescribed a procedure, the President must follow it.
- **Dissent:** Vinson, with Reed and Minton, would uphold the seizure as holding the status quo until Congress could act, and rejects the "messenger-boy concept of the Office."
- **Quote:** "With all its defects, delays and inconveniences, men have discovered no technique for long preserving free government except that the Executive be under the law, and that the law be made by parliamentary deliberations."

## Best counter-case

[[Trump v. Slaughter]]

- **Distinguishing line:** If the power at stake belongs to the President alone under Article II, a contrary statute is void whatever Congress has said (Slaughter); if Congress also holds power over the subject, the President's authority depends on what Congress has said (Youngstown).
- **Holding:** 6–3, Roberts for the Court. The FTC Act's for-cause removal provision "is contrary to the separation of powers," because vesting the executive power in one President "was to establish a hierarchy"; anything left of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]] beyond the no-executive-power "observation" is overruled.
- **Driving fact:** The kind of power, not the agency's structure. The FTC makes binding rules, adjudicates in-house and sues for penalties, so it exercises executive power; the number of commissioners is irrelevant.
- **Argue for Slaughter:** Article II has no "herein granted"; [[The Decision of 1789]] liquidated the removal power with Madison's view that the legislature "has no right to diminish or modify" it; a default rule and an indefeasible rule are therefore "one and the same."
- **Argue for Slaughter:** Independence from the President is not independence from politics; it yields "increased subservience to congressional direction."
- **Argue for Congress:** The separation was adopted "not to promote efficiency but to preclude the exercise of arbitrary power" (Brandeis, quoted by Frankfurter in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer|Youngstown]]), and Congress creates offices by law and may set their terms.
- **Argue for Congress:** Sotomayor, dissenting: there is "no evidence that those who shaped or ratified the Constitution adopted the . . . general rule of at-will removal," and Federalist Nos. 39 and 77 contemplated legislative regulation of tenure.
- **Companion case:** [[Trump v. Cook]], decided the same day, protects Federal Reserve Governors on the history of the First and Second Banks. The same Court lets Congress condition removal in one agency and not another depending on which early practice it credits; details in [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].
- **Name the method:** Slaughter is formalism with one box; Humphrey's was formalist in method with a functionalist fourth box; [[United States v. Nixon]] is formalist on who decides and functional on the privilege; [[INS v. Chadha]] and [[Clinton v. City of New York]] refuse to balance, while White in Chadha and Breyer in Clinton would balance.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Youngstown held that the President was at his lowest ebb."** Black wrote for the Court and never used the categories; the three categories are Jackson's concurrence. Name the opinion you are applying.
- **"Both branches agreed, so the arrangement is valid."** [[INS v. Chadha]] and [[Clinton v. City of New York]] struck schemes the President and Congress had accepted. Jackson's category one enlarges the President's executive discretion; it cannot give him a share in the § 7 lawmaking procedure, which is why Breyer's reliance on category one failed in Clinton.
- **"Clinton forbids the President to decline to spend."** [[Clinton v. City of New York]] held only that a statutory power to "cancel" enacted provisions, depriving them of "legal force or effect," violates the Presentment Clause, and did not reach delegation. Scalia's "not a dime's worth of difference" between cancellation and discretionary spending shows the rule turns on drafting.
- **"Buckley bars Congress from creating independent offices."** Congress's power to create offices and define their powers "is broad indeed"; [[Buckley v. Valeo]] holds only that Congress may not fill them itself.
- **"Humphrey's Executor protects independent agencies."** [[Trump v. Slaughter]] overruled everything but the no-executive-power observation. The syllabus's label "v. FEC" is wrong: [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]] concerned the FTC, and the FEC case is [[Buckley v. Valeo]].
- **"Trump v. Cook holds that Cook cannot be removed because independence is good policy."** The Article II ground is historical (the First and Second Banks), and the ground of decision was statutory process: Cook received no notice or chance to respond, so the removal was "erroneous and void." Whether she may be removed for cause remains open ([[Trump v. Cook]]).
