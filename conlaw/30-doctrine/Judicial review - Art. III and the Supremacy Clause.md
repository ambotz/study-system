## Rule statement

A federal court deciding a case must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute and refuse to give the statute effect. [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]: "a law repugnant to the constitution is void; and . . . courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument." The power is an incident of deciding a case — "little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]) — resting on the Arising Under Clause, the judicial oath, and the Supremacy Clause's "in Pursuance thereof" ([[Article VI - other materials]]). The contest is over reach, not existence: [[The Federalist No. 78]] and Marbury defend review as ordinary choice of law, while [[Brutus No. 11]] predicted, and [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] later claimed, that the Court's exposition binds everyone ([[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]]).

## Elements

1. **Is there a case — an actual dispute between adverse parties in which something turns on the decision?**
   - The Justices refused twenty-nine legal questions from Washington because no lawsuit had brought them ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]).
   - Tocqueville's three characteristics state the same requirement: rights "must be contested," the judge decides a particular point rather than a general principle, and the judicial power "is by its nature devoid of action" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).
2. **Is the party invoking review directly injured?**
   - The party must have "sustained, or is immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury"; "a general interest common to all members of the public" is not enough ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]).
   - The modern three-part test is in [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
3. **Does the court have jurisdiction from a valid source?**
   - Congress cannot enlarge the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond the Art. III, § 2, cl. 2 list, because "[a]ffirmative words are often, in their operation, negative of other objects than those affirmed" ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]).
   - Jurisdiction is also the lever Congress controls: see [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].
4. **Does the case require the court to resolve a genuine conflict between the statute and the Constitution?**
   - "Those who apply the rule to particular cases, must of necessity expound and interpret that rule. If two laws conflict with each other, the courts must decide on the operation of each" ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]).
   - Where the statute admits a reading that avoids the conflict, that reading disposes of the case and the constitutional question never arises — the standard criticism of Marshall's reading of § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789.
5. **Is the remedy confined to the parties?**
   - The court gives judgment on the rights before it; the statute stays on the books, "censured" but "not abolished," and loses force only as similar suits multiply ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).
   - An injunction runs against "the acts of the official, the statute notwithstanding" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
   - Whether officials who were not parties must follow the court's reasoning routes to [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Standard of review / burden

- **Burden:** The party invoking the judicial power must show both invalidity and a direct injury from enforcement, "and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
- **Standard:** The founding-era materials articulate no tier of scrutiny. Hamilton would have courts void only acts "contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution," the root of later deference to Congress ([[The Federalist No. 78]]).
- **Deference on means:** [[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]] will not "inquire into the degree of its necessity," but will strike a law passed "under the pretext of executing its powers."
- **Deference to Congress's judgment:** Where Congress has deliberately resolved a doubtful question, Chase would give weight to "[t]he deliberate decision of the National Legislature" ([[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]]).
- **Practice:** Long practice "commencing with the organization of the judicial system" can settle a construction outright, so that "the question is at rest" ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Acts committed to executive discretion are "only politically examinable," and questions "in their nature political" can "never be made in this court" ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]). Marbury's line between discretionary acts and ministerial duties owed to individuals is the seed of the political-question doctrine ([[A Map of Article III]]).
- **What fills the gap:** Where no plaintiff can show injury, only the political branches' own constitutional judgment, exercised under the Art. VI oath ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]).

## Exceptions

- **No advisory opinions:** However purely legal the question and however much the executive wants the answer. The Justices' three grounds were separation of powers, their position as "judges of a court in the last resort," and the Opinion Clause read expressio unius ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]).
- **Generalized grievances:** Unreviewable, which leaves some clauses judicially unenforceable. The Ineligibility Clause challenge in [[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]] failed for want of a plaintiff; Tocqueville conceded that some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit."
- **Federal taxpayers and abstract state claims:** Neither federal taxpayer status nor a State's interest in the abstract scope of federal power will do, and a State may not sue the United States as parens patriae ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
- **Practice and acquiescence:** Settled practice can foreclose the inquiry: "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled" ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]).
- **Congressional control of jurisdiction:** A structural limit on review, not an exception to its logic. The Exceptions Clause reaches appellate jurisdiction, as [[Ex parte McCardle (1869)|Ex parte McCardle]] "seems to" accept, and Art. III leaves the existence of lower courts to Congress ([[A Map of Article III]]).
- **Not decided:** Whether Congress may strip every federal court of jurisdiction over a class of federal constitutional claims (the "not both" position).
- **Not decided:** Whether Congress may abolish a court in order to remove its judges. [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] sustained the Repeal Act without addressing tenure, and no displaced judge was before the Court.
- **Not decided by Marbury:** Whether the Court's interpretation binds officials who were not parties. [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]] says yes in a passage it concedes was unnecessary; [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] describe review as a power over judgments. Marshall's own later claim that "by this tribunal alone can the decision be made" comes from a federal-state dispute ([[McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)|McCulloch v. Maryland]]).

## Leading case

[[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]

- **Holding:** Congress cannot add to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction; § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, read to do so, is "not . . . warranted by the constitution"; and a court must decline to apply an unconstitutional statute (unanimous; Marshall, C.J.).
- **Driving fact:** The forum. Marbury wins on right (the appointment was complete at signature, and withholding the commission was "violative of a vested legal right") and on remedy, then loses because he sought mandamus in the Court's original jurisdiction.
- **Reasoning:** Three lines of argument. Structure: written limits are pointless unless superior ("[b]etween these alternatives there is no middle ground"). Judicial duty: choosing between conflicting laws in a case "is of the very essence of judicial duty." Text: the Arising Under Clause, the treason rule "addressed especially to the courts," the judicial oath, and the Supremacy Clause's "in pursuance" language.
- **Self-denying exercise:** The only statute struck down was a grant of power to the Court itself, so no other branch had an order to defy.
- **Quote:** "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
- **Quote for the departmentalist side:** "courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument."
- **Not the first exercise of review:** [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]] (1796) upheld the carriage tax on the merits with no one questioning the power, and the Justices had already refused to advise Washington; Marbury is "the most famous exposition" of review, not its invention ([[A Map of Article III]]).
- **Modern status:** [[United States v. Nixon (1974)|United States v. Nixon]] quotes the "province and duty" line to hold that courts, not the President, decide a claim of executive privilege, reciting that the Court has "unequivocally reaffirmed" Marbury.

## Best counter-case

[[Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)|Dred Scott v. Sandford]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Review in Marbury's mould refuses a power the Court was offered in a case it could not otherwise decide; review in Dred Scott's mould reaches past a finding of no jurisdiction to void another branch's settlement of a national political question.
- **What happened:** Having held that Scott could not be a "citizen" and the Circuit Court lacked jurisdiction, Taney went on to void the Missouri Compromise under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause (7–2). Congress had already repealed the Compromise in 1854, so the ruling operated as a declaration about Congress's future power.
- **Merits:** The citizenship and territorial holdings are in [[Territories and citizenship - Art. IV § 3 and Dred Scott]].
- **The Article III objection (Curtis, J., dissenting):** Nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" into a judgment of dismissal, and "[a] great question of constitutional law . . . is not, in my opinion, a fit subject to be thus reached."
- **Argue for the Court:** The territorial ruling is itself jurisdictional — if the Compromise did not free Scott, he was a slave and could not sue — so the merits are an alternative ground for the same judgment. Marbury, too, decided right and remedy before holding it lacked jurisdiction, and [[Texas v. White (1869)|Texas v. White]] shows a merits question (secession) that had to be decided to decide original jurisdiction.
- **Argue against the Court:** A court without jurisdiction can only say so and dismiss. Taney's only reason for going further is that an uncorrected error "might be drawn into precedent," which would justify deciding anything.
- **The correction came by Article V:** The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments reversed Dred Scott, as the Sixteenth later reversed *Pollock*'s income-tax holding, which had departed from [[Hylton v. United States (1796)|Hylton v. United States]]. Taney himself said "there is a mode prescribed in the instrument itself by which it may be amended."
- **Companion counter-case:** [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]], one week after Marbury, sustained the Repeal Act on "practice and acquiescence" without naming the tenure question — [[The Federalist No. 78]]'s weakest branch declining to defend itself. Read together, the Court asserted review in principle and avoided every order the political branches could resist.
- **Theory of both objections:** [[Brutus No. 11]] — judges "will not confine themselves to any fixed or established rules, but will determine, according to what appears to them, the reason and spirit of the constitution," and "there is no power provided in the constitution, that can correct their errors."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Marbury established judicial supremacy."** Marbury holds that a court must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute in the case before it. The line "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" is [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]]'s (1958), in a passage Cooper conceded was unnecessary, and Cooper's supporting authorities speak of judgments, not opinions.
- **"The Court struck down (annulled) the statute."** On the readings' own account the power is "the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon (1923)|Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]), and the law is "censured" but "not abolished" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]). The vocabulary matters whenever the question is who else is bound.
- **"Marbury had to reach the constitutional question."** Section 13's mandamus language sits in a sentence about appellate jurisdiction, so it can be read to supply a remedy only where jurisdiction already exists. On that reading Marbury loses on the statute and there is no conflict with Article III.
- **"A clear constitutional violation must be judicially remediable."** The Ineligibility Clause is as bright a rule as the document contains, and Justice Black sat thirty-four years because no one could show a direct injury ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]).
- **"The judicial oath proves judicial review."** The Art. VI oath binds legislators and executive officers too, so the oath argument proves that every officer must follow his own reading — the departmentalist's premise, not the supremacist's ([[Article VI - other materials]]).
- **"The Supremacy Clause is the best text for review of federal statutes."** The Clause names state judges, so it is the strongest text for review of state law and for binding state courts. Against a federal statute the work is done by "in Pursuance thereof," and [[Cooper v. Aaron (1958)|Cooper v. Aaron]], a federal-state case, does not automatically settle a clash between coordinate federal branches.
