## Rule statement

A federal court deciding a case must treat the Constitution as "a superior, paramount law," and where a statute and the Constitution both apply and conflict, the court applies the Constitution and refuses to give the statute effect: "a law repugnant to the constitution is void; and . . . courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]). The power is an incident of deciding a case, not a free-standing authority to annul legislation; the Court itself describes it as "little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]). Its textual footholds are the Arising Under Clause, the clauses addressed to courts, the judicial oath, and the Supremacy Clause's grant of supreme rank only to laws "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution ([[Article VI - other materials]]). The contest is over reach rather than existence: [[The Federalist No. 78]] and [[Marbury v. Madison]] defend review as ordinary choice of law between a superior and a subordinate authority, while [[Brutus No. 11]] and, a century and a half later, [[Cooper v. Aaron]] treat the Court's exposition as final for everyone, which is the separate question worked out in [[Judicial supremacy and departmentalism - Art. VI oath]].

## Elements

1. **A case.** There must be an actual dispute between adverse parties in which something turns on the decision; the Justices refused twenty-nine legal questions from Washington because no lawsuit had brought them ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]). Tocqueville's three characteristics state the same requirement: rights "must be contested," the judge decides a particular point rather than a general principle, and the judicial power "is by its nature devoid of action" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]).
2. **A plaintiff who is directly injured.** The party must "have sustained, or [be] immediately in danger of sustaining, a direct injury"; "a general interest common to all members of the public" is not enough ([[Ex parte Levitt]]). Developed in [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
3. **Jurisdiction over the case, from a valid source.** Congress cannot enlarge the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond the cases listed in Art. III, § 2, cl. 2, because "[a]ffirmative words are often, in their operation, negative of other objects than those affirmed" and no clause is presumed "to be without effect" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]). Jurisdiction is the first question, and it is what Congress can adjust: see [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].
4. **A genuine conflict between the statute and the Constitution that the case requires the court to resolve.** "Those who apply the rule to particular cases, must of necessity expound and interpret that rule. If two laws conflict with each other, the courts must decide on the operation of each" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]). Where the statute admits a reading that avoids the conflict, that reading disposes of the case and the constitutional question never arises — the criticism of Marshall's construction of § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789.
5. **The Constitution prevails, and the remedy runs to the parties.** The court gives judgment on the rights before it; the statute stays on the books, "censured" but "not abolished," and loses force only as similar suits multiply ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]). An injunction runs against "the acts of the official, the statute notwithstanding" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).

## Standard of review / burden

The party invoking the judicial power bears the burden of showing both invalidity and a direct injury from enforcement, "and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]). On the merits there is no articulated tier of scrutiny in the founding-era materials: Hamilton would have courts declare void only acts "contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution," which is the root of later deference to Congress ([[The Federalist No. 78]]), and [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] makes the corresponding point about means — a court will not "inquire into the degree of its necessity," though it will strike a law passed "under the pretext of executing its powers." Where Congress has deliberately resolved a doubtful question, that "deliberate decision of the National Legislature" carries weight ([[Hylton v. United States]]), and long practice "commencing with the organization of the judicial system" can settle the construction outright, so that "the question is at rest" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]). Where the act is committed by the Constitution to executive discretion, there is no standard because the question is not judicial: such acts are "only politically examinable," and questions "in their nature political" can "never be made in this court" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]) — the political-question route, whose seed is Marbury's line between discretionary acts and ministerial duties on which individual rights depend ([[A Map of Article III]]). Where no plaintiff can show injury, nothing fills the gap but the political branches' own constitutional judgment, exercised under the Art. VI oath ([[Ex parte Levitt]]).

## Exceptions

- No advisory opinions, however purely legal the question and however much the executive wants the answer; the Justices' three grounds were separation of powers, their position as "judges of a court in the last resort," and the Opinion Clause's expressio unius ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]).
- Generalized grievances are unreviewable, which leaves some clauses judicially unenforceable altogether: the Ineligibility Clause challenge in [[Ex parte Levitt]] failed for want of a plaintiff, and Tocqueville conceded that some laws "can never give rise to that exact species of contestation which is termed a lawsuit."
- Federal taxpayer status and a State's interest in the abstract scope of federal power will not do; a State may not sue the United States as parens patriae for its citizens ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
- Practice and acquiescence can foreclose the inquiry: "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]).
- Congress's control of jurisdiction is a structural limit on review, not an exception to its logic — the Exceptions Clause reaches appellate jurisdiction, as [[Ex parte McCardle]] appears to accept, and Art. III leaves the existence of lower courts to Congress ([[A Map of Article III]]).
- Not decided: whether Congress may strip all federal courts of jurisdiction over a class of federal constitutional claims (the "not both" position), and whether Congress may abolish a court in order to remove its judges — [[Stuart v. Laird]] sustained the Repeal Act by silence, with no displaced judge before the Court.
- Not decided by Marbury: whether the Court's interpretation binds officials who were not parties. [[Cooper v. Aaron]] says yes in dicta the Court admits was unnecessary; [[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]] and [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] describe review as a power over judgments only.

## Leading case

[[Marbury v. Madison]]

- Holds that Congress cannot add to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction, that § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 so read is "not . . . warranted by the constitution," and that a court must therefore decline to apply it.
- The driving fact is the forum: Marbury wins on right and on remedy — the appointment was complete at signature, and withholding the commission was "violative of a vested legal right" — and loses because he sought mandamus in the Court's original jurisdiction.
- Four arguments, structural first: written limits are pointless unless superior ("[b]etween these alternatives there is no middle ground"); interpreting law in a case is "of the very essence of judicial duty"; then the text — the Arising Under Clause, the treason rule "addressed especially to the courts," the judicial oath, and the Supremacy Clause's "in pursuance" language.
- The self-denying exercise is the point of the case: the only statute struck down was a grant of power to the Court itself, so no other branch had an order to defy.
- Worth quoting: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."
- Worth quoting for the departmentalist side: "courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument."
- Not the first exercise of review. [[Hylton v. United States]] (1796) upheld the carriage tax on the merits with no one questioning the power, and the Justices had already refused to advise Washington ([[The Correspondence of the Justices]]); Marbury is "the most famous exposition" of review, not its invention ([[A Map of Article III]]).

## Best counter-case

[[Dred Scott v. Sandford]]

- The case Brutus predicted: a Court that, having held it lacked jurisdiction because Scott could not be a "citizen," went on to void the Missouri Compromise on the most divisive question in the country, using the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause to bar a whole class of legislation.
- The distinguishing fact from [[Marbury v. Madison]] is what the exercise of review cost someone else: Marshall struck a grant of power to his own Court, while Taney struck a thirty-seven-year-old sectional settlement and reached the question after finding no jurisdiction. Curtis's dissent is the argument from Article III itself: nothing "after a plea to the merits can enter" into a judgment of dismissal, and "[a] great question of constitutional law . . . is not, in my opinion, a fit subject to be thus reached."
- Arguing for the Court: the territorial ruling is itself jurisdictional, because if the Compromise did not free Scott he was a slave and could not sue, so the merits are an alternative ground for the same judgment — and alternative grounds are holdings. Marbury, too, decided right and remedy before holding it had no jurisdiction.
- Arguing against: a court without jurisdiction can only say so and dismiss, and the only reason Taney gives for going further is that an uncorrected error "might be drawn into precedent," which would justify deciding anything.
- The structural answer the case provoked is Article V, not judicial correction: the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments reversed the holding, as the Sixteenth later reversed the direct-tax classification descended from [[Hylton v. United States]]. Taney himself had said "there is a mode prescribed in the instrument itself by which it may be amended."
- The companion counter-case is [[Stuart v. Laird]], decided one week after Marbury: the Court sustained the Repeal Act on "practice and acquiescence" without naming the tenure question, which is [[The Federalist No. 78]]'s weakest branch declining to defend itself. Read together, the Court asserted review in principle and avoided every order the political branches could resist.
- [[Brutus No. 11]] supplies the theory of both objections — judges "will not confine themselves to any fixed or established rules, but will determine, according to what appears to them, the reason and spirit of the constitution," and "there is no power provided in the constitution, that can correct their errors."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Treating [[Marbury v. Madison]] as the source of judicial supremacy. Marbury holds that a court must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute in the case before it; [[Cooper v. Aaron]] added, in dicta it conceded was unnecessary, that "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution." Cooper's own supporting authorities speak of judgments, not opinions.
- Saying a court "strikes down" or "annuls" a statute. On the readings' own account the power is "the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]); the law is "censured" but "not abolished" ([[Tocqueville on the American Judiciary]]). The vocabulary matters whenever the question is who else is bound.
- Reaching the constitutional question when a statutory reading disposes of the case. Section 13's mandamus language sits in a sentence about appellate jurisdiction, so it can be read to supply a remedy where jurisdiction already exists; on that reading Marbury simply loses and there is no conflict with Article III.
- Assuming a clear constitutional violation must be judicially remediable. The Ineligibility Clause is as bright a rule as the document contains, and Justice Black sat thirty-four years because no one could show a direct injury ([[Ex parte Levitt]]).
- Citing the judicial oath as a knock-down argument for review. The Art. VI oath binds legislators and executive officers too, so it proves that every branch must follow its own reading — which is the departmentalist's premise, not the supremacist's ([[Article VI - other materials]]).
- Forgetting that the Supremacy Clause names state judges. It is the strongest text for review of state law and for binding state courts; against a federal statute the work is done by "in Pursuance thereof," and [[Cooper v. Aaron]] is a federal-state case whose principle does not automatically settle a clash between coordinate federal branches.
