## Rule statement

Every officer of the United States must be appointed by the President with the Senate's consent or, for an inferior officer, by the President alone, a court of law or a department head if Congress so provides; Congress may create an office but never fill it. An officer is "any appointee exercising significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States" ([[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]). The Necessary and Proper Clause cannot authorize what the Appointments Clause "by clear implication prohibits." The design splits the royal prerogative over offices: Congress creates them, the President nominates, and the Senate consents ([[Introduction to Article II]]; [[The Federalist No. 76]]). The contest is over the officer line: Buckley's test is narrower than the founding-era meaning Jennifer Mascott recovers, "any government official with responsibility for an ongoing governmental duty."

## Elements

1. **Does the position carry significant authority under federal law, making its holder an officer rather than an employee?**
   - The test has three parts: an appointee in a continuing position; significant, not ministerial, authority; exercised under federal law rather than under a House's internal rules.
   - Binding the public makes an officer: rulemaking, civil enforcement, and "primary responsibility for conducting civil litigation in the courts of the United States for vindicating public rights."
   - A body that only investigates, holds hearings and reports to Congress exercises powers Congress could use through its own committees; its members are not officers, and Congress may appoint them.
   - The statutory label does not control: the FEC was placed in the legislative branch by statute, and the Court looked to the authority exercised "pursuant to the laws."
   - An employee falls outside the Clause entirely.
2. **If an officer, is the officer principal or inferior?**
   - The classification decides only whether Senate consent may be dispensed with.
   - The classification never adds Congress to the list of appointers.
   - The assigned readings state no test for inferior status; [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] upheld a court's appointment of an independent counsel as an inferior officer.
3. **For a principal officer, did the President nominate and the Senate alone consent?**
   - Confirmation by both Houses is an independent defect, because the Clause names only the Senate.
   - When the Senate is in recess, Art. II § 2 cl. 3 is the only alternative to consent (*NLRB v. Noel Canning*).
4. **For an inferior officer, did Congress by law vest the appointment in a listed authority?**
   - The list is "the President alone, . . . the Courts of Law, or . . . the Heads of Departments."
   - The Speaker and the President pro tempore are neither courts of law nor heads of departments.
   - Outside the Clause, each House appoints only its own officers "to assist in the legislative processes" (Art. I § 2 cl. 5; § 3 cl. 5).
5. **Was the appointment complete?**
   - An appointment is complete when the President signs the commission; the seal is "conclusive testimony" of the signature and "of the completion of the appointment" ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]).
   - Transmission "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law."
   - Completion matters only for an office not held at the President's pleasure; Marbury's five-year term is what made his right vested.
6. **Are the challenged duties a new office or a germane addition to an existing one?**
   - Justices sat as circuit judges without separate commissions, because "practice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer" ([[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]).
   - Later cases hold that germane additional duties need no second appointment (*Shoemaker v. United States*, 1893; *Weiss v. United States*, 1994).

## Standard of review / burden

- **Standard:** Formal and categorical, not balanced. The Clause names who may appoint, Congress is not on the list, and [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]] was unanimous on the point although the Court split on everything else.
- **Standard (meaning of "officer"):** "Officers of the United States" is "a term intended to have substantive meaning," not "etiquette or protocol"; the drafters "had a less frivolous purpose in mind."
- **Standard (no functional accommodation):** White, the Court's leading functionalist, accepted the administrative state's accommodations on delegation, adjudication and removal but refused one here: the Appointments Clause had never been made to "yield to permit congressional appointments of members of a major agency."
- **Burden:** The challenger must be the right plaintiff. Structural appointment defects are enforced by parties the officer regulates, investigates or sues; a citizen's "general interest common to all members of the public" fails at the threshold ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]; see [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]]).
- **What fills the gap:** Founding-era practice, treated as evidence of original meaning rather than mere tradition. [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] calls it "a contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature": "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled."

## Exceptions

- **Employees:** A person without significant authority under federal law need not be appointed under the Clause, which is why a purely informational commission could be staffed by congressional appointment.
- **Inferior officers:** Congress may by law dispense with Senate consent and vest appointment in the President alone, a court of law or a department head — the route that makes a court-appointed prosecutor possible ([[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]]).
- **Each House's own officers:** Article I lets the House choose its Speaker and other officers and the Senate its President pro tempore and other officers, to assist in the legislative process.
- **Recess appointments:** Art. II § 2 cl. 3 is the alternative to Senate consent when the Senate is not sitting. It matters because Hamilton's prediction in [[The Federalist No. 76]] that the Senate would rarely refuse a nominee has not held.
- **Germane additional duties:** Congress may assign an existing officer duties germane to the office without a new appointment, the doctrinal footing later supplied for the result in [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]].
- **No revocation after signature:** "Some point of time must be taken when the power of the executive over an officer, not removable at his will, must cease," so a completed appointment to a fixed-term office cannot be undone by withholding the paper ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]). Had the office been held at pleasure, "the circumstance which completes his appointment is of no concern."
- **Not decided: what "significant" means.** The Court has said little beyond the sentence itself. A 2007 OLC opinion requires a continuing position with "a portion of the sovereign powers of the federal government"; Mascott's broader reading would make thousands of current employees officers; *Lucia v. SEC* (2018) held SEC administrative law judges to be officers.
- **Not decided: whether Congress may abolish a court to remove its judges.** No displaced 1801 circuit judge was a party in [[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]], and the opinion never mentions good-behaviour tenure or the Compensation Clause; see [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].
- **Separate question: removal.** Congress may not keep a power to remove an officer who executes the laws (*Bowsher v. Synar*), but whether it may limit the President's own removal power is a different question. An independent agency's members are still presidential appointees; see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

## Leading case

[[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]]

- **Holding:** The FEC's voting members were officers who had to be appointed under Art. II § 2 cl. 2, and the provisions giving the Commission "primary responsibility for conducting civil litigation" were invalid, because "[s]uch functions may be discharged only by persons who are 'Officers of the United States.'"
- **Driving fact:** The President pro tempore and the Speaker chose four of the six voting commissioners, and even the President's two nominees required confirmation by both Houses.
- **Reasoning (history):** The Committee of Detail's draft gave the Senate the appointment of ambassadors and Supreme Court judges; the Committee of Eleven substituted presidential nomination with Senate consent — "a deliberate change made by the Framers with the intent to deny Congress any authority itself to appoint those who were 'Officers of the United States.'"
- **Reasoning (structure):** Congress could not use the Necessary and Proper Clause to pass a bill of attainder in the teeth of Art. I § 9, and "[n]o more may it vest in itself, or in its officers, the authority to appoint officers of the United States"; see [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
- **Rejected argument:** A commission policing federal elections will investigate the incumbent President's own campaign, so presidential appointment hands one candidate his referee. The structural rule did not bend; Congress's 1976 answer was presidential appointment of all six commissioners with Senate consent.
- **Remedy:** The Court let the Commission keep its investigative and informational functions, treated its past acts as valid, and stayed its judgment so Congress could reconstitute the agency.
- **Theory:** [[The Federalist No. 76]] — "[t]he sole and undivided responsibility of one man will naturally beget a livelier sense of duty," while an appointing assembly produces the bargain "Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that."
- **Quote:** "[T]here is no provision of the Constitution remotely providing any alternative means for the selection of the members of the Commission or for anybody like them."

## Best counter-case

[[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]]

- **Distinguishing line:** Unbroken practice beginning at the founding can settle that an existing officer may take on added duties without a new appointment; a novel scheme with no founding-era pedigree gets the Clause's text applied strictly.
- **Holding:** Supreme Court Justices could sit as circuit judges with no circuit commission; "the question is at rest, and ought not now to be disturbed."
- **Driving fact:** The objection was "of recent date," while the Justices had ridden circuit since 1789 with everyone's acquiescence. [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]] faced a scheme invented in 1974.
- **Argue for Buckley's side:** A circuit judgeship is a distinct office to which no Justice was nominated or confirmed; if practice can supply a commission, the Clause is advisory. Paterson never mentions the Appointments Clause, good-behaviour tenure or the Compensation Clause.
- **Argue for Stuart's side:** Early, unbroken practice is evidence of what the text meant, not an exception to it, and the later germane-duties cases reach the same result on doctrinal grounds.
- **Argue the institutional reading:** One week after [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] claimed judicial review at no cost to the political branches, the same Court declined to use it against a statute the casebook calls of "dubious constitutionality," with impeachments of Federalist judges under way. Ask whether self-preservation is a legitimate judicial consideration when Marbury says the duty is to "say what the law is."
- **Counter on a different axis:** [[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]] enforces an appointment against the executive's wishes: the appointment was complete at signature and withholding the commission was "violative of a vested legal right," though the Court denied the remedy for want of jurisdiction.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Congress may appoint inferior officers."** It may not. The alternative list is "the President alone, . . . the Courts of Law, or . . . the Heads of Departments," and Congress and its officers appear nowhere in the Clause for any officer, principal or inferior.
- **"Congress's label decides where the agency sits."** [[Buckley v. Valeo (1976)|Buckley v. Valeo]] ignored the statute's placement of the FEC in the legislative branch and looked to the authority exercised. Be ready to say why that method is hard to square with [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)|Humphrey's Executor]], which accepted Congress's description of the FTC as performing duties "as a legislative or as a judicial aid."
- **"Appointment and removal rise and fall together."** Congress may neither fill an office nor fire its holder, but a limit on the President's own removal power is a separate question with a separate answer; see [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].
- **"The appointment was incomplete because the commission was never delivered."** Delivery "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law," and if possession of the paper were the office, "fire or theft" could destroy an office ([[Marbury v. Madison (1803)|Marbury v. Madison]]).
- **"Any citizen can challenge a defective appointment."** A citizen and member of the Supreme Court bar had no standing to challenge Justice Black's seat under the Ineligibility Clause (Art. I § 6 cl. 2) ([[Ex parte Levitt (1937)|Ex parte Levitt]]). The question was raised a few more times in Black's thirty-four years on the Court, and the Court never ruled on it.
- **"[[Stuart v. Laird (1803)|Stuart v. Laird]] is an Appointments Clause holding."** Stuart decides a commission question on practice alone, with no constitutional analysis; the germaneness rationale that justifies the result came from later cases, not from Paterson's opinion.
