## Rule statement

"[A]ny appointee exercising significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States is an 'Officer of the United States,'" and every officer must be appointed by one of the methods Art. II § 2 cl. 2 prescribes: the President nominates and, with the Senate's advice and consent, appoints, except that Congress may by law vest the appointment of "inferior Officers" in the President alone, the courts of law, or the heads of departments ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]). Congress may create an office and define its powers, but it may not fill the office itself or through its own officers, and the Necessary and Proper Clause cannot authorize what the Appointments Clause "by clear implication prohibits." The contest is over the officer line, not the appointers: [[Buckley v. Valeo]]'s "significant authority" test is narrower than the founding-era usage some originalists recover, under which an "officer" was any government official with responsibility for an ongoing governmental duty.

## Elements

1. **Is the position an office, or mere employment?** Ask whether the appointee holds a continuing position and exercises significant authority under federal law rather than under a House's internal rules; a body that only investigates, holds hearings and reports to Congress exercises powers Congress could exercise through its own committees, and its members are not officers.
2. **If an officer, is the officer principal or inferior?** The classification decides only whether Senate consent may be dispensed with; it never widens the list of appointers.
3. **Apply the default method for a principal officer.** Presidential nomination plus the advice and consent of the Senate alone; confirmation by both Houses is an independent defect, because the Clause names only the Senate.
4. **Apply the alternative method for an inferior officer, if Congress has provided one by law.** The appointment may be vested only in "the President alone, in the Courts of Law, or in the Heads of Departments." The Speaker and the President pro tempore are neither courts of law nor heads of departments.
5. **Check that Congress has not kept the appointment for itself.** Each House appoints its own officers "to assist in the legislative processes" under Article I, and nothing more.
6. **Fix the moment the appointment is complete.** The appointment is complete when the President signs the commission; the seal is "conclusive testimony" of the signature and "of the completion of the appointment," and transmission "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law" ([[Marbury v. Madison]]).
7. **Ask whether the duties are new duties or germane additions to an existing office.** Supreme Court Justices sat as circuit judges without separate commissions, and "practice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer" ([[Stuart v. Laird]]).
8. **Do not let the statutory label do the work.** The Federal Election Commission was described by statute as part of the legislative branch, and the Court looked instead to the authority its members exercised "pursuant to the laws."

## Standard of review / burden

Review is formal and categorical rather than balanced: the Clause names who may appoint, Congress is not on the list, and [[Buckley v. Valeo]] was unanimous on the point even though the Court was otherwise divided and even though its leading functionalist joined. "Officers of the United States" is "a term intended to have substantive meaning," not "etiquette or protocol," and the drafters "had a less frivolous purpose in mind"; the accommodations the administrative state has won on delegation, adjudication and removal do not extend here. Where the text is silent, practice fills the gap and does so as evidence of original meaning rather than mere tradition: [[Stuart v. Laird]] rests entirely on founding-era practice, calls the contrary objection "of recent date," and holds that "[t]his practical exposition is too strong and obstinate to be shaken or controlled." Remedially the Court is cautious — [[Buckley v. Valeo]] let the Commission keep its investigative and informational functions, treated its past acts as valid, and stayed its judgment so Congress could reconstitute the agency, which it did in 1976. The practical burden is on the challenger to be the right plaintiff: Appointments Clause limits get enforced when raised defensively by someone the officer is regulating, investigating or suing, and a citizen's generalized objection to how an office was filled fails at the threshold ([[Ex parte Levitt]]). See [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].

## Exceptions

- **Employees, as distinct from officers.** Someone without significant authority under federal law need not be appointed under the Clause, which is why a purely informational commission could be staffed by congressional appointment.
- **Inferior officers.** Congress may by law shift the appointment away from Senate confirmation to the President alone, a court of law, or a department head — the route that makes appointment of a prosecutor by a court possible.
- **Each House's own officers.** Article I lets the House choose its Speaker and other officers and the Senate its President pro tempore and other officers, to assist in the legislative process.
- **Recess appointments.** Art. II § 2 cl. 3 is the alternative to Senate consent when the Senate is not sitting, and it matters exactly because Hamilton's prediction of rare Senate refusal has not held.
- **Germane additional duties.** Congress may assign an existing officer duties germane to the office without a new appointment, which is the doctrinal footing later supplied for the result in [[Stuart v. Laird]].
- **No revocation after signature.** Because "[s]ome point of time must be taken when the power of the executive over an officer, not removable at his will, must cease," a completed appointment to a fixed-term office cannot be undone by withholding the paper ([[Marbury v. Madison]]).
- **Not decided: what "significant" means.** The Court has said little beyond the sentence itself; the executive branch reads it as a continuing position exercising "a portion of the sovereign powers of the federal government," and the broader originalist reading would make thousands of current employees officers.
- **Not decided: whether Congress may abolish a court to remove its judges.** No displaced 1801 circuit judge was a party in [[Stuart v. Laird]], and the opinion never mentions good-behaviour tenure or the Compensation Clause. See [[Congressional control of the courts - Art. III jurisdiction and court size]].
- **Separate question: removal.** Congress may not reserve a power to remove an officer who executes the laws, but it may sometimes limit the President's own removal power; an agency may be independent of the President while the President still appoints its members. See [[Removal - Art. II § 1 and the Humphrey's exception]].

## Leading case

[[Buckley v. Valeo]]

- Holds that the Federal Election Commission's voting members were officers who had to be appointed under Art. II § 2 cl. 2, and that the provisions giving the Commission "primary responsibility for conducting civil litigation in the courts of the United States for vindicating public rights" were invalid because "[s]uch functions may be discharged only by persons who are 'Officers of the United States.'"
- The fact that drove it was the appointing scheme: four of six voting commissioners were chosen by the President pro tempore and the Speaker, and even the President's two nominees required confirmation by both Houses.
- The drafting history is load-bearing. The Committee of Detail's draft gave the Senate the appointment of ambassadors and Supreme Court judges, and the Committee of Eleven replaced it with presidential nomination subject to Senate confirmation — "a deliberate change made by the Framers with the intent to deny Congress any authority itself to appoint those who were 'Officers of the United States.'"
- The Necessary and Proper argument is answered by analogy to Art. I § 9: Congress could not use that Clause to pass a bill of attainder, and "[n]o more may it vest in itself, or in its officers, the authority to appoint officers of the United States when the Appointments Clause by clear implication prohibits it." See [[Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause - Art. I § 8 cl. 18]].
- The best counter-argument on the merits, which the Court refused, is the conflict of interest: a commission that polices federal elections will investigate the incumbent President's own campaign, so presidential appointment hands one candidate his referee. The structural rule does not bend, and Congress's 1976 answer was bipartisan balance with presidential appointment.
- The sentence worth quoting: "[T]here is no provision of the Constitution remotely providing any alternative means for the selection of the members of the Commission or for anybody like them."
- The theory behind it is [[The Federalist No. 76]]: "one man of discernment is better fitted to analyze and estimate the peculiar qualities adapted to particular offices, than a body of men," because "[t]he sole and undivided responsibility of one man will naturally beget a livelier sense of duty," while an appointing assembly produces the bargain "Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that."

## Best counter-case

[[Stuart v. Laird]]

- Holds that Supreme Court Justices could sit as circuit judges with no commission as circuit judges, because "practice and acquiescence under it for a period of several years, commencing with the organization of the judicial system, affords an irresistible answer," and "the question is at rest, and ought not now to be disturbed."
- The distinguishing fact is the age of the objection against the age of the practice: the challenge was "of recent date," while the Justices had ridden circuit from 1789 with everyone's acquiescence. [[Buckley v. Valeo]] faced a scheme invented in 1974 with no founding-era pedigree at all.
- Arguing the Buckley side against Stuart: a circuit judgeship is a distinct office, a Justice was never nominated to it or confirmed for it, and if practice can supply a commission the Clause is advisory; the opinion never mentions the Appointments Clause, good-behaviour tenure or the Compensation Clause, and sustains the Repeal Act by silence.
- Arguing the Stuart side against Buckley: early, unbroken practice beginning with the organization of the government is "a contemporary interpretation of the most forcible nature," so it is evidence of what the text meant rather than an exception to it, and later doctrine reaches the same result by holding that germane additional duties need no second appointment.
- The institutional reading is the exam-rich one. One week after [[Marbury v. Madison]] claimed the power of judicial review in a case where exercising it cost the political branches nothing, the same Court declined to use it against a statute the casebook calls of "dubious constitutionality," with impeachments of Federalist judges under way. Ask whether institutional self-preservation is a legitimate judicial consideration when Marbury's own rhetoric says the duty is simply to "say what the law is."
- Keep [[Marbury v. Madison]] in reserve as the counter-case on a different axis: it enforces an appointment against the executive's wishes, holding that the appointment was complete at signature and that withholding the commission was "violative of a vested legal right," while refusing the remedy on jurisdictional grounds.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Thinking the inferior-officer alternative opens a door for Congress. It does not. The alternative list is "the President alone, . . . the Courts of Law, or . . . the Heads of Departments," and Congress and its officers appear nowhere in the Clause for any officer, principal or inferior.
- Treating Congress's statutory label as the answer. [[Buckley v. Valeo]] ignored the statute's placement of the Commission in the legislative branch and looked to the authority exercised; be ready to say why that method is hard to square with the removal cases that accepted Congress's description of an agency as a "legislative or . . . judicial aid."
- Confusing appointment with removal. Congress may neither fill the office nor fire its holder, but a limit on the President's own removal power is a different question with a different answer; an answer that runs them together misses that an independent agency's members are still presidential appointees.
- Saying the appointment was incomplete because the commission was never delivered. Delivery "is a practice directed by convenience, but not by law," and if possession of the paper were the office, "fire or theft" could destroy an office.
- Forgetting that the vested right in [[Marbury v. Madison]] depends on the five-year term. Had the justice of the peace served at pleasure, Jefferson could simply have removed him and "the circumstance which completes his appointment is of no concern."
- Reaching for the Necessary and Proper Clause. The question is never Congress's power "to create an office or a commission, which is broad indeed," but its power to say who fills it, and a structural prohibition is not a gap the Clause can fill.
- Overstating what "significant authority" settles. The Court has never said much more than the phrase; the executive branch and academic originalists have supplied competing definitions, and the line between informing Congress and binding the public is the one that actually decides cases.
- Assuming any citizen can raise an appointments defect. The structural provisions get enforced by regulated parties as a defense; a citizen and member of the Supreme Court bar had no standing to challenge a Justice's seat ([[Ex parte Levitt]]), and the defect went unlitigated for thirty-four years.
- Citing [[Stuart v. Laird]] as an Appointments Clause holding. It decides a commission question on practice alone, with no constitutional analysis, and the germaneness rationale that justifies the result was supplied by later cases, not by Paterson's opinion.
