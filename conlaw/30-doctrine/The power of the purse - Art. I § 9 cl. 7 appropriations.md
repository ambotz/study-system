## Rule statement

No federal money may be spent unless a statute appropriates it: "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." Congress may therefore fund, refuse to fund, or fund on conditions, and a condition the executive thinks unconstitutional does not free the executive to spend, because voiding a restriction creates no appropriation. Congress in turn may not use the purse to do what another clause forbids, as with the pay cutoff for three named men in [[United States v. Lovett]]. The contest is enforcement: neither a State nor a federal taxpayer may sue ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]), but an unreviewed district court opinion lets the House sue over spending with no appropriation at all ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]).

## Elements

1. **Is there an appropriation made by law?**
   - Identify the statute, and whether it is permanent (available indefinitely) or current (renewed, usually each year, which ties the executive to Congress "via purse string").
   - Both an authorization and an appropriation are required before money is spent, and an appropriation "must be expressly stated; it cannot be inferred or implied" ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]).
2. **Is the complaint spending with no appropriation, or spending outside the scope of a contested one?**
   - Spending with no appropriation is a constitutional claim under cl. 7.
   - Spending under an appropriation whose scope is disputed is a statutory claim; in Burwell the § 1324 count and the employer-mandate theory were dismissed because "Article I is not a talisman."
   - Burwell Note 2 presses the line: the Secretaries said § 1402 was paid from an existing permanent appropriation, so deciding that the claim was constitutional came close to deciding the merits.
3. **If Congress attached a condition or rider, is the condition itself forbidden by some other clause?**
   - Congress may generally refuse to fund operations it disagrees with, because no money leaves the Treasury without a law.
   - The limits come from other provisions: the Bill of Attainder Clause ([[United States v. Lovett]]; see [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]]), the Compensation Clauses protecting the President's and judges' salaries, and arguably fields of exclusive presidential power ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]], Note 1).
4. **If the President objects to a rider, which response did he choose?**
   - Veto the whole bill.
   - Sign, object and comply (Roosevelt on § 304 in [[United States v. Lovett]]; Obama on §§ 1112–1113).
   - Sign and announce that "the executive branch will construe" the provision not to bind him (Obama on § 2262).
   - Spend without an appropriation, which cl. 7 forbids.
   - The must-pass bill is why signing is the usual choice: the Senate voted 69 to 0 against § 304 and yielded because the House would pass no appropriation without it, and Obama signed H.R. 1473 "because of the importance of avoiding a lapse in appropriations."
5. **Where does the executive's position fall in the Youngstown scheme?**
   - Spending against an express funding denial is category three, where the President needs a power "conclusive and preclusive," and a power to spend unappropriated money is the least plausible candidate ([[Presidential power - Art. II and the Youngstown categories]]).
   - A claim that Congress did appropriate is a category-one argument, which is what the Secretaries made in Burwell.
6. **Who can bring the claim?**
   - The House as an institution, on a no-appropriation theory: yes (Burwell; the D.C. Circuit agreed in the border-wall case, *U.S. House of Representatives v. Mnuchin* (2020)).
   - Individual members, a State suing in its own right over a grant it declined, a State as parens patriae against the United States, and a federal taxpayer: no ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
   - A party injured by a specific payment or nonpayment can sue in the ordinary way, as the three employees did in the Court of Claims in Lovett.
7. **For war, is Congress defunding, repealing, or merely continuing to pay?**
   - A funding cutoff is self-executing: without an appropriation, money cannot be drawn ([[Terminating War]]).
   - Repeal of an authorization while appropriations continue sends mixed signals; Congress repealed the Tonkin Resolution in January 1971, and Nixon fought on while Congress kept funding the war "for several more years."
   - Continued appropriations are weak evidence of authorization, because Congress cannot easily defund troops in the field; War Powers Resolution § 8(a) forbids inferring authorization from "any provision contained in an appropriation Act" ([[Declarations of War]]; [[Nixon, Veto of the War Powers Resolution]]).

## Standard of review / burden

- **Standard:** Cl. 7 states a rule, not a standard. A court that reaches the merits asks only whether an appropriation exists, so the whole fight is over reaching the merits.
- **Burden:** A plaintiff must show "some direct injury suffered or threatened." A federal taxpayer's interest is "comparatively minute and indeterminable," and a State that accepted nothing can frustrate a conditional grant "by the simple expedient of not yielding" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
- **Burden:** Suits between the branches get an "especially rigorous" standing analysis, and the House has standing only on the constitutional no-appropriation theory ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]). See [[Standing - Art. III § 2 cases and controversies]].
- **Not reviewed / political question:** A State's complaint that Congress usurped reserved powers "by the mere enactment of the statute" is "political, and not judicial in character," because "[w]e have no power per se to review and annul acts of Congress on the ground that they are unconstitutional" (Mellon).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** The government's mirror-image argument has lost twice. [[United States v. Lovett]] refused to treat a pay cutoff as an unreviewable appropriations choice, since otherwise "congressional action, aimed at three named individuals, . . . could never be challenged in any court." [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] held the no-appropriation claims justiciable because they "involve pure questions of constitutional interpretation."
- **Standard:** Where the executive keeps funding recipients Congress disqualified, review runs through the APA: non-enforcement is reviewable when the agency "has consciously and expressly adopted a general policy which is in effect an abdication of its statutory duty," especially where it is "actively supplying segregated institutions with federal funds, contrary to the expressed purposes of Congress" ([[Adams v. Richardson]]). [[Heckler v. Chaney]] later made non-enforcement presumptively unreviewable but reserved Adams in footnote 4.
- **What fills the gap:** Where no one can sue, the limits are enforced politically: by Congress's refusal to appropriate, by the President's veto, and by each member's oath, which is the answer Giles gave at Savannah ([[The Savannah Fire Debate]]).

## Exceptions

- **Permanent appropriations.** Funds made available indefinitely need no annual renewal and so escape the yearly purse-string check ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]).
- **Lump-sum and "not exceeding" appropriations.** The First Congress's entire first appropriations act was four sums "not exceeding" stated amounts, and a statutory discretion to "decline to spend" is valid beyond "the slightest doubt" (Scalia in [[Clinton v. City of New York]]). Stevens concedes the tradition and distinguishes it because none of those statutes authorized a change in statutory text.
- **General riders barring classes of employees.** A rider denying pay to members of organizations advocating overthrow of the government had become standard in appropriations acts and was not challenged; naming three individuals is what made § 304 an attainder ([[United States v. Lovett]]).
- **Refusals to honor rather than to pay.** Congress withheld money for a bust of Chief Justice Taney "for many years" after Sumner objected; the Compensation Clause protects judges' pay, not their portraits, and casebook Note 2 asks whether Congress may use the purse "purely to reject or condemn a Supreme Court opinion" ([[The Lincoln-Douglas Debates]]).
- **Riders directing the President's command.** Article IX against Andrew Johnson charged him with telling General Emory that the appropriations rider requiring orders to go through the general of the army was unconstitutional; the Senate acquitted, and the rider's validity was never adjudicated ([[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]).
- **Not decided: impoundment.** Nixon's claim of a "constitutional right" to impound was rejected in *Train v. City of New York* as a matter of statutory interpretation, which "implicitly confirmed that Congress may confer discretion upon the Executive to withhold appropriated funds." After the casebook, *Department of State v. AIDS Vaccine Advocacy Coalition* (2025) let a late-year "pocket rescission" of foreign aid proceed on an interim stay without deciding its legality.
- **Not decided: conditions in a field of exclusive presidential power.** Obama's objections to §§ 1112–1113 (detainee transfers, foreign affairs) and § 2262 (White House advisers) were asserted and never adjudicated ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
- **Not decided: a duty to spend.** Cl. 7 says money shall not be drawn without an appropriation; it does not say appropriated money must be drawn. A refusal to spend is better analysed under [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] than under cl. 7 (Burwell cold-call; [[Adams v. Richardson]]).

## Leading case

[[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]

- **Holding:** "The House of Representatives as an institution would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the Treasury without a valid appropriation," and that injury supports Article III standing (Collyer, J., D.D.C. 2015).
- **Holding:** The employer-mandate theory is "fundamentally a statutory argument," and "citing its most general provisions does not transform a statutory violation into a constitutional case or controversy."
- **Driving fact:** Congress refused to appropriate for the § 1402 cost-sharing reimbursements and the executive paid them anyway, so the claim "is not about how Section 1402 is being applied, but rather how it is funded."
- **Reasoning:** The Secretaries' suggestion that the House use political remedies left them "apparently oblivious to the irony": defunding is exactly what the House had done, and if the executive can spend anyway, "the purse strings would be cut."
- **Reasoning:** To the argument that the branches have never sued each other, "there will never be a history of litigation until the first lawsuit is filed"; the court also declined to treat Scalia's dissent in *United States v. Windsor* as precedent.
- **Aftermath:** On the merits in May 2016 the court held the payments violated Art. I, § 9 and stayed its injunction pending appeal; the Trump administration stopped the payments and settled, so no appellate court reviewed the standing holding, and the Supreme Court has never decided whether a House may sue to enforce cl. 7.
- **Quote:** "Congress's power of the purse is the ultimate check on the otherwise unbounded power of the Executive."

## Best counter-case

[[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]

- **Distinguishing line:** A plaintiff may enforce the purse only by showing an injury that belongs to it rather than to everyone, so the House suing over money actually drawn without an appropriation can sue, while a taxpayer or a State that declined a grant cannot.
- **Holding:** Both suits were dismissed "for want of jurisdiction, without considering the merits": Massachusetts had no justiciable injury in its own right, could not sue as parens patriae against the United States, and Frothingham's interest as a federal taxpayer was too remote.
- **Driving fact:** Frothingham's interest was "shared with millions of others," and the effect of any payment on her taxes was "so remote, fluctuating and uncertain" that equity could not act; Burwell's House claimed injury to a constitutional role that belongs to it alone.
- **Driving fact:** Massachusetts challenged a statute that "imposes no obligation but simply extends an option"; Burwell challenged money actually leaving the Treasury with no appropriation behind it.
- **Argue for Mellon's side:** The judiciary's duty is "interpreting and applying [the laws] in cases properly brought before the courts," and its review "amounts to little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment"; if one taxpayer may sue, "then every other taxpayer may do the same" as to "every other appropriation act."
- **Argue for Burwell's side:** "The political tug of war anticipated by the Constitution depends upon Article I, § 9, cl. 7 having some force," and a check that cannot be enforced when the executive ignores it is no check; the suit "is inherently limited by the extraordinary facts of which it was born."
- **Second counter-case:** [[United States v. Lovett]] runs the purse against Congress rather than the executive, and shows that control of appropriations is not plenary when its purpose is punishment.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Congress's control of the purse is unlimited."** [[United States v. Lovett]] holds that punishment "through the instrumentality of an Act specifically cutting off the pay of certain named individuals" is "no less galling or effective than if it had been done by an Act which designated the conduct as criminal."
- **"An unconstitutional rider frees the President to spend."** Voiding a restriction creates no appropriation, so for a pure funding denial the President's only remedy is not to spend ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]], Note 2).
- **Reading [[Clinton v. City of New York]] as a purse case.** Clinton protects the § 7 procedure; Scalia's impoundment history and Stevens's concession leave the President's statutory discretion to decline to spend intact. See [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7]].
- **"Every executive misreading of a spending statute violates the Constitution."** [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] dismissed the statutory counts; only spending with no appropriation at all is a cl. 7 violation.
- **"Repealing a war authorization ends the war."** Congress repealed the Tonkin Resolution in 1971 and kept funding the war; the reliable tool is the funding cutoff ([[Terminating War]]).
- **Confusing the spending power with the Appropriations Clause.** Whether Congress may spend on an object at all is the Art. I, § 8, cl. 1 question of [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]], argued out at Savannah; whether money may leave the Treasury without a law is cl. 7. Baldwin invoked cl. 7 as the structural check because the enumeration alone gave no "absolute directions" ([[The Savannah Fire Debate]]).
