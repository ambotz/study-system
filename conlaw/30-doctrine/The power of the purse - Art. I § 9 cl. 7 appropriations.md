## Rule statement

"No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law" (Art. I, § 9, cl. 7). Both an authorization and an appropriation are required before public money is spent, and an appropriation "must be expressly stated; it cannot be inferred or implied" ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]). The clause is therefore a one-way ratchet in Congress's favour: Congress may fund, decline to fund, or fund on conditions, and the executive's remedy for a condition it thinks unconstitutional is not to spend anyway, because voiding a restriction does not create an appropriation. The limits run the other way too — Congress may not use the purse to accomplish what another clause forbids it to do directly, as with the pay cutoff aimed at three named men in [[United States v. Lovett]]. The live contest is enforcement: on [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] neither a State nor a taxpayer may sue, which leaves the limits to politics, while [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] holds that the House as an institution may sue when the executive draws funds with no appropriation at all.

## Elements

1. **Is there an appropriation made by law?** Identify the statute and whether it is permanent (available indefinitely) or current (renewed, usually annually, which is what ties the executive to Congress "via purse string").
2. **Is the complaint that money was spent without any appropriation, or that it was spent outside a contested one?** The first is a constitutional claim under Art. I, § 9, cl. 7; the second is statutory, and it matters because only the constitutional claim supported House standing in [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] — "Article I is not a talisman."
3. **If Congress attached a condition or rider, test the condition itself, not the power to attach it.** Congress may generally refuse to fund operations it disagrees with; the limits come from the Constitution's other provisions, above all the Bill of Attainder Clause ([[United States v. Lovett]]) and any field in which the power is exclusively the President's.
4. **For an attainder challenge, ask specificity plus punishment.** Does the provision apply "either to named individuals or to easily ascertainable members of a group," and does it "inflict punishment on them without a judicial trial"? Permanent exclusion from government service "is punishment, and of a most severe type," and the form of the statute does not matter ([[United States v. Lovett]]).
5. **If the President objects, identify which of the four responses he chose.** Veto the whole bill; sign, object and comply ([[United States v. Lovett]], and Obama on §§ 1112–1113); sign and announce that the executive "will construe" the provision not to bind him (Obama on § 2262); or spend without an appropriation, which the clause forbids ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
6. **Place the executive's position in the Youngstown scheme.** Spending in the teeth of an express funding denial is category three, where the President must show a power "conclusive and preclusive"; a claim that Congress did appropriate is a category-one argument, which is what the Secretaries made in [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]].
7. **Ask who can bring the claim.** The House as an institution, on a non-appropriation theory, yes; individual members, a State in its own right, a State as parens patriae, and a federal taxpayer, no ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]).
8. **For war, ask whether the cutoff is a defunding or a mere repeal.** A repeal of an authorization while appropriations continue sends mixed signals; a funding cutoff is self-executing and unambiguous, which is why Vietnam ended when Congress cut off funds and not when it repealed the Tonkin Resolution ([[Terminating War]]).

## Standard of review / burden

The clause states a rule, not a standard, so where a court reaches the merits it simply asks whether an appropriation exists; the whole difficulty is getting a court to reach the merits. Justiciability is the battleground and it cuts both ways. [[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]] holds that a federal taxpayer's interest is "comparatively minute and indeterminable" and that a State's complaint that Congress usurped reserved powers is "political, and not judicial in character," because "[w]e have no power per se to review and annul acts of Congress on the ground that they are unconstitutional"; what fills the gap is the political process and the oaths of the other branches. [[United States v. Lovett]] refuses the mirror-image argument that a pay cutoff is an unreviewable appropriations measure, on the ground that otherwise "congressional action, aimed at three named individuals, which stigmatized their reputation and seriously impaired their chance to earn a living, could never be challenged in any court." [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] applies an "especially rigorous" standing analysis to suits between the branches and grants standing on the constitutional theory only, holding the claims justiciable because they "involve pure questions of constitutional interpretation" — and note that no appellate court ever reviewed that ruling, and the Supreme Court has never decided whether a House may sue to enforce the clause. Where the executive's non-enforcement of a funding condition is at issue the review question shifts to the APA: an agency's non-enforcement is reviewable when it has "consciously and expressly adopted a general policy which is in effect an abdication of its statutory duty," particularly where the agency is "actively supplying segregated institutions with federal funds, contrary to the expressed purposes of Congress" ([[Adams v. Richardson]]). The political-question route is the one the government reaches for and has generally lost on here: [[United States v. Lovett]] and [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] both reject it.

## Exceptions

- **Permanent appropriations.** Funds made available indefinitely need no annual renewal and sever the yearly purse-string check ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]).
- **Lump-sum and "not exceeding" appropriations.** The First Congress's entire first appropriations act was four sums "not exceeding" stated amounts, and a statutory discretion to decline to spend is valid beyond "the slightest doubt" (Scalia in [[Clinton v. City of New York]]); Stevens concedes the tradition and distinguishes it as not authorizing any change in statutory text.
- **Spending in aid of an enumerated power.** The Savannah objectors did not deny that money could be spent to protect the customs revenue or to support trade; Harper's and Claiborne's fallback was that rebuilding the port would repay itself "from the quick return the revenue would gain" ([[The Savannah Fire Debate]]).
- **General riders barring classes of employees.** A rider denying pay to members of organizations advocating violent overthrow of the government was standard practice and was not challenged; naming three individuals is what made § 304 an attainder ([[United States v. Lovett]]).
- **Not decided: impoundment.** [[Clinton v. City of New York]] leaves untouched a statutory power to withhold; Nixon's claim of a "constitutional right" to impound was rejected in Train v. City of New York as a matter of statutory interpretation, which "implicitly confirm[ed] that Congress may confer discretion upon the Executive to withhold appropriated funds."
- **Not decided: whether Congress may condition funds in a field of exclusive presidential power.** Obama's claims under §§ 1112–1113 (detainee transfers, foreign affairs) and § 2262 (advisers) were asserted and never adjudicated ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
- **Not decided: the mirror-image duty to spend.** The clause says money shall not be drawn without an appropriation; it does not say appropriated money must be drawn, and the asymmetry is why a refusal to spend is better analysed under [[Enforcement discretion - Art. II § 3 Take Care]] than under this clause ([[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]], [[Adams v. Richardson]]).
- **Salaries the Constitution protects.** Congress cannot reduce the President's or federal judges' compensation, so those are outside the ordinary rider power ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).

## Leading case

[[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]]

- Holds that "[t]he House of Representatives as an institution would suffer a concrete, particularized injury if the Executive were able to draw funds from the Treasury without a valid appropriation," and that the injury supports Article III standing.
- Holds, on the other half, that the employer-mandate theory is "fundamentally a statutory argument," and that "citing [Article I's] most general provisions does not transform a statutory violation into a constitutional case or controversy."
- The fact that drove it: Congress had refused to appropriate for the § 1402 cost-sharing reimbursements and the executive paid them anyway, so the claim "is not about how Section 1402 is being applied, but rather how it is funded."
- Disposes of the government's political-remedies argument with the observation that the Secretaries were "apparently oblivious to the irony" — defunding is exactly what the House had done, and if the executive can spend anyway "the purse strings would be cut."
- Answers the no-history argument with "there will never be a history of litigation until the first lawsuit is filed," and declines to treat a dissent joined by two other Justices as precedent.
- Know the aftermath and its limits: Collyer ruled for the House on the merits in May 2016 and stayed her injunction; the appeal was held in abeyance, the payments stopped in October 2017, and the case settled in December 2017, so no appellate court ever passed on the standing holding. The D.C. Circuit later accepted House Appropriations Clause standing in the border-wall litigation.
- The sentence worth quoting: "Congress's power of the purse is the ultimate check on the otherwise unbounded power of the Executive."

## Best counter-case

[[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]

- Cuts the other way on the question Burwell answers: who, if anyone, may take a spending limit to court. Mellon's answer is essentially nobody — not a federal taxpayer, not a State in its own right, not a State as parens patriae.
- The distinguishing fact is the identity and injury of the plaintiff. Frothingham's interest was "shared with millions of others" and the effect on her taxes "so remote, fluctuating and uncertain" that equity could not act; the House's injury in [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] was to a constitutional role that belongs to it alone.
- The second distinguishing fact is what the executive did. Massachusetts complained about a statute that "imposes no obligation but simply extends an option," frustrable "by the simple expedient of not yielding"; Burwell complained about money actually leaving the Treasury with no appropriation behind it.
- Argue the Mellon side: the judiciary's role is "to interpret[] and apply[] [the laws] in cases properly brought before the courts," and judicial review "amounts to little more than the negative power to disregard an unconstitutional enactment"; if one plaintiff may challenge one appropriation, "then every other taxpayer may do the same" as to "every other appropriation act."
- Argue the Burwell side: the political tug of war "depends upon Article I, § 9, cl. 7 having some force," and a check that cannot be enforced when the executive ignores it is no check; the suit "is inherently limited by the extraordinary facts of which it was born."
- The pair also frames the deeper exam question — who polices the spending power at all. On Mellon's logic the answer is Congress and the President through their oaths, which is exactly the answer the Savannah objectors gave themselves when Giles said the House "ought not to attend to what 'the affairs of men' or what generosity and humanity required, but what the Constitution and their duty required" ([[The Savannah Fire Debate]]).
- Second counter worth holding in reserve: [[United States v. Lovett]] runs the clause against Congress rather than against the executive, and shows that plenary control of appropriations is not plenary at all when the purpose is punishment.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **Treating "Congress controls the purse" as unlimited.** [[United States v. Lovett]] holds that Congress cannot do through a pay cutoff what the Bill of Attainder Clause forbids directly, and that punishment "through the instrumentality of an Act specifically cutting off the pay" is "no less galling or effective than if it had been done by an Act which designated the conduct as criminal."
- **Thinking an unconstitutional rider frees the President to spend.** It does not. An unconstitutional restriction may be void, but voiding it creates no appropriation, so for a pure funding denial the President's only remedy is not to spend ([[Obama, Statement on H.R. 1473]]).
- **Calling a signing statement a line-item veto.** A signing statement changes nothing in the law and a court remains free to disagree, unlike the cancellation in [[Clinton v. City of New York]], which deprived enacted text of "legal force or effect." The real objection to the § 2262 statement is the older one to the suspending and dispensing powers, not the Presentment Clause.
- **Reading [[Clinton v. City of New York]] as protecting the purse.** It protects the § 7 procedure. Scalia's history of impoundment, and Stevens's concession that spending-discretion statutes remain valid, mean the case leaves the President's statutory power to decline to spend intact.
- **Assuming any executive misreading of a spending statute is a constitutional violation.** [[U.S. House of Representatives v. Burwell]] dismissed the statutory counts; the line is between spending with no appropriation and spending under a contested one, and Collyer came close to deciding the merits in order to decide standing.
- **Assuming a State can sue for its citizens.** "[I]t is the United States, and not the state, which represents them as parens patriae" ([[Massachusetts v. Mellon; Frothingham v. Mellon]]), and a State that has accepted nothing and been ordered to do nothing has no injury at all.
- **Thinking repealing a war authorization ends a war.** Congress repealed the Tonkin Resolution in January 1971 and Nixon kept fighting on his Commander in Chief authority while Congress kept funding; the war ended when the money stopped ([[Terminating War]]).
- **Forgetting that the purse lets one House coerce the other.** The Senate voted 69 to 0 against § 304 and yielded because the House would pass no appropriation without it ([[United States v. Lovett]]); the same hostage dynamic made Obama sign H.R. 1473 "because of the importance of avoiding a lapse in appropriations."
- **Missing the spending-power/appropriations distinction.** Whether Congress may spend on an object at all is the Art. I, § 8, cl. 1 question of [[Spending power - Art. I § 8 cl. 1 general welfare and conditional grants]], argued out at Savannah; whether money may leave the Treasury without a law is this clause. Baldwin invoked the Appropriations Clause as the structural check precisely because the enumeration alone gave no "absolute directions" ([[The Savannah Fire Debate]]).
