## Rule statement

Article II § 1 vests "the executive Power" in a single President, and removal is a corollary of that vesting: an officer who exercises executive power must be removable by the President at will, whatever the agency's structure, so a statute limiting removal to cause is unconstitutional as to that officer ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]). [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] survives at most as the "observation" that a body exercising "no part of the executive power" falls outside the rule — "[i]f anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it" — so the operative question is the character of the power, not the number of agency heads. Two protections survive: the Federal Reserve's Governors, on the ground that the Fed "follows in this tradition" of the First and Second Banks' insulation from the President, and, where a protection survives, "cause" is judicially reviewable and sets "a substantial threshold" with pre-removal notice and a chance to respond ([[Trump v. Cook]]). The contest is over what [[The Decision of 1789]] did: the majority reads the First Congress to have recognized a constitutional power Congress "has no right to diminish or modify," so a default rule and an indefeasible one "are one and the same," while the dissent reads it to have chosen a default that Congress may vary; see [[Modes of constitutional argument]].

## Elements

1. **Is the person an officer of the United States at all?** Not every congressionally chartered entity has executive power — the Court names the Boy Scouts and Georgetown University — and the historical argument for the Fed turns partly on the First Bank's directors answering to private stockholders, which Hamilton thought "an essential ingredient in its structure" ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]; [[Trump v. Cook]]).
2. **Does the office exercise executive power?** This is the whole test, and it is satisfied by any one of the following.
   - Rulemaking that fleshes out a statutory regime: the power to "flesh out such statutory regimes . . . is executive through and through."
   - Investigation, and in-house adjudication of charges against private parties.
   - "[T]he 'discretionary power to seek judicial relief'" — suing for penalties and restitution ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
   - There is no fourth box: when an agency "executes" a statute against private parties, "it exercises executive power—no ifs, ands, or quasis about it," so the 1935 characterization of the FTC as "predominantly quasi-judicial and quasi-legislative" was "a highly circumscribed and almost fictional view."
3. **If the office exercises executive power, it is removable at will**, and any statutory limit on the grounds is void. Multimember structure, partisan balance, expertise and fixed terms are all irrelevant: "those who fall within the President's 'general administrative control' must be removable by the President at will."
4. **If it exercises no executive power, the rule does not run** and Congress may protect it — the only surviving content of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]].
5. **Then check the reserved categories**, which [[Trump v. Slaughter]] expressly did not decide: the Federal Reserve "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States"; judges of non-Article III courts; and inferior officers with limited duties, whose for-cause protection *Morrison v. Olson* (1988) upheld and which Slaughter does not touch. *Free Enterprise Fund* (2010) still bars two layers of for-cause protection, so a single protected layer below at-will commissioners is the surviving configuration.
6. **Where a for-cause protection does survive, run the four-part Cook inquiry** ([[Trump v. Cook]]).
   - *Reviewability.* Removal "is a decision only the President can make (short of impeachment). But that does not mean that he may make that decision for any reason, or no reason." Unless Congress commits cause to the President or bars review — and in § 242 it did neither — the courts decide whether cause was identified.
   - *Meaning of cause.* A common-law term of art: "substantial, reasonable and just." The Government's "any concern about conduct, ability, fitness, or competence" is "too lenient"; confining cause to the 1935 statutory list is "too stringent." Cause turns "on the seriousness of the alleged misconduct, and the extent of any nexus that may exist to the Governor's professional duties," and asks whether the charge shows "an unfitness for the place" or is a pretext for a "more congenial" replacement. Pre-office conduct is not categorically excluded.
   - *Process.* A fixed term plus removal for cause carries a common-law right to notice of the charges and an opportunity to respond, which may be "on written materials only." "Cook must resign, now!!!" is not notice: at minimum the officer is owed "some explanation of the evidence at issue, some avenue for a response, and a deadline by which a response would be due."
   - *Remedy.* A court may keep the officer in place pending litigation if she is otherwise entitled to a preliminary injunction, because equity protected "the possession of officers de facto, . . . pending a litigation" over title.

## Standard of review / burden

On the constitutional question there is no deference to Congress: the Court rejected the proposal that courts police removal limits only for reasonableness, because that principle "is neither limiting nor much of a principle" and would let Congress insulate most of the government, and it refused to give weight to long modern practice — "[w]e have never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power," citing [[INS v. Chadha]], which struck the legislative veto despite hundreds of statutes using it ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]). Stare decisis was run as a four-factor inquiry (quality of reasoning, consistency, workability, reliance), and reliance failed because Congress relied on Humphrey's to build agencies "insulated from presidential control" and "[t]hat is precisely the problem" — "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere." On the statutory question the burden runs the other way: review of a President's determination of cause is "deferential," but it is pretext review, and the Court will not "exhibit a naiveté from which ordinary citizens are free" (*Department of Commerce v. New York*); in [[Trump v. Cook]] the Government, as stay applicant, had to show likelihood of success and failed, because "[t]o accept any one of those arguments would in effect transform the Federal Reserve's for-cause protection into at-will employment." Nothing here is a political question: the removal of an officer is justiciable, and [[United States v. Lovett]] refused to treat a congressional proscription of named individuals as unreviewable because otherwise such action "could never be challenged in any court." The gap the doctrine leaves is filled by impeachment (Art. II § 4) and by the electorate, which is the only check on removals the Court now holds absolutely immune from criminal prosecution ([[Trump v. United States]]).

## Exceptions

- Offices exercising no executive power at all — the only thing [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]] still stands for; a multimember body that only investigates and reports to Congress fits ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
- The Federal Reserve's Governors, whose for-cause protection "is consistent with the Constitution" because the Fed follows the First and Second Banks with "a similar degree of independence from Presidential control," a tradition that need not be "trapped in amber" ([[Trump v. Cook]]).
- Purely adjudicatory bodies and judges of non-Article III courts, reserved in Part III-B of [[Trump v. Slaughter]]; *Wiener* (1958), the only case ever to apply Humphrey's, involved "a solely adjudicatory body," and the Court calls its constitutional analysis "one brisk paragraph" without deciding it.
- Inferior officers with limited duties, governed by *Morrison v. Olson*, which Slaughter uses (footnote 28) but does not overrule.
- Impeachment is a separate removal route that no Article II holding displaces, and Madison's answer in 1789 was that impeachment is "a supplemental security for the good behavior of the public officers," not the exclusive method; see [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].
- The rule runs only against limits on the President's removal, not in favor of Congress removing: the First Congress rejected both Senate consent to removal (Bland) and impeachment-only (Smith), *Myers v. United States* (1926) struck down Senate participation, and the Tenure of Office Act of 1867 — under which Johnson was impeached for firing Stanton — was diluted under Grant and repealed under Cleveland ([[The Decision of 1789]]; [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson]]).
- The defunding workaround is limited but not closed: [[United States v. Lovett]] held that a rider cutting off three named employees' pay was a bill of attainder because its purpose was "permanently to bar them from government service," and the Court expressly did **not** reach the removal or due process claims — so whether Congress may remove an officer by defunding the office remains open; see [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]] and [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]].
- Not decided in [[Trump v. Cook]]: whether Cook may in fact be removed for cause, whether the alleged conduct is cause, and the constitutional due process claim.
- Not decided after [[Trump v. Slaughter]]: whether the Fed's bank-supervision and consumer-regulation functions fall inside the Bank tradition or only monetary policy does, since the reservation is written "to the extent that" the Fed follows it.

## Leading case

[[Trump v. Slaughter]]

- Holds the FTC Act's removal limit "contrary to the separation of powers enshrined in the Constitution," reversing and remanding; 6–3, Roberts, C.J., with Thomas declining to join the reservations in Part III-B, Gorsuch concurring, and Sotomayor dissenting with Kagan and Jackson.
- The fact that drove it: the President fired two commissioners without claiming statutory cause, telling them their service was "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities," so the statute was squarely violated and the constitutional question could not be avoided — the same posture as Roosevelt's firing of Humphrey.
- The second driving fact: today's FTC "administers some 80 statutes," makes binding rules, adjudicates in-house and sues in federal court. "The FTC unquestionably exercises executive power, and must therefore be controlled by the Chief Executive, in whom such power is vested," and "this is not a close case."
- The argument is text plus structure plus liquidation. Vesting the executive power in one President "was to establish a hierarchy"; "[t]o remain accountable to the President, those officers must be removable by the President"; the [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]] "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the question, with Madison's position that the Legislature "has no right to diminish or modify" the power; and Marshall, Kent, Story and every President from Washington through John Quincy Adams treated it as settled.
- It relocates the line. [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]] and *Seila Law* turned on agency structure; Slaughter turns on the kind of power, so "quasi-legislative" and "quasi-judicial" disappear as categories and *Collins*'s structure-over-power approach is in effect reversed.
- Gorsuch's concurrence states the cost of the win: independent agencies exercise delegated legislative and judicial power too, and "after today's decision, the President can effectively exercise all those powers too," which sharpens the problem in [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]] and [[Gundy v. United States]].
- Sentences worth quoting: "no ifs, ands, or quasis about it"; "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere"; "a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power"; "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."

## Best counter-case

[[Trump v. Cook]]

- Decided the same day as [[Trump v. Slaughter]], 5–4 with a scrambled lineup: Roberts joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh and Jackson, against Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch and Barrett, four of whom were in the Slaughter majority. A for-cause protection survives.
- The distinguishing fact is neither structure nor function but history. The Fed is not distinguished on the ground that it exercises no executive power — Thomas is right that the Board regulates banks and imposes penalties — but on the ground that the First Bank (1791) and the Second Bank (1816) were insulated from the President, only five of the Second Bank's twenty-five directors being presidentially appointed.
- Argue the Fed's side with Slaughter's own method. *Myers* placed "the greatest weight" on early congressional practice; the same First Congress that made the Decision of 1789 chartered in 1791 a bank the President did not control, so 1789 and 1791 must be read together. A historical exception is formalist if the history is right.
- Argue the President's side with Thomas: the First and Second Banks "possessed no sovereign power" and were private corporations, while the Board is a federal regulator modelled by Warburg, Aldrich and Wilson on European central banks; on that account the majority's real ground is that independence is good policy, which is an argument "against the Constitution."
- Press the [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] tension both ways: McCulloch upheld the Bank as an instrument for carrying federal powers into execution, which cuts toward Thomas (an instrument of executing the laws should answer to the executive), while Cook treats the same institution as the model of insulation from the President.
- The [[The Federalist No. 70]] objection applies with equal force to a seven-member Board setting monetary policy as to a five-member FTC — plurality "tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility" — so the unity argument cannot be what separates the two cases.
- On the statutory holding Cook cuts against the President even where a protection survives: "for cause" is not committed to him, its sufficiency was a question for the courts at common law, and "[n]othing could be more corrosive of the independence that Congress sought to preserve" than a standard under which "any perceived or alleged misstep (past or present) could provide a ready pretext."
- Secondary counters worth naming: [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]] itself, for the proposition that "one who holds his office only during the pleasure of another cannot be depended upon to maintain an attitude of independence against the latter's will"; and [[The Federalist No. 76]], where Hamilton defends the Senate's check on appointments as "an efficacious source of stability in the administration," which Bland read in 1789 as implying a Senate role in removal.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- Saying Humphrey's was flatly overruled. The overruling is conditional in form — what survives is the "observation" that a body exercising no executive power is outside the rule — and *Morrison*, *Wiener* and the non-Article III courts are untouched, so an answer that treats every for-cause protection as void overshoots.
- Carrying forward the single-head line from *Seila Law* and *Collins*. After [[Trump v. Slaughter]] the number of agency heads is irrelevant; an answer that saves a multimember commission because it is multimember is applying a rule the Court abandoned.
- Reading [[Trump v. Cook]] as holding that Cook cannot be removed. The ground of decision was statutory process; the Court expressly did not decide whether the mortgage allegations are cause, did not reach the constitutional due process claim, and decided only likelihood of success on a stay.
- Explaining the Fed carve-out functionally — because central-bank independence is valuable, or because markets need it. The stated rationale is historical, tied to the First and Second Banks; the functional points (credibility, "the appearance of independence is key to the Federal Reserve's design") appear in the statutory discussion of what "cause" must mean, not as the Article II ground.
- Citing [[The Decision of 1789]] as having settled that Congress may not limit removal. The editors say only two things are "clear": the House rejected a Senate role in removal and rejected impeachment-only. Whether the vote endorsed Madison's constitutional theory or a mixed coalition including Laurance's congressional-choice position "is not so clear," and the three readings (constitutional recognition, legislative grant, limited to purely executive officers) drive Myers, Humphrey's and Slaughter respectively.
- Treating [[United States v. Lovett]] as a removal holding, or confusing [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States]] with [[Buckley v. Valeo]]. Lovett was decided on the Bill of Attainder Clause and expressly left removal and due process undecided; Humphrey's Executor is the FTC case, not the FEC case, whatever the syllabus's caption says.
