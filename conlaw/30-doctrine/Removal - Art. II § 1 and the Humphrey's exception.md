## Rule statement

The President may fire at will any officer who exercises executive power, so a for-cause limit on removing that officer is unconstitutional, whether the agency has one head or five ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]). Vesting the executive power in one President "was to establish a hierarchy," and "[t]o remain accountable to the President, those officers must be removable by the President." [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] survives only for a body exercising "no part of the executive power"; "[i]f anything more is left of Humphrey's, we overrule it." The Federal Reserve Governors' protection survives on a historical ground tied to the First and Second Banks, and there "cause" is reviewable and sets "a substantial threshold" ([[Trump v. Cook]]). The contest is over [[The Decision of 1789]]: the majority reads it as recognizing a power Congress "has no right to diminish or modify," the dissent as choosing a default Congress may vary.

## Elements

1. **Does the statute give Congress or the Senate a role in removal, rather than limiting the President's grounds?** A congressional role fails under every reading of 1789.
   - The First Congress rejected both Senate consent to removal (Bland) and impeachment as the only method (Smith); the editors call that rejection the one thing that is "clear" ([[The Decision of 1789]]).
   - The Tenure of Office Act required Senate consent; [[The Impeachment of Andrew Johnson|Johnson was impeached]] for firing Stanton under it; the Act was diluted under Grant, repealed under Cleveland, and called invalid in [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] (1926).
   - Congress may not keep for itself a power to remove an officer who executes the laws (*Bowsher v. Synar*, paired with [[Buckley v. Valeo]] as the removal half of the limits on congressional control of officers).
   - A pure cause limit leaves the President the only remover and routes to element 2; [[Trump v. Slaughter]] treats it as no less invalid than the Myers statute's Senate veto.
2. **Does the office exercise executive power?** Any one of these powers is enough ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]).
   - Rulemaking that fleshes out a statutory regime, which is "executive through and through."
   - Investigation, and in-house adjudication of charges against private parties.
   - "[T]he 'discretionary power to seek judicial relief'" — suing for penalties and restitution, which [[Buckley v. Valeo]] held only officers may do.
   - There is no fourth box: an agency executing a statute against private parties "exercises executive power—no ifs, ands, or quasis about it."
   - Not every congressionally chartered entity exercises executive power; the Court names the Boy Scouts and Georgetown University.
3. **If the office exercises executive power, the officer is removable at will.**
   - "[T]hose who fall within the President's 'general administrative control' must be removable by the President at will."
   - Multimember structure, partisan balance, expertise and fixed terms do not save a protection.
   - The single-head line of [[Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau|Seila Law]] (2020) and *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) no longer decides anything.
4. **If the office exercises no executive power, Congress may protect it.**
   - The Humphrey's residue is the only authority for the protection.
   - Example: a multimember body that only investigates and reports to Congress ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
5. **Does the office fall in a category Slaughter reserved or left untouched?**
   - The Federal Reserve, "to the extent that it follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States" — decided in [[Trump v. Cook]].
   - Judges of non-Article III courts, and purely adjudicatory bodies like the one in *Wiener v. United States* (1958), whose constitutional analysis the Court calls "one brisk paragraph."
   - Inferior officers with limited duties, whose protection [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]] (1988) upheld and Slaughter does not overrule.
   - [[Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board|Free Enterprise Fund]] (2010) still bars two layers of for-cause protection.
   - Hypothetical: an ALJ removable only for cause by FTC commissioners. After Slaughter the commissioners are at will, so the single protected layer below them is a Morrison question and may survive.
6. **Where a protection survives, apply the four-part Cook inquiry** ([[Trump v. Cook]]).
   - **Reviewability.** Removal "is a decision only the President can make (short of impeachment). But that does not mean that he may make that decision for any reason, or no reason."
   - **Who decides cause.** Courts decide whether cause was identified unless Congress commits cause to the President or bars review; 12 U.S.C. § 242 does neither.
   - **Threshold.** "Cause" is a common-law term meaning "substantial, reasonable and just." Any concern about "conduct, ability, fitness, or competence" is "too lenient"; the 1935 statutory list of in-office faults is "too stringent."
   - **Factors.** Cause turns "on the seriousness of the alleged misconduct, and the extent of any nexus that may exist to the Governor's professional duties." Pre-office conduct is not categorically excluded.
   - **Pretext.** The ultimate question is whether the charge shows "an unfitness for the place" or is an effort to install a "more congenial" replacement.
   - **Process.** A fixed term plus for-cause removal carries notice of the charges and a chance to respond, which may be "on written materials only." "Cook must resign, now!!!" is not notice.
   - **Interim remedy.** A court may keep the officer in place pending litigation if she otherwise qualifies for a preliminary injunction, because equity protected "the possession of officers de facto, . . . pending a litigation."

## Standard of review / burden

- **Presumption:** None in Congress's favor on the constitutional question. The Court rejected policing removal limits only for reasonableness, because that principle "is neither limiting nor much of a principle" and would let Congress insulate most of the government ([[Trump v. Slaughter]]).
- **Standard (practice):** Long modern practice earns no weight: "[w]e have never endorsed such a practice-makes-perfect theory of congressional power." [[INS v. Chadha]] struck the legislative veto despite hundreds of statutes using it.
- **Standard (stare decisis):** Four factors — quality of reasoning, consistency, workability, reliance. Reliance failed because Congress relied on Humphrey's to build agencies "insulated from presidential control," and "[t]hat is precisely the problem."
- **Burden (statutory cause):** In [[Trump v. Cook]] the Government, as stay applicant, had to show likely success and failed, because accepting any of its arguments "would in effect transform the Federal Reserve's for-cause protection into at-will employment."
- **Standard (statutory cause):** Review of the President's cause finding is "deferential" but is pretext review; the Court will not "exhibit a naiveté from which ordinary citizens are free" (*Department of Commerce v. New York*).
- **Not reviewed / political question:** Nothing here is a political question. Cook reviews cause itself, and [[United States v. Lovett]] refused to treat a pay cutoff aimed at three named officers as unreviewable, since otherwise such action "could never be challenged in any court."
- **What fills the gap:** Impeachment (Art. II § 4) and the electorate. After [[Trump v. United States]], a removal within the President's "conclusive and preclusive" authority cannot ground a criminal prosecution, and Slaughter widens that zone to every executive officer; see [[Presidential immunity and privilege - Art. II]].

## Exceptions

- **No executive power:** The only surviving content of [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's]]; Congress may protect a body that only investigates and reports ([[Buckley v. Valeo]]).
- **Federal Reserve Governors:** Their protection "is consistent with the Constitution" because the Fed follows the First and Second Banks "with a similar degree of independence from Presidential control," a tradition that need not be "trapped in amber" ([[Trump v. Cook]]). Consequence: a Governor can be removed only on reviewable cause, after notice.
- **Non-Article III judges and adjudicators:** Reserved in Part III-B of [[Trump v. Slaughter]]; *Wiener* involved "a solely adjudicatory body," and the Court left its analysis undecided.
- **Inferior officers with limited duties:** Governed by [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]], which Slaughter cites for Morrison's footnote 28 concession but does not overrule.
- **Impeachment:** A separate removal route no Article II holding displaces. Madison in 1789 called it "a supplemental security for the good behavior of the public officers," not the exclusive method; see [[Impeachment - Art. II § 4 high crimes and misdemeanors]].
- **Removal by defunding:** [[United States v. Lovett]] struck a rider cutting off three named employees' pay as a bill of attainder, because its purpose was "permanently to bar them from government service." The Court expressly did not reach the removal claim, so whether Congress may remove an officer by defunding the office is open; see [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations]] and [[Bills of attainder - Art. I § 9 cl. 3]].
- **Not decided (Cook):** Whether Cook may in fact be removed for cause, whether the mortgage allegations are cause, and the constitutional due process claim.
- **Not decided (Cook's Article II holding):** Whether it is dictum, since the Government conceded the statute's constitutionality and the ground of decision was statutory process; Alito and Barrett objected to reaching it on a stay application.
- **Not decided (Fed scope):** Whether the Fed's bank-supervision and consumer-regulation functions fall inside the Bank tradition or only monetary policy does, since Slaughter's reservation reads "to the extent that" the Fed follows it.

## Leading case

[[Trump v. Slaughter]]

- **Holding:** The FTC Act's removal limit is "contrary to the separation of powers enshrined in the Constitution"; reversed and remanded. 6–3, Roberts, C.J.; Thomas joined all but Part III-B; Gorsuch concurred; Sotomayor dissented, joined by Kagan and Jackson.
- **Driving fact (the firing):** The President removed two commissioners without claiming statutory cause, calling their service "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities." The statute was squarely violated, as in Roosevelt's firing of Humphrey, so the constitutional question could not be avoided.
- **Driving fact (the agency):** Today's FTC "administers some 80 statutes," makes binding rules, adjudicates in-house and sues in federal court. "The FTC unquestionably exercises executive power," and "this is not a close case."
- **Reasoning (text and history):** One President means a hierarchy; the [[The Decision of 1789|Decision of 1789]] "liquidate[d] & settle[d]" the question; Marshall, Kent and Story, and every President from Washington through John Quincy Adams, treated removal as settled.
- **Reasoning (precedent):** Humphrey's "quasi-judicial and quasi-legislative" label was "a highly circumscribed and almost fictional view." Later cases put litigation ([[Buckley v. Valeo|Buckley]]), rulemaking ([[INS v. Chadha|Chadha]]) and interpretation (*Bowsher*) on the executive side, so the test moves from agency structure to the kind of power.
- **Separate opinions:** Gorsuch (concurring) warns that independent agencies also exercise delegated legislative and judicial power, and "after today's decision, the President can effectively exercise all those powers too"; see [[Nondelegation - Art. I § 1 and the intelligible principle]]. Sotomayor (dissenting) presses reliance, finds "no evidence" that the founders adopted a "general rule of at-will removal," and reads 1789 as a mere default.
- **Quote:** "[N]o ifs, ands, or quasis about it"; "[n]o branch may rely on adverse possession to claim power that the Constitution vests elsewhere"; "Our Constitution creates three branches, but only one President."

## Best counter-case

[[Trump v. Cook]]

- **Distinguishing line:** A removal protection survives only where the office exercises no executive power or sits in a founding-era tradition of insulation from the President, as the Fed does through the First and Second Banks; neither agency structure nor the policy value of independence counts.
- **The vote:** Decided the same day, 5–4: Roberts, joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh and Jackson. All four dissenters (Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Barrett) were in the Slaughter majority.
- **Driving fact (history, not function):** The Fed is not saved for lacking executive power; Thomas is right that the Board regulates banks and imposes penalties. It is saved because the First Bank (1791) answered to private stockholders — Hamilton wanted it "under a private not a public Direction" — and the President appointed only five of the Second Bank's twenty-five directors.
- **Argue for the Fed:** Use Slaughter's own method. [[Myers v. United States|Myers]] placed "the greatest weight" on early congressional practice, and the First Congress that made the Decision of 1789 chartered an uncontrolled bank in 1791, so 1789 and 1791 must be read together. A historical exception is formalist if the history is right.
- **Argue for the President:** Thomas: the Banks "possessed no sovereign power" and were private corporations, while the Board is a federal regulator modelled on European central banks, so the majority's real ground is policy — "arguments against the Constitution." [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] helps him: an instrument for carrying federal powers into execution should answer to the executive.
- **Argue that unity cannot separate the cases:** [[The Federalist No. 70]]'s objection that plurality "tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility" applies to a seven-member Board as much as to a five-member FTC.
- **Argue that Slaughter misreads the founding:** Hamilton's target in No. 70 was a council "whose concurrence is made constitutionally necessary to the operations of the ostensible Executive"; an independent agency acts in its own name and the President needs its consent to nothing. Add Hamilton's "displace" in No. 77, which Slaughter dismisses as one of "two passing comments."
- **Argue from the Humphrey's side:** "[O]ne who holds his office only during the pleasure of another cannot be depended upon to maintain an attitude of independence against the latter's will." [[The Federalist No. 76]] praises the Senate's role as "an efficacious source of stability in the administration," which Bland read in 1789 as implying a Senate role in removal; see [[Appointments - Art. II § 2 cl. 2 and the officer line]].

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

- **"Humphrey's was flatly overruled."** The overruling is conditional in form: the "observation" about bodies with no executive power survives, and [[Morrison v. Olson|Morrison]], *Wiener* and the non-Article III courts are untouched. An answer treating every for-cause protection as void overshoots.
- **"A multimember commission is safe because it is multimember."** That was the *Seila Law* and *Collins* line. After [[Trump v. Slaughter]] the number of heads is irrelevant; only the character of the power matters.
- **"Cook holds that Cook cannot be removed."** The ground of decision was statutory process, on a stay application. The Court did not decide whether the mortgage allegations are cause and did not reach due process; Kavanaugh stresses that removal for cause remains open.
- **"The Fed survives because central-bank independence is valuable."** The Article II rationale is historical, tied to the First and Second Banks. The functional points ("the appearance of independence is key to the Federal Reserve's design") appear in the statutory discussion of what "cause" means.
- **"The Decision of 1789 settled that Congress may not limit removal."** The editors say only the rejection of a Senate role and of impeachment-only is "clear"; whether the vote endorsed Madison's theory "is not so clear." The three readings map onto the cases: constitutional recognition is Myers and the Slaughter majority; legislative grant is closest to the Slaughter dissent's default rule; limited to purely executive officers is Sutherland in Humphrey's.
- **"Lovett is a removal case" or "Humphrey's is the FEC case."** [[United States v. Lovett]] was decided on the Bill of Attainder Clause and left removal undecided. [[Humphrey's Executor v. United States|Humphrey's Executor]] concerns the FTC; [[Buckley v. Valeo]] is the FEC case, whatever the syllabus caption says.
