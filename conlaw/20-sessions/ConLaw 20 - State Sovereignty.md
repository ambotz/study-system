### [[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|SAMTA Case (1985)]]

- What prong of interstate Commerce?
    - Could be Channels or Instrumentalities OR Substantial effects.
    - Local economic activity affecting interstate commerce ([[Wickard v. Filburn (1942)|Wickard]] & [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]]).
    - [[United States v. Darby (1941)|Darby]] itself was on FLSA.

### Blackmun's Opinion

- National political process passed the FLSA → Texas was represented and still the FLSA was passed.
    - → "political safeguard" of federalism.
    - → Response: Court should also be a safeguard. The idea of preserving federalism should not be left to Congress, because Congress can be too partisan.
- Pro political safeguard:
    1. More people will pay attention to what their Senators are doing.
    2. Better for Congress to dictate bands of federalism via policy vs. Courts to police boundary (unelected; [[Brutus No. 11|Brutus]]).
- States directly saying a law is unconstitutional?
    - Unpopular because federal govt. strong arms.
- Exam Note: Always level set what prong of interstate commerce you are in and what case supplies.

### [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]

- Commerce power for gun regulation at points of sale = channels + commerce itself + substantial effects via economic activity/class of activities.
- Is there a difference between State officials directly enforcing federal law ([[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]) vs. a federal law requiring minimum wage ([[Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985)|Garcia]])?
    - → What's the tangible difference? Baude: none [?] in this aspect.

### [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] cont.

- Can regulate the states but can't commandeer states.
    - Where's the line?
        - Congress regulating private parties directly.
        - Congress using state mechanisms (means) that are necessary and **proper** → problem with Brady Act, not proper means.

### J. Scalia Art. 2

- Congress can't bypass Executive branch (federal enforcement) by asking states to enforce themselves the laws of Congress.
    - → Normative arg. to read federalism broadly; resources arg.
    - → Makes it easier to endorse/execute + states are now implicitly bought in.

### Against Commandeering

1. Forcing States to buy into the law (implicitly); resources of state drained.
2. Side-stepping Art. 2 & the take care clause by Executive → accountability should run to the Executive / federal govt.
    - Margin: Marshall great power
    - Yet, under political safeguard theory, it's clear Executive is in charge; **just vote**.

- BUT, this means states can't enforce federal laws **even if they wanted to** → can't accept cooperative federalism.
    - What if States enact the law themselves? That should be fine. Separation of powers issue gone.

### Other points on [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]]

- First opinion to approach interstate Commerce bottoms up, from the states.
- History: States rejected explicit state powers enumerated during drafting of Constitution because they thought it would be read too narrowly → chose 10A instead.
    - Lesson: Courts / US have read federal powers broadly over time.

### [[Printz v. United States (1997)|Printz]] Effects

- Allows States to not enforce federal laws (i.e., immigration, gun laws).
