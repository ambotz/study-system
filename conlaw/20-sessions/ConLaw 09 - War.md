- Art. 2, Sec. 2, Cl. 2 → POTUS commander in chief → proof that maybe there isn't a military executive theory (or there wouldn't be a need to call this out) [check: the Commander in Chief Clause is Art. II, § 2, cl. 1]

### [[The power of the purse - Art. I § 9 cl. 7 appropriations|Power of the purse]] arguments

- Response: Congress can't use the power of the purse as an end run
    - Response to Congress attempting to curtail executive through war funding decisions

### Two kinds of war?

- "Declare" change indicates President has inherent power to "repel attacks"
    - **Historical context based on [[Madison's Notes on the War Power|Madison's notes]] of framing debates**

### [[The Prize Cases (1863)|Prize Cases]]

- Does Congress need to authorize blockade of ships during Civil War?
    - Majority - Yes [check: in the Prize Cases the majority held no congressional authorization was needed; the dissent said it was]
    - Dissent - No
    - 3rd option: Congress can never declare war on parts of the country (no one takes this view)
- Lincoln responded without Congressional approval
    - He responded to "repel" the Confederate act → within Madison's reasoning
    - Still, isn't martial law a step above "repelling" attacks?

### Difference between [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]]?

- Not clear what side Congress is on here (they're out of session)
- War is at home, not in Korea ("Theatre of war" J. Black)

### [[The Emancipation Proclamation|Emancipation proclamation]]

- Only freed slaves in South → war power only extends to the South. What about parts of South where there's no war?
- Fully in [[Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)|Youngstown]] territory since seizure on land

### [[War powers - Art. I § 8 cl. 11 and the Commander in Chief|War Powers Resolution]] (1973)

- Law that passed over veto; Nixon thought it was unconstitutional

1. §5(b): 60 days after use of force POTUS must terminate use of force until Congress declares war. → Executive war power rebuttal
    - **Disagreement**
2. §5(c): Congress can pass by concurrent resolution to make president stop whenever
    - Constitutionally violates the [[Bicameralism and presentment - Art. I § 7|presentment clause]] → Nixon's argument ([[INS v. Chadha (1983)|Chadha]])
    - **Everyone agrees**

- §5(b): If President doesn't have authority to continue hostilities indefinitely w/o Congress → Not unconstitutional
