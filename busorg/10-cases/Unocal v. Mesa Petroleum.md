## Rule

Because of "the omnipresent specter that a board may be acting primarily in its own
interests," a defensive measure is reviewed under an **enhanced duty** before the
business judgment rule attaches: the directors must show **reasonable grounds to
believe a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness existed**, satisfied by good
faith and reasonable investigation, **and** that the response was **reasonable in
relation to the threat posed**.

## Facts

- Mesa held about **13%** of Unocal and launched a **two-tier front-loaded** tender
  offer: $54 cash for 64 million shares, roughly 37%, with the back end to be
  eliminated by securities purportedly worth $54. The structure is the whole case —
  a two-tier offer is "a classic coercive measure designed to stampede shareholders
  into tendering at the first tier, even if the price is inadequate, out of fear of
  what they will receive at the back end."
- A federal court order forced Mesa to disclose that the back-end securities would
  be **highly subordinated** and would leave Unocal's capitalisation significantly
  changed. Unocal called them "junk bonds," and the board concluded they were worth
  far less than $54.
- Goldman Sachs and Dillon Read advised that the **minimum cash value from a sale or
  orderly liquidation of 100% of Unocal exceeded $60 per share**, so $54 was wholly
  inadequate.
- The board was **eight independent outside directors and six insiders**. The eight
  outsiders met **separately** with the advisors and lawyers and then unanimously
  recommended rejection and a self-tender. Unocal says proof of good faith and
  reasonable investigation "is materially enhanced... by the approval of a board
  comprised of a majority of outside independent directors."
- The **April 13 meeting ran nine and a half hours** with thirteen directors present,
  including full presentations on the board's obligations under Delaware and federal
  law and on valuation. **No agenda or written materials were circulated in advance**,
  and no decision on the self-tender was made that day.
- Unocal's exchange offer was for the remaining 49% at **$72 per share in debt
  securities**, adopted at a second meeting after a detailed presentation, having
  considered a range of $70 to $80.
- The offer was conditioned on Mesa first acquiring 64 million shares (the **Mesa
  Purchase Condition**) and **excluded Mesa** from participating (the **Mesa
  exclusion**). The exclusion is the issue of first impression.
- Counsel advised that Mesa could be excluded only for what the directors reasonably
  believed to be a **valid corporate purpose**, and the board identified one: because
  the offer was prorated at 49%, every Mesa share accepted would displace another
  holder's, and including Mesa would mean Unocal financing Mesa's own inadequate bid.
- On April 22 the board **waived the Mesa Purchase Condition as to 50 million
  shares**, on advice responding to holders' concern that no shares would be bought by
  either offeror.
- Mesa had a **national reputation as a greenmailer**, and the trial court found the
  board acted in good faith to forestall greenmail. *The threat found was the coercive
  two-tier bid "coupled with the threat of greenmail."*
- The directors were advised to **tender their own shares** into the exchange offer.
  *Argued and rejected as a disqualifying interest — if the exclusion is valid, the
  directors share the same benefit as every other holder.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The board had the **power** to act. Authority comes from 8 Del. C. § 141(a) over
  the "business and affairs" of the corporation and from **8 Del. C. § 160(a)**,
  conferring "broad authority upon a corporation to deal in its own stock," and it is
  "well established that in the acquisition of its shares a Delaware corporation may
  **deal selectively** with its stockholders, provided the directors have not acted
  out of a sole or primary purpose to entrench themselves in office."
- The power also derives from "its fundamental duty and obligation to protect the
  corporate enterprise, which includes stockholders, from harm reasonably perceived,
  **irrespective of its source**." A board "is not a passive instrumentality."
- Because of "the omnipresent specter that a board may be acting primarily in its own
  interests, rather than those of the corporation and its shareholders, there is an
  **enhanced duty** which calls for judicial examination at the threshold before the
  protections of the business judgment rule may be conferred."
- **Prong one.** Directors "must show that they had reasonable grounds for believing
  that a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness existed because of another
  person's stock ownership," and "they satisfy that burden 'by showing good faith and
  reasonable investigation.'" Proof is materially enhanced by approval of a board with
  a majority of outside independent directors.
- **Prong two — the element of balance.** "If a defensive measure is to come within
  the ambit of the business judgment rule, it must be **reasonable in relation to the
  threat posed**." That requires an analysis of the nature of the bid and its effect on
  the corporate enterprise.
- The relevant concerns "may include: **inadequacy of the price offered, nature and
  timing of the offer, questions of illegality, the impact on 'constituencies' other
  than shareholders** (i.e., creditors, customers, employees, and perhaps even the
  community generally), **the risk of nonconsummation, and the quality of securities
  being offered** in the exchange." A board "may reasonably consider the basic
  stockholder interests at stake, including those of short term speculators."
- "A corporation does not have unbridled discretion to defeat any perceived threat by
  any **Draconian** means available," the restriction being that the directors "may not
  have acted solely or primarily out of a desire to perpetuate themselves in office,"
  with the further caveat that "inequitable action may not be taken under the guise of
  law."
- On these facts both purposes were valid: defeating an inadequate offer, and, if it
  succeeded anyway, giving the 49% who would otherwise take junk bonds $72 of senior
  debt. The exclusion was necessary to both, since including Mesa would subsidise
  Mesa's own $54 bid and Mesa "could not, by definition, fit within the class of
  shareholders being protected from its own coercive and inadequate tender offer."
- The selective exchange offer is "consistent with the principle that 'the minority
  stockholder shall receive the substantial equivalent in value of what he had
  before'" — a merger-context concept the court carries into tender offer law.
- Delaware corporate law "is not static. It must grow and develop in response to,
  indeed in anticipation of, evolving concepts and needs," and "[m]erely because the
  General Corporation Law is silent as to a specific matter does not mean that it is
  prohibited."
- Having satisfied both prongs, the board's action "is entitled to be measured by the
  standards of the business judgment rule," and a court will not substitute its
  judgment unless a preponderance of the evidence shows the decision was primarily
  based on perpetuating the directors in office or on another breach such as fraud,
  overreaching, lack of good faith, or being uninformed. **Reversed; preliminary
  injunction vacated.**

**Rejected**

- *A corporation may never discriminate against a single shareholder, so a selective
  exchange offer excluding the bidder is per se impermissible.* The blanket rule
  cannot stand. Selective repurchase is "neither unknown nor unauthorized" — the only
  difference from prior cases is that the approved transaction there was **greenmail**
  paid to a raider, which denied all other holders favoured treatment. "[G]iven Mesa's
  past history of greenmail, its claims here are rather ironic."
- *The business judgment rule cannot apply because the directors will benefit by
  tendering their own shares, a benefit not shared by all holders.* Mesa concedes that
  if the exclusion is valid the directors and all other stockholders share the same
  benefit. "The answer of course is that the exclusion is valid," and the directors'
  participation "does not rise to the level of a disqualifying interest."
- *The transaction is an interested-director transaction because several board members
  are large stockholders.* A substantial shareholding "alone does not create a
  disqualifying 'personal pecuniary interest'."
- *The exclusion lets the directors abdicate the fiduciary duties they owe Mesa.* The
  board continues to owe Mesa due care and loyalty, but "in the face of the destructive
  threat Mesa's tender offer was perceived to pose, the board had a **supervening
  duty** to protect the corporate enterprise, which includes the other shareholders."
- *The response is punitive, aimed solely at Mesa's exercise of its rights of corporate
  democracy.* Nothing precludes Mesa from acting in its own self-interest, but it acted
  in a way an independent-majority board reasonably found contrary to Unocal's
  interests. "[T]here is no support in Delaware law for the proposition that, when
  responding to a perceived harm, a corporation must guarantee a benefit to a
  stockholder who is deliberately provoking the danger being addressed. There is no
  obligation of self-sacrifice."
- *Directors should be passive in the face of a tender offer, leaving the decision to
  stockholders.* "[T]hat clearly is not the law of Delaware, and as the proponents of
  this rule of passivity readily concede, it has not been adopted either by courts or
  state legislatures."

## Context

Unocal is a **two-prong test assembled from one prior case and one new idea**.
[[Cheff v. Mathes]], assigned the same day, supplies prong one word for word —
reasonable grounds to believe a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness existed,
satisfied by good faith and reasonable investigation — along with the observation
about the inherent conflict when corporate funds are used to remove a threat to
control. Prong two, proportionality, is Unocal's own contribution, and it is what
converts a motive inquiry into a review of the measure itself.

The famous **list of cognisable threats** is dictum in the sense that only two were
found here, but it has done more work than the holding. Inadequacy of price, timing,
illegality, impact on other constituencies, risk of non-consummation, and the quality
of the securities offered — a list that broad is why the first prong is so rarely
where a defence fails. Compare the constituency point with
[[Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes]], decided the following year, which cuts it off at
the moment of sale: other constituencies may be considered only where rationally
related benefits accrue to stockholders, and not at all during an active auction.

What Unocal does **not** yet contain is the draconian screen. Unitrin later converts
prong two into a sequence — first ask whether the measure is preclusive or coercive,
and only if it is neither reach the range of reasonableness — and
[[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] applies that structure to deal protections. Read
Unocal for the burden and the balance; read Unitrin through Omnicare for the modern
mechanics.

The exclusion at the heart of the case has since been legislated away in practice.
The SEC's all-holders rule under Rule 14d-10 now requires a self-tender to be open to
all holders of the class, so a Mesa-style exclusion could not be structured today.
The doctrine outlived the device, which is the ordinary pattern in this part of the
course — [[Moran v. Household International]], decided months later, gave boards the
poison pill and made discriminatory self-tenders unnecessary.

The line from Unocal runs forward through three cases in this chunk.
[[Moran v. Household International]] applies it to a pill adopted **before** any
threat exists. [[Paramount v. Time]] expands the notion of threat to include
**substantive coercion** — that stockholders might tender "in ignorance or a mistaken
belief" about a strategic plan — and rejects any rule that an all-cash all-shares
offer cannot be a threat. [[Air Products v. Airgas]] then takes that expansion to its
end point, upholding a pill against a non-coercive, all-cash, fully financed, fully
informed offer because inadequate price alone counts.

Note also the procedural posture running through all of these. Unocal, like Revlon
and QVC, is a **preliminary injunction** case. The standard exists to give Chancery a
tool to use in real time, which is exactly what
[[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]] says when it holds that a fully informed vote
washes out a post-closing damages claim.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Interlocutory appeal from a preliminary injunction issued by the Court of Chancery
  against Unocal's exchange offer.
- Chancery held that a selective exchange offer excluding Mesa was **legally
  impermissible as such** — a blanket rule the Supreme Court refuses to adopt.
- Reversed; the injunction vacated.
- The posture explains the structure of the opinion. Because the question is whether
  the board had the **power** to do this at all, the court begins with authority
  before touching fairness or business judgment: "Absent such authority, all other
  questions are moot."

### Facts

T. Boone Pickens, Jr. controlled Mesa Petroleum, which by April 1985 owned about 13%
of Unocal. On April 8 Mesa launched a two-tier front-loaded cash tender offer for 64
million shares — roughly 37% of Unocal — at $54 per share, with the back end to
eliminate the remaining public shares through an exchange of securities purportedly
worth $54. A federal district court in California then ordered Mesa to issue a
supplemental proxy statement, which disclosed that the back-end securities would be
highly subordinated and that Unocal's capitalisation would differ significantly from
its existing structure. Unocal called them junk bonds.

Unocal's board consisted of eight independent outside directors and six insiders. It
met on April 13 with thirteen directors present, for nine and a half hours, with no
agenda or written materials circulated beforehand. Counsel presented at length on the
board's obligations under Delaware corporate law and the federal securities laws.
Peter Sachs then presented for Goldman Sachs and Dillon Read, opining that the Mesa
proposal was wholly inadequate and that the minimum cash value obtainable from a sale
or orderly liquidation of 100% of Unocal exceeded $60 per share. The Court of
Chancery found the Sachs presentation was designed to show the directors the scope of
the analyses performed rather than the underlying facts and figures.

Sachs also laid out defensive options, including a self-tender in the range of $70 to
$75, which would add $6.1 to $6.5 billion of debt. The directors were told the main
effect would be to reduce exploratory drilling but that the company would remain
viable. The eight outside directors then met separately with the advisors and
lawyers, and unanimously recommended that the board reject the Mesa offer as
inadequate and pursue a self-tender as a fairly priced alternative. The board
reconvened and unanimously rejected the Mesa offer as grossly inadequate. Despite the
length of the meeting, it made no decision that day on the self-tender.

The board met again on April 15 for two hours, four directors by telephone and one
absent. After a detailed presentation on terms, it settled on $72 within a considered
range of $70 to $80, heard about the debt securities and the restrictive covenants
that would be required, and unanimously approved the exchange offer. The resolution
provided that if Mesa acquired its 64 million shares, Unocal would buy the remaining
49% for debt securities with an aggregate par value of $72 per share, subject to
further conditions including the exclusion of Mesa.

The offer commenced April 17 and Mesa sued immediately. On April 22 the board, on
advice from Goldman Sachs and Dillon Read, waived the Mesa Purchase Condition as to
50 million shares, responding to holders' concern that if they tendered to Unocal
neither offeror would end up buying. The directors were also advised to tender their
own shares as a mark of confidence. Counsel advised that Mesa could be excluded only
for a reasonably believed valid corporate purpose, and the board's discussion centred
on compensating the back-end holders: because the exchange offer was prorated at 49%,
every Mesa share accepted would displace another holder's, and letting Mesa tender
would mean Unocal financing Mesa's own $54 bid.

### Issue

- Whether a Delaware board has the power and the duty to oppose a takeover bid it
  reasonably perceives to be harmful to the corporate enterprise.
- If so, by what standard that opposition is reviewed before the business judgment
  rule attaches.
- Whether a self-tender that excludes the hostile bidder from participating is
  permissible, or whether such discrimination is impermissible as a matter of law.
- Whether the directors' own participation in the exchange offer, or their
  shareholdings, disqualify them from the rule's protection.

### Holding

- **On power: the board had it.** Section 141(a), § 160(a) and the board's
  fundamental duty to protect the corporate enterprise from harm reasonably
  perceived, whatever its source, together supply the authority. A board is not a
  passive instrumentality.
- **On the standard: enhanced scrutiny, then business judgment.** Because of the
  omnipresent spectre of self-interest, directors bear the burden of showing
  reasonable grounds to believe a danger existed — met by good faith and reasonable
  investigation — and that the response was reasonable in relation to the threat
  posed. Only then does the business judgment rule attach.
- **On the exclusion: valid here, and no per se rule against it.** The threat was a
  grossly inadequate coercive two-tier offer coupled with a greenmail threat; the
  response was proportionate, because including Mesa would have subsidised its own
  bid and Mesa could not belong to the class being protected from that bid.
- **On the directors' interest: none disqualifying.** Since the exclusion is valid,
  the directors share the same benefit as all other holders, and large shareholdings
  alone create no personal pecuniary interest.
- **Disposition:** reversed; preliminary injunction vacated.

### Rule

- A board addressing a pending takeover bid has an obligation to determine whether
  the offer is in the best interests of the corporation and its shareholders.
- Because of the omnipresent spectre that the board may be acting primarily in its
  own interests, an enhanced duty calls for judicial examination at the threshold
  before the business judgment rule may be conferred.
- Directors must show reasonable grounds for believing a danger to corporate policy
  and effectiveness existed because of another person's stock ownership, satisfied by
  good faith and reasonable investigation.
- Proof is materially enhanced by the approval of a board comprised of a majority of
  outside independent directors.
- A defensive measure must be reasonable in relation to the threat posed, requiring
  analysis of the nature of the bid and its effect on the corporate enterprise.
- Cognisable concerns include inadequacy of price, nature and timing of the offer,
  questions of illegality, impact on non-shareholder constituencies, risk of
  non-consummation, and the quality of securities offered.
- A corporation has no unbridled discretion to defeat a perceived threat by any
  draconian means available, and directors may not act solely or primarily to
  perpetuate themselves in office.
- Inequitable action may not be taken under the guise of law.
- In acquiring its own shares a corporation may deal selectively with its
  stockholders, absent an entrenchment purpose.

### Reasoning

*(Moore, J.)*

- The opinion is built in the order **power, standard, application**, and says why:
  without authority to act at all, neither fairness nor business judgment is
  pertinent. That sequencing is what lets the court dispose of Chancery's per se rule
  without reaching whether this particular offer was fair to Mesa.
- Authority is stacked from three sources — § 141(a), § 160(a), and an inherent duty
  to protect the enterprise from harm "irrespective of its source." The third is the
  broadest and the one that does the work, because it covers harm originating with a
  *shareholder*, which is not obviously within the other two.
- The enhanced duty is justified structurally rather than morally. The court does not
  accuse the Unocal board of anything; it says the **category** of decision carries an
  inherent conflict, so the presumption must be earned rather than assumed.
- Prong one is then set at a level a careful board can meet — good faith and
  reasonable investigation — and made easier still by an independent majority. The
  practical consequence is that Unocal cases are decided at prong two.
- Proportionality is introduced as "the element of balance," and the list of
  cognisable threats is expansive by design, because the court is trying to describe
  an inquiry rather than to enumerate a closed set.
- On the exclusion, the reasoning is that the **remedy must fit the harm**. The threat
  was a coercive structure that would leave 49% holding junk bonds; the response
  guaranteed that 49% senior debt at $72; and the person creating that harm cannot be
  a member of the class protected from it.
- The passage on Delaware law not being static is doing real work, not decorating. The
  court is authorising a device the DGCL nowhere mentions, and its justification is
  that statutory silence is not prohibition — the same move that sustains the poison
  pill in Moran months later.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion's soft spot is that both prongs were satisfied on a record that would
look thin in a duty of care case decided the same year. There was no agenda and no
written materials before a nine-and-a-half hour meeting; the financial presentation
was designed to convey the scope of the analyses rather than the numbers; and the
$72 figure was chosen from a range at a two-hour follow-up meeting.
[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]], decided three months earlier, found gross negligence on a
comparable absence of materials. The distinction the court never articulates is that Van Gorkom
concerned a decision to sell the company while Unocal concerned a decision to defend
it — but that distinction is doing a lot of unspoken work.

### Cold-call notes

- **Recite both prongs and who bears the burden.** Reasonable grounds to believe a
  danger existed, satisfied by good faith and reasonable investigation; and a response
  reasonable in relation to the threat. The **directors** carry both. That burden
  allocation is the point of enhanced scrutiny.
- **Say where prong one comes from.** Cheff, verbatim, assigned the same day. What
  Unocal adds is prong two. If asked what is new in Unocal, the one-word answer is
  proportionality.
- **Know the threat list.** Inadequacy of price, nature and timing, illegality, impact
  on other constituencies, risk of non-consummation, quality of the securities
  offered. Six items, and the breadth is why defences almost never fail at prong one.
- **Be ready to explain why the exclusion was upheld.** The offer was prorated at 49%,
  so every Mesa share accepted displaces another holder's, and Unocal would be
  financing the very bid it was defending against. Then add the conceptual half: Mesa
  "could not, by definition, fit within the class of shareholders being protected from
  its own coercive and inadequate tender offer."
- **Do not say a discriminatory self-tender is available today.** The SEC's all-holders
  rule, Rule 14d-10, requires a self-tender to be open to all holders of the class.
  The doctrine survives; the device does not.
- **Have the independent-majority point ready.** Proof of good faith and reasonable
  investigation is "materially enhanced" by approval from a board with a majority of
  outside directors, and here those eight met separately before recommending. That is
  the single most transferable practice lesson in the case.
- **Expect the Van Gorkom comparison.** No agenda, no written materials, a
  presentation about the scope of the analysis rather than the numbers. The honest
  answer is that the court is reviewing a decision to *defend*, not a decision to
  *sell*, and that Unocal's burden is reasonableness rather than gross negligence.
- **Numbers worth holding.** 13% stake; $54 two-tier for 64 million shares, about 37%;
  advisors' floor above $60; $72 exchange offer for the remaining 49%; $6.1–6.5
  billion of new debt; eight outside directors of fourteen; a nine-and-a-half hour
  meeting.
