## Rule

A creditor with derivative standing may challenge **specific transfers of value** from an
insolvent corporation to its controller under **entire fairness** — but may **not** rebut the
business judgment rule as to **ordinary business strategy** merely by alleging that directors own
equity or are dual fiduciaries for the sole stockholder, because "when directors make decisions
that appear rationally designed to increase the value of the firm as a whole, Delaware courts do
not speculate about whether those decisions might benefit some residual claimants more than
others."

## Facts

- **EBF owned 100% of the equity and all of the Junior Notes**, and the board had the ability to
  **defer interest** on those notes but chose not to. In an orderly liquidation the Junior Notes
  "would not receive anything." That combination — a discretionary payment, to the controller,
  on an instrument otherwise worth nothing — is the paradigm specific transfer.
- **A conscious decision not to act is a decision.** "From a semantic and even legal viewpoint,
  'inaction' and 'action' may be substantive equivalents, different only in form."
- The Company paid **service and licence fees to ASIA**, an entity owned by EBF, alleged to
  **exceed market rates**. Same structure, same analysis.
- **The risk-on strategy conferred no direct or specific benefit.** Amending the Operating
  Guidelines to permit riskier investments "affected the value of the entity as a whole." *Argued
  and rejected as a basis for entire fairness.*
- **Insolvency was adequately pleaded on the balance sheet test.** The Company started with $100
  million of equity capital, borrowed six times that in long-term debt, and leveraged its equity
  another **500 times** writing credit default swaps. A **$48 million** payment in 2008 wiped out
  half its equity capital; a **$320 million** payment in 2010 exceeded six times what remained.
  By the September 2011 financials it had **$600 million of bond debt against assets with a fair
  saleable value of $426 million**.
- **The defendants' own brief made the insolvency case worse**, disputing the complaint by
  asserting that liabilities were actually **$747 million** — $147 million more than alleged.
- The Company and its subsidiary **lost their AAA/Aaa ratings** by the end of 2008, entered
  runoff, and by August 2010 had no investment grade debt or counterparty credit ratings at all,
  with "no realistic prospect of returning to solvency."
- **Three of five directors could not invoke § 102(b)(7)** because the complaint pleaded that they
  were not independent of EBF — Vertin an EBF partner whose compensation was tied to EBF's
  investments in credit derivative product companies, and Sullivan an EBF employee.
- The charters of both entities **required the businesses to be conducted in compliance with the
  Operating Guidelines**, which the ratings agencies had required. *Background — the amendment of
  those Guidelines is the challenged strategy.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Standard of conduct.** "[T]he standard of conduct for directors requires that they strive in
  good faith and on an informed basis to maximize the value of the corporation for the benefit of
  its **residual claimants**." In a solvent corporation those are the stockholders; on insolvency
  "its creditors take the place of the shareholders as the residual beneficiaries of any increase
  in value," and creditors **join** the class of residual claimants while stockholders remain in
  it behind them.
- **Standing.** "Under Gheewalla, Quadrant gains standing to bring a derivative claim by pleading
  that the Company is insolvent," provable by either the **balance sheet** test — "liabilities in
  excess of a reasonable market value of assets held" — or the **cash flow** test.
- **Three tiers of review.** Which applies depends initially on whether the directors "(i) were
  disinterested and independent (the business judgment rule), (ii) faced potential conflicts of
  interest because of the decisional dynamics present in particular recurring and recognizable
  situations (enhanced scrutiny), or (iii) confronted **actual conflicts** of interest such that
  the directors making the decision did not comprise a disinterested and independent board
  majority (**entire fairness**)."
- **Solvent versus insolvent transfers to a 100% stockholder.** "A transfer of value from a
  **solvent** subsidiary to the holder of 100% of the equity **cannot give rise to a fiduciary
  wrong**," because the sole residual claimant "is in the same position before and after."
  But on insolvency "a transfer of value to the sole stockholder does not inure to the ratable
  benefit of all of the residual claimants. The payment now **transfers value previously owned
  beneficially and indirectly by all of the residual claimants to the party in control**."
- The **interest payments** and the **excessive fees** therefore state derivative claims, reviewed
  under **entire fairness with the burden on the defendants**, because EBF "stood on both sides."
- **The risk-on strategy claim fails.** "Current Delaware law does not require the Board to shut
  down Athilon's business and manage towards a near-term dissolution for the benefit of creditors."
  Even when insolvent, "directors are free to pursue value maximizing strategies," and a board
  acting "with due diligence and good faith... does not become a guarantor of that strategy's
  success" — it "**are protected by the business judgment rule**."
- **Efficient liquidation is equally protected.** "[T]he efficient liquidation of an insolvent firm
  might well be the method by which the firm's value is enhanced," and "[h]ere too the business
  judgment rule would protect a board's decision to pursue an efficient liquidation."
- **The "reverse-Trados" theory is sound in principle and fails in application.** In
  [[In re Trados]] the dual fiduciary problem arose because directors' affiliated entities held
  preferred with special rights diverging from the common. Here, insolvency makes creditors the
  primary residual claimants, so directors loyal to the equity face the mirror conflict. "The fault
  in this reasoning lies **not in the theory, but in its application** to business decisions that
  **generally affect the value of the entity as a whole** and which do not confer **specific
  benefits** on the directors themselves or... on the competing beneficiaries of fiduciary duties."
- **Equal treatment is a presumptive safe harbour**, drawn from [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]]: a
  dividend on a parent-owned class only "might well be self-dealing," but where "a proportionate
  share of this money was received by the minority shareholders" and the parent "received nothing
  ... to the exclusion of its minority stockholders," the business judgment standard applies.
- **Equal treatment is not absolute.** A fiduciary's personal motives can produce a loyalty breach
  through bad faith, shown "where the fiduciary intentionally acts with a purpose other than that
  of advancing the best interests of the corporation." Quoting El Paso: "Greed is not the only
  human emotion that can pull one from the path of propriety; so might hatred, lust, envy, revenge,
  ... shame or pride." But "[i]t is not enough... simply to argue in the abstract that a particular
  director has a conflict of interest or is acting in bad faith because she is affiliated with a
  particular type of institution."
- **Holding otherwise would contradict Gheewalla.** If a creditor could establish a lack of
  independence merely by alleging that equity-holding or equity-affiliated directors adopted a
  risky strategy, "the directors of an insolvent corporation would face precisely the same type of
  fiduciary conflict that Gheewalla sought to avoid."
- **The traditional irrationality argument also fails.** The complaint "does not support an
  inference that no rational person would take on additional risk," since "a riskier strategy could
  return greater value for the Company and **all of its residual claimants, including its
  creditors**."
- **§ 102(b)(7) applies to creditor derivative claims.** "When creditors assert derivative claims
  for breach of fiduciary duty, they are seeking to impose **personal liability on directors**, so
  Section 102(b)(7) potentially applies." Three directors cannot invoke it because the complaint
  pleads a lack of independence from an interested controller — established by "pleading facts
  showing that directors depend on an interested controller for their income or employment."
- Motion to dismiss **denied** as to the interest non-deferral and the excessive fees; **granted**
  as to the business strategy claim.

**Rejected**

- *Because the Company is insolvent and the directors are dual fiduciaries for the sole equity
  holder, a decision to pursue a riskier strategy that favours the equity draws entire fairness.*
  The theory is right and the application is wrong: it applies to transactions conferring direct
  and specific benefits, not to decisions affecting the firm as a whole. "[T]he Board does not face
  a conflict between the interests of the primary residual claimants (the creditors) and the
  interests of secondary residual claimants (the stockholders)" on a decision of that kind.
- *An insolvent company's board must stop taking risk and manage toward dissolution for the
  creditors' benefit — a variant of the trust fund doctrine.* Not the law. Directors "continue to
  have the task of attempting to maximize the economic value of the firm," may "take action that
  might, if it does not pan out, result in the firm being painted in a deeper hue of red," and
  there is no "magic dividing line that should signal the end to some, most, or all risk-taking."
- *Directors' affiliation with the controller, or the controller's general benefit from continued
  operations, rebuts the business judgment rule.* Shandler rejected exactly that: "[e]ven when [the
  entity] was insolvent, the board was entitled to exercise a good faith business judgment to
  continue to operate the business if it believed that was what would maximize [its] value."
- *Material outside the complaint defeats the pleading-stage inference of insolvency.* Considering
  it "would require converting the motion to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment. They have
  not been considered."

## Context

Quadrant is the operating manual for [[NACEPF v. Gheewalla]], and the pairing is the point of the
session. Gheewalla says creditors of an insolvent corporation may sue **derivatively** and never
directly; Quadrant says what they can actually win, and the answer is a **sorting rule**:

| Challenged decision | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Specific transfers** of value to the controller — interest on notes the controller holds, above-market fees to a controller affiliate | **Entire fairness**, burden on defendants |
| **Firm-wide business decisions** — take more risk, keep operating, liquidate efficiently | **Business judgment rule** |

The reason for the line is the same one Gheewalla gave for refusing direct claims. If dual-fiduciary
status plus an insolvent balance sheet were enough to make every strategic choice an entire fairness
case, directors would face exactly the conflict Gheewalla was built to prevent — paralysed between
creditors who want the downside protected and stockholders who want the upside pursued.

The **"reverse-Trados"** framing is the most useful thing in the opinion for exam purposes.
[[In re Trados]]: solvent company, directors loyal to **preferred** holders whose contract rights
diverge from the **common** residual. Quadrant: insolvent company, directors loyal to the **equity**
whose interests diverge from the **creditors** who are now the primary residual claimants. Same
structure, mirrored. And the same limit applies in both directions — Trados condemned the MIP and the
merger because they conferred specific benefits, not because the board preferred one constituency in
the abstract.

The [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]] safe harbour is the older Delaware version of the same sorting rule.
A parent that takes something "to the exclusion of and detrimental to its minority stockholders"
self-deals; a parent that receives only "a proportionate share" does not. Equal treatment moves the
case into business judgment, subject to the bad faith exception that El Paso describes so vividly.

Three practical points to carry.

**Inaction is action.** The board's decision **not** to defer interest was reviewed like an
affirmative transfer. Boards do not escape scrutiny by leaving a value-shifting default in place.

**§ 102(b)(7) reaches creditor derivative claims**, so these suits must be pleaded as loyalty or bad
faith. Here three directors lost the defence at the pleading stage because they depended on the
controller "for their income or employment" — the ordinary
[[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test|independence]] analysis, applied to creditors'
claims.

**The trust fund doctrine is dead.** Directors of an insolvent firm may keep taking risk, and may
equally choose efficient liquidation. Both are protected. What is not protected is paying the
controller on the way through.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Motion to dismiss under Court of Chancery Rule 12(b)(6), where "the pleading standards... are
  minimal."
- Quadrant, a holder of Athilon debt securities, asserted **derivative** fiduciary claims against
  the board and against EBF, plus **direct** fraudulent transfer claims against EBF and ASIA.
- Denied as to two theories, granted as to the third.
- The posture matters because the whole case turns on **characterisation** rather than on evidence.
  Three challenged decisions, identical pleading standard, and two survive while one does not —
  which makes this the cleanest available illustration of where the line falls.

### Facts

Athilon Capital Corp. was a credit derivative product company created to sell credit protection to
large financial institutions. Its wholly owned subsidiary, Asset Acceptance, wrote credit default
swaps on senior tranches of collateralised debt obligations, and Athilon guaranteed them. In a
typical transaction Asset Acceptance sold protection to a bank referencing a designated pool of
investment grade securities; if the pool's net losses exceeded a contractual figure, Asset Acceptance
was liable up to a fixed limit and Athilon was liable as guarantor.

The business model depended on a AAA/Aaa rating, and to obtain and keep it the ratings agencies
required a limited business purpose and adherence to Operating Guidelines. Both charters restricted
the businesses accordingly and required them to be conducted in compliance with those Guidelines.

The capital structure was extraordinarily thin. The Company started with $100 million of equity
capital, borrowed roughly six times that in long-term debt, and then leveraged its equity capital
another 500 times writing swaps. A $48 million payment in 2008 to unwind an unsuccessful mortgage-
backed swap wiped out half the equity capital; a $320 million payment in 2010 exceeded six times
what remained. By the end of 2008 both entities had lost their AAA/Aaa ratings and entered runoff,
and by August 2010 neither had any investment grade debt or counterparty credit ratings. The
September 2011 financials showed $600 million of outstanding bond debt against assets with a fair
saleable value of $426 million; the defendants' reply brief asserted the liabilities were actually
$747 million.

EBF & Associates took over the insolvent company, acquiring 100% of the equity and all of the Junior
Notes. Vertin was an EBF partner whose compensation was tied to the performance of EBF's investments
in credit derivative product companies; Sullivan was an EBF employee; Gonzalez was also alleged to
lack independence.

Quadrant's complaint identified three decisions. First, the board could defer interest on the Junior
Notes and did not, even though those notes would receive nothing in an orderly liquidation, so the
payments flowed to EBF. Second, the Company paid service and licence fees to ASIA, an EBF affiliate,
allegedly above market rates. Third, the board amended the Operating Guidelines to permit investment
in riskier securities and speculative investments — a strategy under which, Quadrant alleged, EBF
would enjoy the upside as holder of the equity and the Junior Notes while the more senior creditors
bore the downside.

### Issue

- Whether Quadrant has standing, which turns on whether the complaint adequately pleads insolvency.
- Whether the decision not to defer interest on notes held by the sole stockholder, and the payment
  of above-market fees to its affiliate, state derivative fiduciary claims and under what standard.
- Whether a board's decision to adopt a riskier business strategy at an insolvent company draws
  entire fairness because the directors are dual fiduciaries for the sole equity holder.
- Whether § 102(b)(7) exculpates the directors from creditor derivative claims.

### Holding

- **On standing: adequately pleaded.** The balance sheet test is satisfied by the leverage history,
  the unwind payments, the loss of ratings, and $600 million of debt against $426 million of assets —
  a figure the defendants' own brief put higher still.
- **On the interest and the fees: entire fairness, claims survive.** EBF stood on both sides, the
  payments transferred value previously owned indirectly by all residual claimants to the party in
  control, and a conscious decision not to defer is as reviewable as an affirmative payment.
- **On the business strategy: business judgment rule, claim dismissed.** The decision affected the
  firm as a whole and conferred no direct or specific benefit, and neither dual-fiduciary status nor
  the controller's general benefit rebuts the presumption. Nor is the strategy irrational — greater
  risk could return greater value for all residual claimants including the creditors.
- **On § 102(b)(7): partially unavailable.** Three directors cannot invoke it at the pleading stage
  because the complaint pleads that they depend on an interested controller for income or employment;
  as to the other two, the court cannot yet determine entitlement.

### Rule

- Directors must strive in good faith and on an informed basis to maximise the value of the
  corporation for the benefit of its residual claimants.
- On insolvency creditors join the class of residual claimants and gain standing to pursue derivative
  claims; insolvency may be pleaded under the balance sheet test or the cash flow test.
- Delaware has three tiers of review — the business judgment rule, enhanced scrutiny, and entire
  fairness — selected initially by whether the directors were disinterested and independent, faced
  potential conflicts in recurring situations, or confronted actual conflicts.
- A transfer of value from a **solvent** subsidiary to a 100% stockholder cannot be a fiduciary wrong;
  the same transfer from an **insolvent** subsidiary transfers value from all residual claimants to
  the controller and draws entire fairness.
- A conscious decision not to act is as much a decision as a decision to act.
- Even when insolvent, directors may pursue value-maximising strategies and do not become guarantors
  of their success; efficient liquidation is equally protected by the business judgment rule.
- Dual-fiduciary status does not rebut the business judgment rule as to decisions that generally
  affect the value of the entity as a whole and confer no direct or specific benefits.
- Equal treatment of stockholders operates as a presumptive but not absolute safe harbour, subject to
  a bad faith exception requiring specific allegations rather than abstract assertions of conflict.
- Section 102(b)(7) applies to creditor derivative claims, since they seek to impose personal
  liability on directors.

### Reasoning

*(Laster, V.C.)*

- The opinion is organised around a single question asked three times — **does this decision confer a
  direct and specific benefit on the controller?** — and the three answers produce the sorting rule
  that is the case's contribution.
- The solvent/insolvent comparison is the analytical core, and it is argued from ownership rather than
  from fairness. Where one person owns everything, moving value to him changes nothing; where
  creditors have joined the residual class, the same movement takes from them and gives to him.
- The treatment of **inaction** is short but consequential. Characterising a failure to defer as
  equivalent to a payment closes an obvious route around the doctrine, since most value-shifting at a
  distressed company happens by letting existing arrangements run.
- The "reverse-Trados" analysis is generous to the plaintiff before it defeats him. Laster accepts the
  theory outright — dual fiduciaries for the equity of an insolvent company face a real conflict — and
  then locates the failure in **scope**, which is a far more durable holding than a denial would have
  been.
- Three authorities are marshalled for the limit, and each is addressed on what the court **did** rather
  than what it said: Shandler dismissed a reckless-gamble claim despite a controller-dominated board;
  Production Resources treated continued operation as an exculpated care claim while letting specific
  transfers through; Trenwick "spoke consistently in terms of the business judgment rule" despite
  pleaded dual-fiduciary status.
- Sinclair supplies the older and more familiar version of the same idea, and the court uses it to show
  that Delaware has long distinguished differential from proportionate treatment.
- The closing argument is structural. A contrary rule would recreate "precisely the same type of
  fiduciary conflict that Gheewalla sought to avoid," which is what makes the limit a matter of
  doctrine rather than of pleading tolerance.
- The § 102(b)(7) analysis is the practical sting. Establishing that directors "depend on an interested
  controller for their income or employment" both rebuts independence for the standard of review and
  strips the exculpation defence, so the same facts do double duty.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The line the opinion draws is administrable but does real work for controllers. A risk-on strategy at
an insolvent company is, economically, a transfer from creditors to equity — the equity holds an
option and the creditors hold the downside — and the court's answer is not that this is untrue but
that courts "do not speculate about whether those decisions might benefit some residual claimants more
than others." That is a choice in favour of director discretion and against precision, justified by
Gheewalla's concern for paralysis. The safety valve is the bad faith route, which requires "specific
allegations and later, actual evidence sufficient to permit a finding that the director faced a
conflict or acted with an improper purpose on the facts of the case" — a demanding standard on facts
where the harm is diffuse by design.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the sorting rule.** Specific transfers to the controller → entire fairness. Firm-wide
  business decisions → business judgment rule. Then say why: Gheewalla's concern that directors of an
  insolvent company not be paralysed between constituencies.
- **Use "reverse-Trados" by name.** Trados is a solvent company with directors loyal to the preferred;
  Quadrant is an insolvent company with directors loyal to the equity. The theory is accepted; only its
  application to firm-wide decisions is rejected.
- **Know both insolvency tests and which one was used.** Balance sheet — liabilities exceeding a
  reasonable market value of assets — pleaded here through $600 million of debt against $426 million of
  assets. Cash flow is the alternative.
- **Explain the solvent/insolvent asymmetry in one sentence.** A transfer to a 100% stockholder of a
  **solvent** company harms nobody, because he owned the value already; the same transfer from an
  **insolvent** company takes value that now belongs beneficially to the creditors.
- **Have the inaction point ready.** Not deferring interest was treated as a decision. "[I]naction' and
  'action' may be substantive equivalents, different only in form."
- **Say that the trust fund doctrine is not the law.** Directors may keep taking risk, may operate at a
  loss, and may choose efficient liquidation — all protected. They "do not become a guarantor" of a
  strategy's success.
- **Know the Sinclair safe harbour and its limit.** Proportionate treatment is presumptively fine;
  differential treatment is self-dealing; and bad faith can defeat even equal treatment, but only on
  specific allegations, not on abstractions about the kind of institution a director is affiliated with.
- **Do not forget § 102(b)(7).** It reaches creditor derivative claims. Directors who depend on an
  interested controller for income or employment lose both their independence and the defence.
- **Numbers worth holding.** $100 million of equity capital; six times that in long-term debt; 500x
  leverage writing swaps; $48 million in 2008 and $320 million in 2010 to unwind two swaps; $600
  million of debt against $426 million of assets, or $747 million on the defendants' own account;
  three of five directors non-independent.
