## Rule

A disclosure-only class settlement will be approved only where the supplemental
disclosures address a **plainly material** misrepresentation or omission and the
release is narrowly confined to disclosure and sale-process claims that have been
sufficiently investigated; supplemental information that merely adds detail to an
already fair summary is not consideration for a release.

## Facts

- The only thing the class received was supplemental disclosure. The settlement
  provided Trulia stockholders no economic benefit, and "[t]he only money that
  would change hands is the payment of a fee to plaintiffs' counsel" — $375,000,
  unopposed by defendants.
- The four supplemental disclosures all concerned J.P. Morgan's financial
  analysis: synergy figures in the value creation analysis, selected comparable
  transaction multiples, selected public trading multiples, and implied terminal
  EBITDA multiples in a relative discounted cash flow analysis.
- The Proxy already ran 224 pages excluding annexes and summarised the J.P.
  Morgan opinion in ten single-spaced pages. The adequacy of what was already
  disclosed is what makes the supplement worthless.
- The record was thin: no motions were decided, not even a motion to expedite;
  fewer than 3,000 pages produced; three depositions, two before the
  agreement-in-principle and one "confirmatory" afterwards.
- The preliminary injunction brief pleaded price, process and preclusive deal
  terms, but argued only disclosure. The other claims were released without ever
  being litigated.
- The settlement was struck on November 19, 2014 — five days after the injunction
  brief and two days after the definitive proxy — and the stockholders then
  approved the merger on December 18 with 99.15% of voting shares in favour.
- No stockholder objected and the defendants filed nothing before the fairness
  hearing. The proceeding had no adversary in it, which is the structural problem
  the opinion is written to address.
- Professor Sean Griffith appeared as amicus curiae by leave of the court, which
  is how an opposing view entered the record at all.
- Trulia was acquired by Zillow for approximately $3.5 billion in stock, with
  former Trulia and Zillow holders taking roughly 33% and 67% of the combined
  company. *Background — the merits of the exchange ratio are never reached.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Rule 23 requires the Court of Chancery to exercise **independent judgment** on
  the fairness of a class settlement, because the settlement binds absent class
  members. "Approval of a class action settlement requires more than a cursory
  scrutiny by the court of the issues presented."
- The court weighs "the reasonableness of the 'give' and the 'get'" — what the
  class surrenders against what the class receives.
- None of the four supplemental disclosures was material or even helpful. Under
  the Delaware standard, borrowed from the federal securities laws, information is
  material only if there is a substantial likelihood a reasonable stockholder
  would consider it important in deciding how to vote, such that it significantly
  alters the total mix.
- Where a board relies on a financial advisor's advice in recommending stockholder
  action, stockholders are entitled to a **fair summary** of the substantive work
  performed. A fair summary is a summary: it need not include everything
  underlying the opinion, and need not supply enough data for stockholders to
  perform their own valuation. "The essence of a fair summary is not a cornucopia
  of financial data, but rather an accurate description of the advisor's
  methodology and key assumptions."
- The Proxy already provided a more-than-fair summary in each of the four
  respects criticised, so the "get" did not warrant the "give" of a release.
  Approval denied.
- Disclosure claims in deal litigation should **optimally be adjudicated outside
  the settlement context**, in an adversarial process, so the court's view of the
  merits is not entangled with the defendants' desire for a broad release.
- Two such settings exist. On a preliminary injunction motion the adversarial
  posture survives and the plaintiff bears the burden of showing a reasonable
  likelihood of proving materiality. On a **mootness fee** application, made after
  defendants voluntarily supplement the proxy, defendants are motivated to oppose
  an excessive fee and the adversarial process again assists the court.
- Going forward, disclosure settlements are "likely to be met with continued
  disfavor... unless the supplemental disclosures address a **plainly material**
  misrepresentation or omission, and the subject matter of the proposed release is
  narrowly circumscribed" to disclosure and sale-process claims that have been
  sufficiently investigated. Plainly material means it should not be a close call.
- Where supplemental information is not plainly material, appointing an **amicus
  curiae** may be appropriate to supply the missing adversary.
- Surviving fiduciary claims are to be tested at the pleading stage with "special
  care," because the risk of strike suits means too much turns on the mere
  survival of a complaint, and thirty years of Unocal and Revlon jurisprudence now
  equips both the litigants and the court to do that.

**Rejected**

- *Delaware has long favoured the voluntary settlement of litigation, and the
  parties, the class and the defendants all support this one.* The fiduciary
  character of a class action displaces that preference; the court must
  independently examine fairness before approving, and unanimity among the
  participants is a symptom of the problem rather than evidence of fairness.
- *Supplemental disclosure makes stockholders better informed and is therefore a
  benefit.* Empirical work cited in the opinion suggests supplemental disclosures
  make no difference in stockholder voting, and disclosures that provide
  extraneous detail do not contribute to a fair summary and add no value.
- *Heightened scrutiny will simply drive plaintiffs to more hospitable forums.* A
  Delaware corporation may enact a **forum selection bylaw** to address that, and
  the court expresses the hope and trust that its sister courts will reach the
  same conclusion when the issue reaches them.

## Context

Trulia is the procedural bookend to [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]], assigned the
same day. Omnicare shows deal litigation working: a real claim, litigated on an
expedited basis, producing an injunction that moved more than a billion dollars of
consideration. Trulia shows the same machinery running empty — the same expedited
posture, the same threat of injunction, and a settlement that transfers nothing to
anyone except plaintiffs' counsel. Reading the two together is the point of the
session.

The diagnosis is an incentive problem, not a bad-faith problem. Chancellor Allen's
line from Solomon v. Pathe supplies the frame: surviving a motion to dismiss means
"economical[ly] rational defendants... will settle such claims, often for a
peppercorn and a fee." Defendants buy closing certainty and deal insurance in the
form of a broad release; plaintiffs' counsel get a fee; supplemental disclosure is
a costless currency; and once the agreement in principle is struck, both sides of
the caption want the same outcome at the fairness hearing. Nobody in the room is
arguing against approval, and the court is asked to evaluate a bargain on a record
built by people who agree.

The numbers in the opinion are the argument. Transactions over $100 million
drawing stockholder litigation rose from 39.3% in 2005 to 94.9% in 2014, falling
to 87.7% in 2015. In Delaware, cases settled solely on supplemental disclosure
went from 45.4% in 2005 to 76.0% in 2012. Rural/Metro is the cautionary example:
the court called it a "very close call" to reject a disclosure settlement whose
release would have extinguished claims that later produced over $100 million for
stockholders, most of it from a post-trial judgment after new counsel took over —
the same [[RBC Capital Markets v. Jervis|Rural/Metro]] litigation assigned in
session 16, which also supplies the aiding-and-abetting standard quoted in the
pages immediately preceding this opinion.

What happened next is worth knowing. Disclosure settlements largely left Delaware
after Trulia, and the Seventh Circuit adopted the same reasoning within months in
the Walgreen litigation. The practice migrated to **mootness fee** applications and
to federal court under § 14(a), which is precisely the second channel Bouchard
identified as adversarial and therefore acceptable. The doctrine did not eliminate
the claims; the doctrine relocated them to a forum where somebody argues the other
side.

The materiality standard here is the ordinary Delaware disclosure standard, which
recurs throughout the course. It is the same standard that decides whether a
stockholder vote was "fully informed" for [[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]]
cleansing and whether the minority vote was informed under
[[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide]], both assigned the following day. A disclosure deficient
enough to defeat Corwin is by definition plainly material, so a single materiality
analysis does work in three places.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Application under Court of Chancery Rule 23 for approval of a proposed class
  settlement, heard by Bouchard, C. after notice to the class.
- Four essentially identical complaints were filed within days of the merger
  announcement and consolidated; the parties reached an agreement in principle
  less than four months later.
- The posture is everything in this case. A settlement hearing is
  **non-adversarial** by construction, and the opinion is largely a response to
  that fact.

### Facts

Zillow and Trulia announced a definitive merger agreement on July 28, 2014, under
which Zillow would acquire Trulia for roughly $3.5 billion in stock through two
successive stock-for-stock mergers into subsidiaries of a new holding company,
leaving former Trulia and Zillow stockholders with approximately 33% and 67% of
Holdco. Four Trulia stockholders filed materially identical breach-of-fiduciary
complaints alleging an unfair exchange ratio.

Holdco filed the preliminary joint proxy on September 11. One plaintiff moved to
expedite and for a preliminary injunction on September 24. Plaintiffs took limited
discovery, deposing Trulia's chairman and chief executive Pete Flint and a banker
from J.P. Morgan, Trulia's financial advisor. Their November 14 injunction brief
asserted claims about the exchange ratio, the single-bidder process, the valuation
and preclusive merger terms, but argued only the disclosure claims, offering no
support for anything else.

The definitive joint proxy was filed November 17. Two days later the parties
signed a memorandum of understanding settling for supplemental disclosures, and
Trulia filed an 8-K containing them the same day. Stockholders approved the merger
on December 18, with 99.15% of shares voted and 79.52% of all outstanding shares
in favour. A confirmatory deposition of a second director followed on February 10,
2015, and the transaction closed a week later.

The stipulation was executed in June 2015 and provided for a fee application not
to exceed $375,000, which defendants agreed not to oppose. Notice went to the
class in July, and Bouchard held a fairness hearing on September 16. Defendants
filed nothing beforehand and no stockholder objected. Professor Sean Griffith of
Fordham, who had researched and objected to disclosure settlements before, sought
leave to appear as amicus on September 22 and was permitted to brief the questions
the court had raised. Plaintiffs answered with a valuation expert's affidavit, and
the parties agreed to a narrower release after the hearing.

### Issue

- Whether the four supplemental disclosures were material, or of any benefit at
  all, to Trulia's stockholders.
- Whether the "get" of those disclosures was adequate consideration for the "give"
  of a class-wide release.
- How disclosure claims in deal litigation ought to be adjudicated, given that a
  settlement hearing supplies the court with no adversary.

### Holding

- **On materiality: none of the four disclosures was material or even helpful.**
  The Proxy already contained a more-than-fair summary of J.P. Morgan's analysis
  in each respect criticised, and extraneous financial detail does not improve a
  fair summary.
- **On fairness: the settlement is not fair or reasonable, and approval is
  denied.** With no meaningful consideration flowing to the class, the release
  cannot be justified.
- **On the path forward: disclosure claims belong outside the settlement
  context** — on a contested preliminary injunction motion or on a mootness fee
  application — and disclosure settlements that are pursued anyway must rest on
  plainly material disclosure and a narrowly circumscribed release.

### Rule

- The Court of Chancery must independently determine that a class settlement is
  fair and reasonable, evaluating the claim, the defences, the obstacles to
  success, and the reasonableness of the give and the get.
- Directors soliciting stockholder action must disclose fully and fairly all
  material information within the board's control.
- Information is material if there is a substantial likelihood a reasonable
  stockholder would consider it important in deciding how to vote, such that it
  significantly alters the total mix of information available.
- Where a board relies on a financial advisor in recommending stockholder action,
  stockholders are entitled to a fair summary of the substantive work the advisor
  performed.
- A fair summary is an accurate description of methodology and key assumptions,
  not a compilation of underlying data, and need not permit an independent
  valuation.
- Disclosure settlements will be disfavoured unless the supplemental disclosures
  are plainly material and the release is confined to disclosure and sale-process
  claims that have been sufficiently investigated.

### Reasoning

*(Bouchard, C.)*

- The fiduciary character of a class action, not any suspicion about these
  parties, is what obliges the court to substitute its own judgment for the
  parties' agreement.
- The economics are laid out before the merits. Plaintiffs' leverage is the threat
  of an injunction; defendants' incentive is to buy closing certainty and a broad
  release cheaply; supplemental disclosure costs defendants almost nothing; and
  many defendants self-expedite by volunteering core documents, which bypasses the
  motion to expedite — "the only gating mechanism... the Court has to screen out
  frivolous cases."
- Once settlement is agreed, "the litigation takes on an entirely different,
  non-adversarial character," and the court receives briefs extolling the
  settlement and nothing on the other side.
- Disclosure settlements are harder to evaluate in that posture than ordinary
  ones, because there has usually been no motion practice and almost no discovery
  to inform the court, as the thin record here illustrates.
- The harm is not merely that the class gets nothing. Releases threaten the loss
  of claims that were never investigated, and Rural/Metro proves the risk is real
  rather than theoretical.
- The remedy is procedural rather than substantive. Both alternatives Bouchard
  identifies work by restoring an opponent — an injunction motion the defendants
  contest, or a fee application the defendants have reason to fight.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The opinion does leave one tension unresolved. Bouchard reroutes disclosure claims
to the preliminary injunction motion and the mootness fee application, but the
mootness fee channel still pays counsel for disclosures the defendants chose to
make without any judicial finding of materiality, and it does so without a class
release to make the payment worth scrutinising at all. The adversary is restored,
but only over the size of the fee.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the give and the get.** That phrase is the holding in four words,
  and the analysis is a comparison: a release of every claim in the transaction
  against four additions to a ten-page banker summary.
- **Be precise about what a fair summary is.** Methodology and key assumptions,
  not underlying data, and expressly not enough for a stockholder to run an
  independent valuation. The commonest wrong answer is that stockholders are
  entitled to the banker's full work product.
- **Know the two alternative channels.** The contested preliminary injunction
  motion, where the plaintiff bears the materiality burden, and the mootness fee
  application, where defendants have reason to oppose. Both work by supplying an
  adversary, which is the unifying idea.
- **Have "plainly material" ready as a term of art.** It means it should not be a
  close call that the information is material. The standard is deliberately
  higher than materiality itself, because the court is buying a release with it.
- **Expect the policy pushback.** If Trulia makes deal litigation unprofitable, do
  meritorious claims disappear too? The answer in the opinion is that meritorious
  claims survive the Rule 12 test applied with special care, and that thirty years
  of Unocal and Revlon jurisprudence lets courts sort them. The honest
  counterpoint is that the screening now happens at the pleading stage, where the
  plaintiff has no discovery.
- **Connect materiality forward.** The same standard decides whether a vote was
  fully informed for Corwin cleansing and for the MFW minority vote, both assigned
  the next day. A plainly material omission defeats both.
- **Numbers worth holding.** $375,000 fee; 224-page proxy; ten-page banker
  summary; four supplemental disclosures, none material; fewer than 3,000 pages of
  discovery; three depositions; 94.9% of large deals litigated in 2014; 76.0% of
  Delaware deal cases settled on disclosure alone in 2012; over $100 million later
  recovered in Rural/Metro.
