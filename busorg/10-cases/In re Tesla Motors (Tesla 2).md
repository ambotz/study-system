## Rule

Entire fairness has two components, **fair dealing and fair price**, and the test is
composite rather than bifurcated. But "[t]he paramount consideration ... is whether the
price was a fair one" — "all roads in the realm of entire fairness ultimately lead to fair
price." A defective process can be survived by a demonstrably fair price; a price within
the range of fairness cannot rescue a process that was "beyond unfair."

## Facts

- The same acquisition as [[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]], now **after a full trial**.
  Four counts remained: derivative loyalty claims against Musk as controller and as
  director, unjust enrichment, and waste.
- **The court never decides whether Musk is a controller.** It "assume[s] Plaintiffs' best
  case on standard of review — that entire fairness applies," gives "no deference," and
  reviews with "the highest degree of scrutiny recognized in our law." *This assumption is
  the structural move that makes the opinion work.*
- **The board used one protection and not the other.** The acquisition **was** conditioned
  on approval by a majority of disinterested stockholders. There was **no independent
  special committee**, "for reasons unexplained."
- **Process flaws, all found as fact.** Musk told Lyndon Rive that Tesla would acquire
  SolarCity, and promised a bridge loan, without board knowledge or approval; directed
  Tesla's CFO to prepare a financial analysis before presenting the proposal to the board;
  participated in selecting outside deal counsel; reviewed the offer letter and the
  announcement blog post; discussed price and explained why a **30% premium** — above
  Evercore's recommendation but within the range — might be needed; was in frequent contact
  with Evercore outside the boardroom; **self-published "Master Plan, Part Deux" mid-
  negotiation** to build stockholder support; was told of Evercore's advice to lower the
  offer before the board was; and publicly demonstrated an **inoperable Solar Roof** before
  the vote. His recusal was "fluid," and when present he "simply could not help but to
  'voice [his] opinion, obviously.'" **Kimbal was never recused.**
- **Process strengths.** Independent top-tier advisors (Wachtell and Evercore), selected
  without Musk or Gracias; favourable timing, with solar stocks at historic lows, after the
  Model X rollout and before the Model 3; the Gigafactory's strategic fit; and "an
  indisputably independent director leading the way."
- **Price evidence.** Tesla has realised roughly **$1 billion** in nominal cash flows from
  legacy SolarCity systems and expects **$2 billion more** — Musk's point being that "even
  if we shut down the business, the $3 billion in cash flow is 50 percent higher than the
  acquisition price that we paid." Evercore projected **$122–235 million** of synergies in
  2017 alone; Tesla internally expected at least **$150 million a year**.
- The stockholder vote was taken with extensive disclosure of SolarCity's liquidity
  position and against public commentary including **Glass Lewis calling the deal a
  "bailout"** with a process "steeped in conflicts." *The vote survived that.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Entire fairness assumed, and satisfied.** Musk proved the acquisition was entirely
  fair; no breach of fiduciary duty. "[A] patently fair price ultimately carries the day."
- **The two components** (*Weinberger*): fair dealing "embraces questions of when the
  transaction was timed, how it was initiated, structured, negotiated, disclosed to the
  directors, and how the approvals of the directors and the stockholders were obtained";
  fair price "relates to the economic and financial considerations."
- **Composite, not bifurcated.** "All aspects of the issue must be examined as a whole
  since the question is one of entire fairness" (*Trados*). Perfection "is not possible, or
  expected."
- **Price is paramount, and the reason is stated**: a demonstrably fair price "is
  inconsistent with the notion that a fiduciary disloyally attempted to channel value to
  himself or third parties at the expense of the beneficiaries of his duties."
- **But price is not a cure-all.** A fair price "does not ameliorate a process that was
  beyond unfair" (*Nine Systems*). "Factors such as coercion, the misuse of confidential
  information, secret conflicts, or fraud could lead a court to hold that a transaction
  that fell within the range of fairness was nevertheless unfair" (*Basho*).
- **Fair price is a range, not a number.** Unlike appraisal, it "is not itself a remedial
  calculation"; the task is "to determine whether the transaction price falls within a range
  of fairness," because "the value of a corporation is not a point on a line, but a range of
  reasonable values." Delaware "does not have a rigid hierarchy of valuation methodologies."
- **Inherent coercion assumed and then found unexercised.** A controller "brings with him
  into the boardroom an element of 'inherent coercion,'" so the court searched for evidence
  Musk exploited it. He did not "engage[] in pressure tactics that went beyond ordinary
  advocacy to encompass aggressive, threatening, disruptive, or punitive behavior."
- **Process infirmities raise the price burden**: "In instances where there are process
  infirmities, the Court is obliged to study fair price even more carefully."

**Rejected**

- *Tesla bailed out an insolvent SolarCity on Musk's schedule.* The insolvency case rested
  on "incredible testimony," and the timing suited Tesla — solar equities at historic lows,
  between two vehicle launches, with the Gigafactory positioned to serve both businesses.
- *There is no contemporaneous evidence of synergies.* Synergies were "a focus of the Tesla
  Board from the very beginning," analysed by Evercore, quantified contemporaneously, and
  disclosed in three categories before closing.
- *Musk's involvement broke the process.* The court agrees Tesla "should have been able to
  negotiate [] unhindered" by his "dominating hand," and that on these facts alone "one
  would be justified in characterizing the process as broken." **"But they do not."** The
  redeeming features emulated arm's-length bargaining.
- *A fairer price was available.* Plaintiffs' alternative valuations were "also ran" theories
  their own experts did not ultimately endorse. The price was "not 'near the low end of a
  range of fairness,' but 'entirely' fair in the truest sense of the word."

## Context

The completion of the arc begun in [[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]] and the best available
illustration that **surviving dismissal is not the same as losing**. Tesla 1 denied a motion
to dismiss on a "close call" about controller status; Tesla 2 assumes that question against
the defendant, applies the most demanding standard Delaware has, and enters judgment for him
anyway.

The structural lesson is the one Slights states himself, and it is the reason the case is
assigned with § 144(b) and (c). Musk and the board "likely could have avoided this expensive
and time-consuming litigation had they just adopted more objectively evident procedural
protections." They used the disinterested stockholder vote and skipped the special
committee — and under [[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide|MFW]] the common law required **both**, ab
initio, to restore business judgment review. "[I]f Chancery opinions are 'parables,' let this
be a parable of unnecessary peril, despite the outcome."

**SB 21 changes that arithmetic, which is the point of the pairing.** Post-amendment
§ 144(b) gives a non-going-private controller transaction a safe harbour on **either** an
independent committee **or** a disinterested stockholder vote **or** fairness — one prong,
not two. On the facts of this very deal, the majority-of-the-disinterested condition alone
would now suffice. See
[[Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)]]. And § 144(e)(2)'s one-third
floor makes it doubtful Musk is a controller at all.

Note also what the vote did here even without cleansing. Fischel called it "the ultimate
market test," and its probative force on **price** survived Glass Lewis publicly calling the
deal a bailout "steeped in conflicts." A vote that cannot cleanse can still be evidence.

## Class layer

### Posture

- **Post-trial verdict** in a derivative action, after motion practice and partial settlement.
- **Posture is doing more work here than in almost any case this term.** Tesla 1 was a
  pleading-stage denial of dismissal; this is a judgment on a full record. Everything alleged
  in Tesla 1 was tested, and much of it was found true — and the defendant still won.
- The court declines to resolve the standard-of-review question at all, assuming the
  plaintiffs' best case. That is available only post-trial, where the court can say the
  defendant wins even on the harshest assumption.

### Facts

Tesla acquired SolarCity in 2016 in an all-stock deal. Musk chaired and led both companies
and was the largest stockholder of each; six of seven Tesla directors had ties to SolarCity
or to Musk. By 2016 SolarCity faced serious liquidity pressure, and the plaintiffs' theory
was that Tesla, under Musk's direction, bailed out a company he could not afford to let fail
— the "pyramid" of Tesla, SolarCity and SpaceX that he had publicly warned must not become
"a house of cards."

The board took one of the two standard protections and not the other. It conditioned the
deal on approval by a majority of disinterested stockholders. It formed no special committee,
which left no independent body to negotiate against Musk — and Musk, by the court's findings,
was everywhere: promising Lyndon Rive the acquisition before the board knew, tasking the CFO
with analysis before presenting, helping choose deal counsel, reviewing the offer letter,
talking price with the banker, publishing a manifesto mid-negotiation, and demonstrating a
Solar Roof that did not work.

Against that, the board retained Wachtell and Evercore without Musk's or Gracias's
involvement, timed the deal when solar equities were depressed and Tesla was between vehicle
launches, and had an indisputably independent director leading. SolarCity has since produced
about $1 billion of cash flow for Tesla with $2 billion more expected.

### Issue

- What standard of review governs, where the plaintiff alleges both a conflicted controller
  and a conflicted board majority, and the defendant points to a disinterested stockholder
  vote.
- Whether the acquisition was entirely fair, given a process containing serious flaws and a
  price the defendant says was plainly favourable.

### Holding

- **On standard: assumed against the defendant.** The court assumes entire fairness applies
  without deciding controller status or board conflict, and affords no deference.
- **On fair dealing: flawed but not broken.** Musk's participation was excessive and his
  recusal fluid, but any inherent coercion "was effectively neutralized by a board focused on
  the bona fides of the Acquisition."
- **On fair price: entirely fair.** The price fell not near the low end of the range but
  squarely within it, supported by realised and projected cash flows, contemporaneously
  analysed synergies, and a disinterested stockholder vote taken on full disclosure.
- **Disposition:** judgment for Musk on the fiduciary duty claims.

### Rule

- Delaware has three tiers of review: the business judgment rule, enhanced scrutiny, and
  entire fairness.
- Entire fairness comprises fair dealing and fair price, examined as a whole rather than
  bifurcated.
- The paramount consideration is whether the price was fair.
- A fair price does not ameliorate a process that was beyond unfair; coercion, misuse of
  confidential information, secret conflicts or fraud can make a within-range transaction
  unfair.
- Fair price is a **range**, not a point; the court's task is not to conduct an appraisal but
  to find where the preponderance of credible evidence lands.
- A controller brings "inherent coercion" into the boardroom, but the question is whether the
  controller **exploited** it through conduct beyond ordinary advocacy.
- Where the process is infirm, the court scrutinises price more carefully.

### Reasoning

*(Slights, V.C.)*

- The architecture is the argument. By assuming the plaintiffs' best case, the court removes
  the standard-of-review fight from the outcome entirely — a judgment for the defendant on
  the harshest assumption is unassailable on that ground.
- The flaws are catalogued at length and without minimisation, then answered structurally
  rather than excused: "If these facts comprised the entirety of the deal process, one would
  be justified in characterizing the process as broken. But they do not."
- On coercion the court asks the right narrow question — not whether Musk *could* dominate,
  but whether he *did* — and the answer turns on the absence of "aggressive, threatening,
  disruptive, or punitive behavior" rather than on his silence, which was never offered.
- The price analysis leans on facts that are hard to characterise away: cash already
  received, synergies quantified before the deal, and a vote taken after Glass Lewis publicly
  called the transaction a bailout.
- The closing rebuke is deliberate and is the transferable part. Winning after trial under
  entire fairness is an expensive way to be right, and the court says so.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; post-trial opinion of a single Vice Chancellor.
- The uncomfortable implication, worth two lines: if a process this flawed survives because
  the price was good, "all roads lead to fair price" risks collapsing fair dealing into a
  makeweight — a controller can behave badly and pay well. The court's answer is *Nine
  Systems* and *Basho*: a price within the range still fails where the process was beyond
  unfair, and coercion or secret conflicts would have changed the result.

### Cold-call notes

- **The question to expect is why the court never decided whether Musk is a controller.**
  Because it did not have to: assuming the plaintiffs' best case and still finding for the
  defendant disposes of the case on any view. That move is only available post-trial.
- **Pair it with Tesla 1 deliberately.** Same deal, same facts, opposite outcomes, entirely
  because of posture. Surviving a motion to dismiss decides nothing about the merits.
- **Have both halves of the price principle.** "All roads lead to fair price" **and** a fair
  price "does not ameliorate a process that was beyond unfair." Giving only the first is the
  most likely incomplete answer.
- **Know what the board did and did not do**: disinterested stockholder condition, yes;
  special committee, no. Under MFW that combination could not restore business judgment
  review. Under post-SB 21 § 144(b) the vote alone would now be enough — which is the
  hypothetical Buccola is set up to ask.
- **The memorable facts are the process flaws.** The inoperable Solar Roof demo, "Master
  Plan, Part Deux" published mid-negotiation, promising Lyndon a bridge loan before the board
  knew, and Kimbal never recused.
- **Have the parable line.** "[L]et this be a parable of unnecessary peril, despite the
  outcome."
- **Numbers:** ~$1 billion realised cash flows, $2 billion more expected, $122–235 million of
  2017 synergies, $150 million a year expected internally, a 30% premium discussed.
