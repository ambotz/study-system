## Rule

A court will not review the **substance** of a board's business decision. Absent
conduct that at least borders on fraud, illegality, or conflict of interest, the
directors' judgment is accepted as final and carries a presumption that it was
formed in good faith to promote the corporation's best interests.

What is the scope of the protection? Process failure that borders f, i, or COI. 

## Facts

- Philip K. Wrigley owned roughly **80% of the stock**, was president, and sat on
  the board. *A holder at that level is exactly where a conflict analysis would
  normally bite, and the court still found no conflict — because Wrigley's
  personal views produced no personal financial gain.*
- The Cubs were the only major league club not scheduling night games. In 1966,
  932 of 1,620 major league games were played at night, and every other club
  scheduled substantially all home games at night.
- The Cubs sustained **operating losses from baseball operations in 1961–65**,
  which the plaintiff attributed to daytime-only attendance.
- The alleged motives were Wrigley's personal beliefs that baseball is a "daytime
  sport" and that lights would have "a deteriorating effect upon the surrounding
  neighborhood," together with an alleged admission that he was not interested in
  whether the Cubs benefited financially.
- The other directors were alleged to have acquiesced and permitted Wrigley to
  dominate the board, knowing he was not motivated by the corporation's best
  interests.
- **No fraud, illegality, or self-dealing was alleged.** Wrigley took nothing from
  the refusal; he gained no side payment and stood on both sides of nothing. *This
  absence is what decides the case.*
- The plaintiff alleged financing for lights was readily available and that the
  cost would be more than recaptured by increased attendance revenue. *Argued and
  rejected — the court declined to weigh the business merits at all.*
- White Sox weeknight games drew substantially better than Cubs weekday games,
  while weekend attendance was similar. *Argued and rejected — "mere failure to
  'follow the crowd'" is not dereliction of duty.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The amended complaint fails to state a cause of action. Dismissal affirmed.
- Resolving "questions of policy and business management" is not a court's
  function. Directors are chosen to decide those questions, and their judgment,
  "unless shown to be tainted with fraud," is accepted as final (quoting *Davis v.
  Louisville Gas & Electric Co.*).
- Director judgment enjoys a presumption that it was formed in good faith and
  designed to promote the corporation's best interests.
- Courts should not interfere unless the directors' conduct **at least borders on**
  fraud, illegality, or conflict of interest.
- Wrigley's neighborhood rationale is not contrary to the corporation's interests.
  A director may legitimately weigh whether patrons will attend games in a
  deteriorating neighborhood, and the corporation's long-run interest in the value
  of the Wrigley Field property "might demand all efforts to keep the neighborhood
  from deteriorating."
- Failing to follow industry practice is not negligence. Directors are elected for
  their judgment and cannot be required to surrender it to the decisions of other
  companies' boards.

**Rejected**

- *Fraud, illegality, and conflict of interest are not the only bases for a
  derivative action against directors.* The court accepted that not every
  jurisdiction insists on one of the three, but held that conduct must at minimum
  border on one of them before a court will intervene.
- *Wrigley acted on personal aesthetic and civic preferences rather than corporate
  purpose, which takes the decision outside the rule's protection.* The court held
  the neighborhood concern plausibly maps onto corporate interest through patron
  attendance and long-run property value. Critically, the court declined to decide
  whether the directors were **right**: "we do not mean to say that we have decided
  that the decision of the directors was a correct one. That is beyond our
  jurisdiction and ability."
- *Nineteen of twenty clubs play night games and the Cubs are losing money, so
  refusing lights is negligent.* Directors "even those of corporations that are
  losing money" need not follow the lead of other corporations in the field.
- *Dodge v. Ford supports intervention where directors pursue non-profit motives.*
  The court read [[Dodge v. Ford Motor Co.]] as itself requiring fraud or a breach
  of good faith before a court enters the internal affairs of a corporation.

## Context

Read against [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]], assigned the same day, because the two
cases point in opposite directions while applying the same rule. Shlensky refuses
to examine whether refusing lights was a sound decision; Van Gorkom examines at
length how a decision came to be made. The distinguishing fact is what the
plaintiff attacked — substance in Shlensky, process in Van Gorkom. **The business
judgment rule protects substance, not process.** Posture sharpens the contrast:
Shlensky was decided on a motion to dismiss with every allegation taken as true
and still failed, while Van Gorkom produced a money judgment after a full trial.

The casebook's note frames the two questions the rest of the course answers.
First, **when does the rule apply**, or equivalently how is its protection lost —
which is the Van Gorkom question, and later the Unocal, Revlon and § 144
questions. Second, **what is the scope of the protection once it applies** —
which is the Shlensky question, and the answer is close to absolute.

On corporate purpose, Shlensky breaks no new ground. The opinion sits alongside
[[Dodge v. Ford Motor Co.]] and rests substantially on the possibility that
Wrigley's daytime-baseball approach was itself profitable, which is how the court
avoids deciding whether a director may pursue non-shareholder ends. The statutory
backdrop is 8 Del. C. § 122, permitting donations for public welfare and
charitable purposes, and Delaware's 2013 public benefit corporation statute, which
supplies an express dual-purpose form for corporations that want one.

Worth carrying forward: the conflict-of-interest trigger named here is the one
that does the work later in the course. A controller on both sides of a
transaction loses the protection ([[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]],
[[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]]), and that is the doctrinal path out of
Shlensky's near-absolute deference.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Stockholders' derivative suit against the directors for negligence and
  mismanagement; the corporation joined as a defendant.
- Appeal from **dismissal of the amended complaint on the defendants' motion** —
  so every allegation is taken as true and the complaint still fails.
- Posture is doing real work here. The plaintiff's own account of Wrigley's
  motives was accepted at face value and produced no cause of action, which is a
  far stronger statement of deference than a verdict after trial would have been.

### Facts

The Chicago National League Ball Club, a Delaware corporation operating out of
Chicago, owned the Cubs and ran Wrigley Field. Philip K. Wrigley held about 80%
of the stock and served as president and director. Shlensky was a minority
stockholder.

Night baseball arrived in 1935, and by 1966 nineteen of the twenty major league
clubs scheduled substantially all home games at night, excluding opening days,
weekends, holidays, and dates league rules blocked. The Cubs alone did not. Across
1961 to 1965 the club lost money on baseball operations, and Shlensky tied those
losses to weekday attendance — the White Sox, playing weeknights across town,
drew far better on those dates, while weekend figures for the two clubs were
comparable.

Shlensky's theory was that Wrigley refused lights for reasons of his own: a
conviction that baseball belongs in daylight, and a concern that lights and night
games would degrade the surrounding neighborhood. Wrigley had allegedly said he
was indifferent to whether the club benefited financially, and would accept night
games if a new stadium were built elsewhere in Chicago. The remaining directors,
on this account, knew all of it and let Wrigley run the board on the question
anyway. Shlensky sought damages and an order compelling the installation of
lights and the scheduling of night games.

### Issue

- Whether a stockholder's derivative complaint states a cause of action against
  directors where it alleges that a business decision was made for the dominant
  shareholder's personal, non-corporate reasons, but alleges no fraud, illegality,
  or conflict of interest.
- Whether directors are negligent in declining to follow a practice adopted by
  nearly every other corporation in their industry.

### Holding

- **On the derivative claim: no cause of action.** A complaint must allege conduct
  that at least borders on fraud, illegality, or conflict of interest before a
  court will examine a business decision. Wrigley's alleged motives failed that
  test because the neighborhood and attendance rationale is one a director could
  legitimately hold in the corporation's interest, and because Wrigley derived no
  personal benefit from the refusal.
- **On the industry-practice claim: not negligent.** Directors are elected for
  their own business judgment and cannot be compelled to adopt the judgment of
  other companies' boards, even when the corporation is losing money. "Mere
  failure to 'follow the crowd'" is not dereliction of duty.
- **Disposition:** order of dismissal affirmed.

### Rule

- Courts will not resolve questions of business policy and management for a
  corporation; the directors are chosen to decide those questions.
- Director judgment carries a presumption that it was formed in good faith and
  designed to promote the corporation's best interests.
- That judgment is accepted as final unless shown to be tainted by fraud.
- A court will intervene only where the directors' conduct at least borders on
  fraud, illegality, or conflict of interest.
- A director may weigh long-run and indirect corporate interests — including the
  condition of the neighborhood surrounding corporate property — without leaving
  the rule's protection.
- Departure from industry practice is not, by itself, negligence.

### Reasoning

*(Sullivan, J.)*

- The court adopts the defendants' framing of Delaware law directly, quoting
  *Davis v. Louisville Gas & Electric Co.*: a conflict between management and a
  dissenting minority "touching matters of business policy" is not a court's to
  resolve.
- The alleged motives are then tested not for whether they were the *best* reasons
  but for whether they are reasons a director could hold consistently with
  corporate interest. Patron willingness to attend a park in a poor neighborhood,
  and the long-run value of the Wrigley Field property, both qualify.
- The court is explicit about the limits of its own competence — whether daytime
  baseball is good business is "beyond our jurisdiction and ability." The refusal
  to decide is the holding, not an aside.
- On the industry-comparison argument, the court treats conformity as the opposite
  of judgment: requiring directors to match other boards would require them to
  abandon the judgment they were elected to exercise.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None. The panel was unanimous.
- The counter-argument to hold instead comes from the plaintiff's framing: if a
  dominant holder concededly acts on personal conviction rather than corporate
  benefit, and the corporation loses money for five straight years as a result,
  "no conflict of interest" is doing a great deal of work on a very narrow
  definition of conflict — one that reaches only financial self-dealing and not
  the substitution of personal preference for corporate purpose.

### Cold-call notes

- **The most likely opening question** is what allegation Shlensky would have had
  to make to survive. The answer is a benefit flowing to Wrigley personally —
  ownership of a neighboring property whose value daytime baseball preserved, for
  instance. Personal *preference* is not conflict of interest; personal *gain* is.
  That line is the whole case.
- **The trap** is arguing the merits. Any answer that explains why lights would
  have made money has already lost, because the court refuses to reach that
  question and says so twice. Keep the answer on what a court may examine.
- **Be ready to be pushed on the 80%.** Wrigley controlled the corporation, and
  the rest of the course is about controllers losing the rule's protection. The
  distinction to have ready: control plus a transaction on both sides is a
  conflict, control plus an idiosyncratic business preference is not. Sinclair Oil
  and Tesla are where the first half of that sentence gets developed.
- **Have the posture ready.** Decided on a motion to dismiss, allegations taken as
  true. Buccola may use it to contrast with Van Gorkom's post-trial judgment, and
  the pairing is the sharpest available illustration that the rule polices process
  rather than outcomes.
- **Dodge v. Ford is the likely pivot.** The casebook sets it up immediately before
  Shlensky and asks whether the two are distinguishable. The honest answer is that
  Henry Ford's testimony conceded he was not trying to make a profit, while
  Wrigley's rationale could be reframed as profit-seeking over a longer horizon —
  and that the reframing is doing the work in both cases.
- **Numbers worth keeping:** 80% ownership, 19 of 20 clubs, 932 of 1,620 games in
  1966, losses 1961–65.
