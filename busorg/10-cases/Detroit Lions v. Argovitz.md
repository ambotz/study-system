## Rule

Once an agent is shown to hold an interest in a transaction adverse to the
principal's, **fraud is presumed** and the burden shifts to the agent to prove
that the principal knew every material fact and freely consented. The fairness of
the resulting deal is no defence.

## Facts

- Jerry Argovitz was Billy Sims's agent and simultaneously **owned 29% of the
  Houston Gamblers**, was obligated on 29% of a $1.5 million letter of credit, and
  served as the club's president at $275,000 a year plus 5% of cash flow.
- Sims knew Argovitz had a franchise. **Sims did not know the size of the stake,
  the salary, the cash-flow interest, or the letter of credit.**
- Negotiations with the Lions were progressing normally and the parties were about
  $500,000 apart. *Argovitz told Sims the Lions were moving "laterally" and
  dragging their feet — which the court found false.*
- Argovitz had his Gamblers partner Lerner — who admitted knowing nothing about
  football — negotiate against Sims. Only Argovitz knew both what Sims was worth
  and how badly the Gamblers needed him.
- **Argovitz never called the Lions for a final offer.** During the Houston
  negotiation a Lions negotiator telephoned; Argovitz was in the office and
  declined the call. Both sides' expert witnesses testified that shopping an offer
  in hand is standard practice. *This omission is the core of the breach.*
- Argovitz stood to collect the $100,000 balance of his agent's fee out of a
  $500,000 loan embedded in the Gamblers' own offer.
- In the Kelly contract, negotiated by an independent agent who did shop the offer,
  the Gamblers gave an untested rookie quarterback terms including a top-three-pay
  guarantee. Sims — a Heisman winner and proven NFL star — got $60,000 less and no
  comparable clauses. *Argovitz never asked for them.*
- Five months later Argovitz had Sims sign a **waiver** of claims arising from the
  conflict, without independent advice and without telling Sims's new agent.
- The Gamblers' deal was arguably a good one — five years, $3.5 million, three
  years of skill and injury guarantees the Lions had resisted. *Argued and
  rejected — fairness does not cure self-dealing.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Argovitz held an interest adverse to Sims. Fraud is therefore presumed, and the
  contract is voidable at the principal's election.
- Argovitz failed to carry his burden of showing disclosure of, or immateriality
  of, five specific facts: the relative values of the two offers, the financial
  gap between the USFL and the NFL, his ownership, salary and cash-flow interests,
  the clauses given to Kelly and never sought for Sims, and Sims's own leverage.
- Disclosing the bare existence of a conflict is not enough. The agent must inform
  the principal "of all facts that come to his knowledge that are or may be
  material or which might affect his principal's rights or interests."
- Failing to obtain personal guarantees without adequately warning Sims about the
  risks of a new league was itself a breach.
- Ratification and waiver rejected: Sims did not know the undisclosed facts before
  December 1983, so neither the conduct nor the signed waiver could operate.
- Rescission ordered.

**Rejected**

- *The contract was fair to Sims, so there is no injury and no remedy.* Quoting
  *Burleson*: the question "does not relate to the mala fides of the agent nor to
  whether or not a greater sum might have been procured... The self-interest of the
  agent is considered a vice which renders the transaction voidable at the election
  of the principal without looking into the matter further than to ascertain that
  the interest of the agent exists."
- *Sims was told about the conflict and chose to proceed.* Lerner, not Argovitz,
  raised the conflict, and raised only its existence. Argovitz was not even
  present. Disclosure of a conflict's existence without its material particulars
  is not disclosure.
- *Sims told Argovitz not to call the Lions.* The court found the instruction
  emotionally driven and traceable to Argovitz's own misrepresentations about the
  Lions' good faith. An agent cannot manufacture the principal's consent by
  distorting the information the consent rests on.
- *A nondisclosure clause in the Kelly contract prevented Argovitz from mentioning
  it.* "Frivolous" — asking for equivalent terms for Sims required no disclosure of
  Kelly's deal.
- *Sims signed a waiver.* Obtained without independent advice, without notice to
  Sims's new agent, and before Sims knew the facts being waived.

## Context

Paired with [[In re Motors Liquidation]] to split agency into its two halves.
Motors Liquidation asks what an agent may **do** on the principal's behalf (whatever he is authorized to do);
Argovitz asks what an agent **owes** while doing it. The casebook's framing is
that the duty of loyalty is the foremost fiduciary duty and that business law does
not forbid acting under a conflict — it demands a higher standard of conduct and
more process.

The burden-shifting structure here is the ancestor of everything the course does
later with conflicted transactions. The same architecture — conflict identified,
presumption lost, burden on the fiduciary to prove fairness or cleansing —
reappears in 8 Del. C. § 144, in [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]], and in
[[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide]]. What changes across those cases is the **cleansing
mechanism**: Argovitz offers only full disclosure plus free consent, while the
Delaware corporate cases add disinterested director approval, stockholder
approval, and the post-SB 21 statutory safe harbours.

Note the disclosure standard is unusually demanding — every material fact known to
the agent, not merely the conflict. That standard is stricter than what a
Delaware board must give stockholders, and the contrast is worth holding.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Action by the Detroit Lions and Sims in Oakland County Circuit Court, removed to
  federal court, seeking a declaration that the Gamblers contract was invalid.
- Bench trial; the court sits in equity and orders rescission.

### Facts

Billy Sims signed with the Houston Gamblers of the new USFL on July 1, 1983, then
signed a second contract with the Detroit Lions on December 16, 1983. The suit
asked which contract was good.

Argovitz had been Sims's agent for four years. In early 1983 he told Sims he had
applied for a USFL franchise in Houston, and in May Sims attended the press
conference announcing approval. What Sims never learned was the scale of
Argovitz's position: 29% ownership, a $275,000 salary as president, 5% of cash
flow, and personal exposure on 29% of a $1.5 million letter of credit.

Through June the Lions and Argovitz closed to within $500,000, with the Lions'
negotiator working on an annuity structure while resisting skill and injury
guarantees. Argovitz told Sims those talks were going nowhere. Then he arranged
for his own partner to make Sims an offer, and flew Sims and his wife to Houston
on June 29 to hear it — from a team Argovitz partly owned.

The Gamblers offered $3.5 million over five years with three years of guarantees
and a $500,000 loan, out of which Argovitz would take the $100,000 balance of his
fee. Sims, believing the Lions were not serious, wanted to accept on the spot.
Argovitz asked whether he should telephone the Lions; Sims, by the court's
finding, said no for purely emotional reasons. The Lions' negotiator called that
same afternoon and Argovitz declined to take the call.

In November Argovitz asked Sims to come to his home to sign papers "mistakenly
overlooked." Among them was a waiver of Sims's claims against Argovitz.

### Issue

- Whether an agent who negotiates his principal's contract with an entity the
  agent partly owns has breached the duty of loyalty, where the agent disclosed the
  existence but not the extent of his interest.
- Whether the principal's instruction not to contact the competing bidder, and his
  later signed waiver, cure the breach.

### Holding

- **On the breach: yes.** The adverse interest was established, so fraud was
  presumed and the burden fell on Argovitz. He failed to prove disclosure of five
  identified material facts, or that those facts would not have influenced Sims.
- **On cure: no.** Ratification and waiver both fail because Sims did not know the
  undisclosed facts until December 1983, and the waiver was signed without
  independent advice.
- **Disposition:** rescission of the Gamblers contract.

### Rule

- An agent owes the principal a duty of loyalty requiring the principal's interests
  to be placed above the agent's own.
- Proof that the agent held an interest in the transaction antagonistic to the
  principal's raises a **presumption of fraud**.
- The burden then falls on the agent to prove the principal had full knowledge of
  the agent's interest **and of every material fact known to the agent** that might
  affect the principal, and freely consented with that knowledge.
- Disclosure of the existence of a conflict, without its material particulars, is
  insufficient.
- The fairness of the transaction is not a defence; self-interest alone renders it
  voidable at the principal's election.
- Ratification and waiver require knowledge of the facts being ratified or waived.

### Reasoning

*(DeMascio, J.)*

- The court reconstructs what a loyal agent would have done: take the Lions' call,
  obtain a final offer, and put both offers in front of the principal. Only then
  could Sims have made "an intelligent and knowing decision."
- Expert testimony from **both sides** established that shopping an offer in hand is
  standard practice. The defendant's own expert had done exactly that for another
  client even believing the rival team had already bid its best.
- The court identifies the causal mechanism for the breach: Argovitz would not use
  the wedge every agent dreams of, because using it would have cost him the player
  for a team he owned.
- The Kelly comparison supplies the measure of damage without requiring a valuation
  — an independent agent extracted better terms for a lesser player from the same
  club in the same window.
- On the waiver, the court treats the circumstances as themselves evidence of the
  breach's continuation rather than its resolution.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; single-judge bench trial.
- The argument available to Argovitz, and worth having ready: Sims knew his agent
  owned a USFL team, was independently advised by Lerner that he could retain other
  counsel, declined, and received guarantees the Lions had refused for three years.
  On that framing the rule punishes an agent for a deal that made the principal
  better off. The court's answer is the *Burleson* passage — the inquiry stops at
  whether the interest existed, because any other rule requires courts to price
  the counterfactual deal that was never negotiated.

### Cold-call notes

- **The likely opening** is what Argovitz should have done. Answer in order: disclose
  the full extent of the interest before negotiating, then call the Lions, then put
  both offers in front of Sims, then let Sims decide with independent counsel. The
  conflict itself was curable; the process was not run.
- **The trap** is arguing the deal was good for Sims. The court says twice, through
  *Burleson*, that fairness is not the question. Any answer weighing the merits of
  the contract has conceded the rule.
- **Be ready on "but Sims said not to call."** The instruction came from a principal
  whose picture of the Lions' interest had been shaped by the conflicted agent.
  Consent extracted on distorted information is not consent.
- **The waiver is the memorable fact.** Five months later, at his house, among papers
  described as overlooked, no independent advice, no notice to the new agent. It is
  the cleanest illustration in the course of why disclosure must precede consent.
- **Carry forward the burden-shift.** Conflict identified, presumption lost, burden
  on the fiduciary. The whole of § 144 and the controlling-stockholder cases are
  variations on this move with different cleansing mechanisms bolted on.
- **The line worth quoting:** "no man can faithfully serve two masters whose
  interests are in conflict."
