## What it is

A **special-act charter**: a private bill passed by the New York legislature on 14
April 1815 creating one named corporation. Before general incorporation statutes,
this was how a corporation came into existence — one legislature, one company, one
act, sitting in the session laws between an act about highways in Mamaroneck and one
about a turnpike company.

It is the corporation whose directors were sued in [[Scott v. Depeyster]] seventeen
years later, so the charter and the case are a matched pair: this document is the
grant of power, and the case is about what happened when the directors went outside
it.

The PDF is a scan of the 1815 session laws with **no text layer** — it has to be read
as images.

## What to notice

Read it as an enumeration. Everything here the legislature had to say **because the
corporation had no powers it was not given**, and almost all of it has since migrated
into the DGCL as a default the charter may vary.

- **§ I — a twenty-year life.** The company is a body corporate "until the first day
  of May one thousand eight hundred and thirty-five." Perpetual existence is now the
  default; here it had to be granted, and it expired.
- **§ II — capital fixed by statute.** Shares of $100, not more than five thousand
  of them, total capital not to exceed $500,000. The actual paid-in capital was
  $301,800, which matters in [[Scott v. Depeyster]] because the secretary stole more
  than half of it.
- **§ III — management fixed by statute.** "[T]he stock, property, affairs and
  concerns" of the company managed by **nineteen directors**, elected annually on the
  second Monday in January, public notice in at least two newspapers for ten days,
  one vote per share, proxies permitted but only from citizens of the United States
  inhabiting the state, election inspected by three stockholders who are not
  directors. Compare 8 Del. C. § 141(a)–(b), where the board's existence is the
  default and its size is left to the charter or bylaws.
- **§ VII — bylaw power, granted expressly.** The president and directors "shall have
  power to make and prescribe such by-laws, rules and regulations," provided they are
  "not repugnant to the constitution and laws." Compare § 109(b), which grants the
  power generally and imposes the same consistency limit.
- **§ VIII — enumerated business powers.** Insurance on inland transportation of
  goods, marine insurance, insurance on lives, tontine, and lending on bottomry and
  respondentia. **Nothing else.** This list is what the court in
  [[Scott v. Depeyster]] reads as an implied prohibition on discounting notes.
- **§ IX — mandatory capital maintenance.** Dividends semi-annually, out of profits
  only, and **no dividend after a loss diminishes the capital until the capital is
  restored.** Compare §§ 154 and 170.
- **§ X — real estate restricted.** The company may hold only land "requisite for its
  immediate accommodation" or taken in satisfaction of debts, and must dispose of the
  latter within five years **on pain of forfeiture to the people of this state**.
- **§ XI — no trading.** Forbidden to deal or trade in goods, or to buy or sell stock
  created by Congress or any state, except to invest or secure its capital, pay debts,
  reinvest, or realise on pledged collateral. Together with § VIII this is the
  restriction the directors actually breached.
- **§ XIII — "declared to be a public act."** To be construed "benignly and favorably,
  for every beneficial purpose herein intended."
- **§ XIV — no limited liability.** For debts contracted before the charter's expiry,
  "the persons composing the said corporation, shall be responsible **in their
  individual and private capacity to the extent of their respective shares**." The
  same section closes by making the president and directors "responsible in their
  individual and private capacities for any loss or losses which may be sustained by
  any person or persons, by reason of such negligence."

**The single most important thing here** is § XIV. Limited liability was not a
feature of incorporation in 1815; it had to be granted, and this charter withheld it.
That makes [[Limited liability - piercing the corporate veil]] a question about the
terms of a statutory privilege rather than about a natural attribute of the form.
