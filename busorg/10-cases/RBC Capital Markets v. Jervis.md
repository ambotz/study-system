## Rule

A financial advisor who, for its own motives, **intentionally dupes** a board into
breaching its duty of care — by misleading the board or creating an informational
vacuum — knowingly participates in that breach and is liable for aiding and
abetting, and the target's § 102(b)(7) provision does not shield the advisor even
though it shields the directors.

## Facts

- RBC was engaged by Rural/Metro's special committee to consider strategic
  alternatives while a competitor, Emergency Medical Services, was simultaneously
  up for sale. The overlap is what created the conflict: the firms bidding for EMS
  would also bid for Rural.
- RBC wanted a **buy-side financing role in the EMS transaction**, and devised a
  plan to favour bidders in the Rural auction in order to capture that business.
  The conflict was not incidental to the engagement; it was the reason RBC shaped
  the process as it did.
- RBC **never disclosed** to the board its interest in obtaining that financing
  role, or how it planned to use the Rural engagement to win it.
- RBC made **eleventh-hour attempts to secure buy-side financing from the winning
  bidder while simultaneously leading the price negotiations**, and did not tell
  the board. Occupying both sides of the same negotiation is the clearest
  expression of the conflict.
- RBC knew the board and the special committee were **uninformed about Rural's
  value**, and made no effort to inform them.
- RBC's desire for Warburg's business "manifested itself in its financial
  analysis," which RBC **modified and delivered on the day the board approved the
  merger**. The board was unaware of the modifications.
- RBC conducted **back-channel communications with Warburg** that the board did not
  know about.
- The resulting sale was poorly timed and at a price the trial court found did not
  reflect appropriate efforts to obtain the best value reasonably available; the
  court found Rural's **stand-alone value exceeded the sale price**.
- RBC's misleading presentation and undisclosed conflicts flowed into the **Proxy
  Statement**, which carried materially misleading information and omitted RBC's
  conflicts.
- The board's own **lack of oversight** afforded RBC "the opportunity to indulge in
  the misconduct which occurred." *The board's failure is part of the causal story,
  not a defence for RBC.*
- The directors were protected by a § 102(b)(7) provision and lacked the scienter
  required for a loyalty violation. *That is why the case is about the advisor.*
- "The manifest intentionality of RBC's conduct" was established by **the bankers'
  own internal communications**.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The elements of aiding and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty, from Malpiede,
  are (i) the existence of a fiduciary relationship, (ii) a breach of the
  fiduciary's duty, (iii) **knowing participation** in that breach by the
  defendants, and (iv) damages proximately caused by the breach.
- "Knowing participation in a board's fiduciary breach requires that the third
  party act with the knowledge that the conduct advocated or assisted constitutes
  such a breach," and a bidder "may be liable to the target's stockholders if the
  bidder attempts to create or exploit conflicts of interest in the board."
- The trial court's formulation is affirmed as a **narrow** holding: "[i]f the
  third party knows that the board is breaching its duty of care and participates
  in the breach by misleading the board or creating the informational vacuum, then
  the third party can be liable for aiding and abetting."
- **It is the aider and abettor that must act with scienter.** The advisor must act
  "knowingly, intentionally, or with reckless indifference," with an "illicit state
  of mind," and the plaintiff must show the advisor had "actual or constructive
  knowledge that their conduct was legally improper." Whether a defendant acted
  with scienter is a **factual** determination.
- RBC knowingly induced the breach by exploiting its own conflicted interests and
  creating an informational vacuum: it concealed its interest in EMS financing and
  its plan to use the Rural engagement to obtain it, knew the board was uninformed
  about value, and hid its eleventh-hour financing attempts while leading the price
  negotiations.
- Aiding and abetting a breach of the **duty of care** is recognised, but on these
  facts only. The claim was premised on RBC's "**fraud on the Board**," where RBC
  "intentionally duped" the directors for its own motives, and the holding "should
  not be read expansively to suggest that any failure on the part of a financial
  advisor to prevent directors from breaching their duty of care gives rise to a
  claim."
- **Section 102(b)(7) protects directors, not third parties.** "The literal
  language of Section 102(b)(7) only covers directors; it does not extend to aiders
  and abettors." The legislature did not intend to create "a perverse incentive
  system wherein trusted advisors to directors could, for their own selfish
  motives, intentionally mislead a board only to hide behind their victim's
  liability shield."
- The exculpatory provision here "is operating as intended." It did not vitiate the
  board's obligation to proceed with due care; it merely proscribed monetary
  liability for failing to do so. RBC bore the burden of showing the directors
  would not have been exculpated and would have shared a common liability.
- The result is not inequitable, because RBC's conduct "accounted for a
  disproportionate amount of the fault," and because **the scienter requirement is
  itself a benefit advisors enjoy that directors do not**: gross negligence by an
  advisor is not enough, so "the aider and abettor standard affords the advisor a
  form of protection by insulating it from liability unless it acts with scienter."
- Affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *Recognising aiding and abetting a duty of care breach creates an anomalous
  imbalance, holding a non-fiduciary liable for a fiduciary's unintentional
  violation.* The concern is overstated because the claim rests on fraud on the
  board and intentional duping, and because scienter "makes an aiding and abetting
  claim among the most difficult to prove."
- *RBC should benefit from Rural's § 102(b)(7) provision, or at least receive
  contribution from the directors.* The provision covers directors by its terms.
  Extending it to a third party who committed a fraud upon the very directors who
  hired and relied upon it would invert the statute's purpose.
- *The trial court improperly asserted the directors' § 102(b)(7) defence on their
  behalf, and exculpation is meant to eliminate liability rather than shift it.*
  The court did not err in considering the statute's impact, and RBC carried the
  burden on common liability and failed to meet it.
- *The result makes advisors sureties for grossly negligent directors, so
  stockholders effectively shift damages from immunised primary wrongdoers to a
  non-immunised third party.* RBC's fault was disproportionate, and the scienter
  threshold means an advisor is never liable for the negligence that exculpation
  covers.
- *A financial advisor is a "gatekeeper," so a failure to prevent a care breach
  suffices.* The Supreme Court expressly declines to adopt the trial court's
  gatekeeper language, even as dictum. An advisor's role "is primarily contractual
  in nature, is typically negotiated between sophisticated parties, and can vary,"
  and the engagement letter defines it. What remains true is narrower: "[t]he
  conflicted banker has an informational advantage" as to its own conflicts, and is
  "under an obligation not to act in a manner that is contrary to the interests of
  the board of directors, thereby undermining the very advice that it knows the
  directors will be relying upon."

## Context

Rural/Metro is the case [[In re Trulia]] holds up as the cautionary example. The
Court of Chancery there called it a "very close call" to reject a disclosure
settlement whose release would have extinguished these claims, which later produced
**over $100 million** for stockholders, most of it from this post-trial judgment
after new counsel took over. Read the two together and the point is that the
release Trulia refused to approve was a release of this case.

The structural lesson is about **where liability comes to rest after exculpation**.
The directors breached the duty of care and paid nothing, because Rural had a
§ 102(b)(7) provision and they lacked scienter. RBC breached no fiduciary duty at
all — it owed none — and paid, because it knew what it was doing. Exculpation does
not eliminate the underlying breach; it eliminates the directors' monetary
liability for it, leaving the breach available as the predicate for someone else's
secondary liability. That asymmetry is the whole case, and it is why advisor
conflicts became the central battleground in Delaware deal litigation after 2015.

The relationship to [[In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation]], assigned the
same day, is the frame for the session. RBC is the **outer bound of liability** and
Mindbody is the **floor**. RBC created the informational vacuum; Vista walked past
one that already existed. In RBC the advisor knew everything and the board knew
nothing; in Mindbody the primary violator knew everything the third party knew, so
there was nothing to create and nothing to supply. The Supreme Court in Mindbody
calls RBC "perhaps the most directly relevant precedent" and then distinguishes it
on exactly that fact.

Note what the case does **not** hold, because the footnote is doing real work. The
Supreme Court affirms the result while refusing the gatekeeper theory that produced
it. Bankers are not gatekeepers with duties running to stockholders; they are
counterparties under negotiated engagement letters, with one asymmetry the law
polices — they alone know their own conflicts, so disclosure and consent are what
"evens the field."

The predicate breach here is a [[Sale of control - Revlon and the duty to maximise price|Revlon]]
breach: the board failed to obtain the best value reasonably available because RBC
manipulated the process. And the misleading financial presentation flowed into the
proxy, which connects to
[[Cleansing by stockholder vote - Corwin and the fully informed vote]] — a proxy
containing materially misleading banker analysis cannot support a fully informed
vote, so cleansing was never available.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from a post-trial judgment of the Court of Chancery holding RBC liable for
  aiding and abetting the Rural/Metro board's breach of the duty of care.
- The directors settled and were in any event exculpated; RBC alone went to
  judgment and appealed.
- Posture drives the doctrine here. Because this is a **post-trial** decision, the
  scienter question — expressly "a factual determination" — was resolved on a full
  record of RBC's internal communications, which is why the holding is so hard to
  replicate at the pleading stage.

### Facts

RBC Capital Markets was engaged by a special committee of Rural/Metro Corporation
to consider strategic alternatives. At the same time Emergency Medical Services, a
competitor of Rural, was itself up for sale. RBC realised that the private equity
firms evaluating EMS would also be natural bidders for Rural, and that a role
advising Rural put it in a position to win buy-side financing mandates on the EMS
deal. So it built the Rural process around that objective, favouring the bidders
who were also pursuing EMS.

None of that was disclosed to the Rural board. RBC ran the sale process, led the
price negotiations, and produced the valuation work on which the directors relied,
while conducting back-channel communications with Warburg — one of the bidders in
both transactions — and pressing for a financing role with the eventual buyer in
the final hours before the vote. RBC also modified its financial analysis and
delivered the modified version on the day the board approved the merger, without
telling the directors it had changed.

The Court of Chancery found that, as a result of RBC's manipulation, the board ran
a sale that failed to maximise price as Revlon required, and that Rural's
stand-alone value exceeded the sale price. The directors, however, were protected
by Rural's § 102(b)(7) exculpatory provision and lacked the scienter required for a
loyalty violation. RBC was not covered by that provision, did possess the necessary
scienter, and was therefore held liable for aiding and abetting the directors'
breach.

Amicus briefs from industry groups warned that recognising a claim for aiding and
abetting a **care** breach would make advisors liable for someone else's
unintentional violation, and would in effect make them sureties for grossly
negligent directors who face no risk themselves.

### Issue

- Whether Delaware recognises a claim for aiding and abetting a board's breach of
  the duty of **care**, and if so on what terms.
- Whether RBC's conduct satisfied the knowing participation element, including the
  scienter required of the aider and abettor.
- Whether Rural's § 102(b)(7) exculpatory provision shields RBC, or entitles RBC to
  contribution from the exculpated directors.

### Holding

- **On the cause of action: recognised, narrowly.** A third party that knows the
  board is breaching its duty of care and participates by misleading the board or
  creating the informational vacuum can be liable, and the holding is expressly
  confined to that.
- **On knowing participation: satisfied.** RBC exploited its undisclosed conflicts,
  concealed its pursuit of buy-side financing while leading negotiations, knew the
  board was uninformed about value, and altered its valuation analysis on the day
  of the vote — a fraud on the board that proximately caused the care breach.
- **On § 102(b)(7): no shield and no contribution.** The statute covers directors
  by its literal terms, and RBC failed to carry its burden of showing the directors
  would have shared a common liability.
- **On equity: the result stands.** RBC's fault was disproportionate, and the
  scienter requirement already gives advisors protection directors lack, since
  gross negligence never suffices for aiding and abetting.
- **Disposition:** affirmed.

### Rule

- Aiding and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty requires a fiduciary
  relationship, a breach of that duty, knowing participation in the breach, and
  damages proximately caused by the breach.
- Knowing participation requires the third party to act with knowledge that the
  conduct advocated or assisted constitutes a breach.
- The aider and abettor must itself act with scienter — knowingly, intentionally,
  or with reckless indifference — and with actual or constructive knowledge that
  its own conduct was legally improper.
- A bidder or advisor may be liable where it attempts to create or exploit
  conflicts of interest in the board.
- Scienter makes an aiding and abetting claim among the most difficult to prove.
- Section 102(b)(7) bars recovery of monetary damages against directors for a claim
  based exclusively on a duty of care violation, but does not extend to third
  parties.
- An exculpatory provision does not vitiate the board's obligation to proceed with
  due care; it proscribes monetary liability if the board fails to do so.

### Reasoning

*(Valihura, J.)*

- The court builds the holding out of Malpiede rather than announcing anything new,
  which is how it can recognise a care-based aiding and abetting claim while
  insisting the holding is narrow.
- Scienter is placed on the **aider and abettor's own conduct**, not merely on the
  primary breach. Knowing the board is breaching is not enough; the advisor must
  know its own participation is legally improper. That double requirement is what
  the court later leans on when it says these claims are among the hardest to
  prove.
- The causal account matters as much as the mental one. RBC did not merely fail to
  correct a board that was already lost — RBC **created** the vacuum, by concealing
  its own conflicts, by withholding the modifications to its valuation, and by
  running back-channel negotiations. The board's own inattention gave RBC "the
  opportunity to indulge in the misconduct," but the misconduct is RBC's.
- On exculpation, the court reads § 102(b)(7) as doing exactly what it says. It
  removes a remedy against directors; it does not erase the breach. Because the
  second Malpiede element asks only whether a fiduciary breached, the exculpated
  breach remains a perfectly good predicate.
- The perverse-incentive passage is the normative core. A rule extending the shield
  to advisors would let a trusted adviser defraud its own client and then shelter
  behind the client's protection.
- The answer to the surety objection is structural rather than equitable: advisors
  and directors face **different liability standards**, and the advisor's is
  higher. A grossly negligent advisor is never liable. Only a scienter-bearing one
  is, and at that point the asymmetry is the point rather than an anomaly.
- The gatekeeper footnote quietly narrows the trial court's rationale. Refusing to
  treat advisors as gatekeepers keeps the duty contractual, and locates the one
  thing the law does require in the informational asymmetry over the advisor's own
  conflicts.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion's hardest problem is one it answers in policy terms rather than
doctrinal ones. Aiding and abetting is secondary liability, which normally means
the secondary actor's exposure derives from the primary actor's. Here the primary
actor has no exposure at all, and the entire judgment falls on the secondary one —
so the liability is derivative in its elements and freestanding in its
consequences. The court's answer, that the standards differ and RBC's fault was
disproportionate, is persuasive on these facts, but it does not resolve the
conceptual point, and it is the strongest version of the amici's objection.

### Cold-call notes

- **State the four Malpiede elements first.** Fiduciary relationship, breach,
  knowing participation, damages proximately caused. Everything in this case
  happens inside element three.
- **Split knowing participation into its two questions.** Did the third party know
  the board was breaching, and did it know **its own** conduct was legally
  improper? The second is the one students drop, and it is the one Mindbody turns
  on the next day.
- **Say why the predicate breach can be an exculpated one.** Section 102(b)(7)
  removes the directors' monetary liability, not the breach, so element two is
  satisfied by a care breach for which nobody pays.
- **Have the narrowness language ready.** The holding is confined to a third party
  that knows of the breach and participates "by misleading the board or creating
  the informational vacuum," and expressly does not reach any failure to prevent a
  care breach.
- **Know that the gatekeeper theory was rejected.** The trial court called advisors
  gatekeepers; the Supreme Court affirmed the judgment while refusing that
  characterisation, on the ground that the advisor relationship is contractual and
  negotiated. It is a good answer to "do bankers owe duties to stockholders?" — no,
  but they must not undermine the advice they know the board is relying on.
- **Be ready for the surety objection.** The strongest form is that stockholders
  shift damages from immunised primary wrongdoers onto a non-immunised third party.
  The court's answer is the different standards: gross negligence never reaches an
  advisor, only scienter does, so the advisor is never liable for what exculpation
  covers.
- **Connect to Trulia.** The disclosure settlement Bouchard describes would have
  released these claims, which later returned over $100 million. That is the
  concrete stake in the give-and-the-get analysis.
