## Rule

Where an LLC agreement **waives fiduciary duties** and **expressly grants the board sole
discretion** over the manner of a sale, there is **no gap** for the implied covenant of good faith
and fair dealing to fill — "if the scope of discretion is specified, there is no gap in the
contract as to the scope of the discretion," and "[t]he implied covenant cannot be invoked to
override the express terms of the contract."

## Facts

- **Sections 2.05 and 3.09 waived all fiduciary duties** — among members, and from the managers to
  the members. That waiver is what removes entire fairness from the case entirely.
- **Section 8.06(a) gave the Board "sole discretion" over "the manner in which... an Approved Sale
  shall occur."** The scope of discretion being specified is what forecloses the implied covenant.
- **The single express safeguard** was that any sale be to an **independent third party**. The
  parties thereby "avoided the possibility of a self-dealing transaction but otherwise left to the
  HCP Entities the ability to structure a deal favorable to their interests."
- **The waterfall created the incentive, and it was obvious on the face of the agreement.** HCP held
  about 78.5% of the Class E units ($1,963,354, entitled to 200% = $3,926,708 first in line) and
  87.5% of the Class D units ($12,000,000, entitled to 200% = $24,000,000 second in line). So "the
  HCP Entities were entitled to the bulk of the first $30 million," and "of each additional dollar
  of sales proceeds, **0% would then go to the HCP Entities**" until several other classes had been
  paid.
- **HCP appointed four of seven managers** under § 3.01(a), and § 8.06 obliged every member to
  consent to a Board-approved sale, with the Board appointed attorney-in-fact for any refusing
  member.
- The alleged conduct was aggressive: no open sales process, little time for the non-affiliated
  managers to find alternatives, undisclosed calls between HCP managers and MTS, and pressure on the
  other managers through "scare tactics about a possible lawsuit" and threats to station HCP
  partners in Trumpet's offices weekly. *None of it matters, because there is no gap.*
- **Shafer conceded at the March 7 meeting that HCP likely should have employed a more open-market
  process.** *Still not a breach of a duty nobody owed.*
- The abbreviated process did move the price — MTS went from **$31 million to $41 million to $43
  million** — which the court notes without relying on.
- **Section 8.06(a)'s notice requirement is affirmative evidence against the plaintiffs.** It
  requires the Board to notify members of an approved sale and describe it, and "[i]f the parties had
  expected that Trumpet would be sold only via an open-market process, there would have been no need"
  for such a provision. Nor does the OA require the Board to tell members about an **ongoing** sales
  process.
- At $43 million, participating Class A and B members receive "almost nothing," and only about
  one-third of their investment **if they waive claims against the Board**; nonparticipating Class A,
  B, C and Common Interest members receive nothing. At a hypothetical $53 million all preferred
  classes would have been paid in full. *Damages framing only.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The LLC Act "permits parties to an LLC agreement to **eliminate fiduciary duties** that members or
  managers would otherwise owe to one another," reflecting the policy "of giv[ing] the maximum effect
  to the principle of freedom of contract."
- **But an LLC agreement "may not eliminate the implied contractual covenant of good faith and fair
  dealing."** "Indeed, the implied covenant inheres in every contract governed by Delaware law," and
  here the OA itself provides that the managers are bound by it.
- Because an implied covenant claim is contractual, its elements are those of a breach of contract
  claim: "a specific implied contractual obligation, a breach of that obligation by the defendant,
  and resulting damage to the plaintiff."
- **Applying the covenant is "a 'cautious enterprise'," and the doctrine is "rarely invoked
  successfully."** It applies only where one party "proves that the other party has acted
  **arbitrarily or unreasonably**, thereby frustrating the fruits of the bargain that the asserting
  party reasonably expected." Expectations are measured **as of the time of contracting**, and
  implied terms must address "developments or contractual gaps that the asserting party pleads
  **neither party anticipated**."
- "**The Court will not rewrite a contract simply because a party now wishes it had gotten a better
  deal.**" The covenant does not "establish a free-floating requirement that a party act in some
  morally commendable sense." **Good faith** means "faithfulness to the scope, purpose, and terms of
  the parties' contract," and **fair dealing** "is something of a misnomer" — it "simply means actions
  consonant 'with the terms of the parties' agreement and its purpose.'"
- **The first step is to look for a gap.** "[T]he implied covenant applies only if the contract is
  silent as to the subject at issue. If the contract directly addresses the matter at hand,
  '[e]xisting contract terms control... such that implied good faith cannot be used to circumvent the
  parties' bargain.'"
- If there is a gap, the court asks "whether it is clear from what was expressly agreed upon that the
  parties who negotiated the express terms **would have agreed to proscribe the act later complained
  of**... had they thought to negotiate with respect to that matter" — not from "its own notions of
  justice or fairness." The covenant "operates only in that **narrow band of cases** where the
  contract as a whole speaks sufficiently to suggest an obligation and point to a result, but does
  not speak directly enough to provide an explicit answer."
- **Waiver counsels extra restraint.** "When an LP [or LLC] agreement eliminates fiduciary duties as
  part of a detailed contractual governance scheme, Delaware courts should be **all the more
  hesitant** to resort to the implied covenant," because such an agreement "implies an agreement that
  **losses should remain where they fall**."
- **No gap exists here.** Section 8.06(a) "explicitly vests the Board with sole discretion as to the
  manner in which a sale is conducted, subject to the limitation that the company is ultimately sold
  to an unaffiliated third-party buyer." The plaintiffs' reading — that "manner" means only
  transactional **form** — "is, to my mind, **unreasonable**," since the provision "could have, but
  does not, say" that discretion is limited to choosing among structures.
- **Specified discretion is not unbounded discretion.** "[W]hen a contract confers discretion on one
  party, the implied covenant requires that the discretion be used reasonably and in good faith. But
  this Court has also held that **if the scope of discretion is specified, there is no gap in the
  contract as to the scope of the discretion**."
- The sole-discretion cases are distinguished on the same ground. An unqualified grant risks abuse
  "for self-interested reasons," but "here, the parties explicitly addressed the potential for
  self-dealing inherent in such clauses" by barring insider sales. "[T]he parties to Trumpet's OA
  **recognized (and filled) the gap** that some courts have discerned in contractual grants of sole
  discretion."
- **Independently, no frustrated expectation.** The express terms "suggest precisely the opposite" of
  an auction expectation: the § 8.06(a) notice requirement would be unnecessary under an open-market
  process, and the OA nowhere requires the Board to disclose an ongoing sales process.
- Motion to dismiss **granted**.

**Rejected**

- *The implied covenant should supply a term requiring an open-market sale or auction, because the
  waterfall gave the HCP-dominated Board an incentive to stop negotiating at about $30 million.* "The
  problem with the Plaintiffs' assertion... is that the **incentive complained of is obvious on the
  face of the OA**. The members, despite creating this incentive, eschewed fiduciary duties, and gave
  the Board sole discretion." An incentive visible at the time of contracting is not an unanticipated
  gap.
- *Section 8.06(a) addresses only the "form" of a sale, because the following clause limits "manner"
  to "a sale of assets, merger, transfer of Membership Interests or otherwise."* Unreasonable. The
  plain meaning is that the Board "can market the company in whatever manner it chooses... and that
  such discretion **includes** decisions about the form of the transaction."
- *Even if § 8.06(a) covers the sales process, the implied covenant requires that discretion be
  exercised reasonably and in good faith.* True as a general proposition, and inapplicable where the
  scope of discretion is specified, because the covenant "cannot be invoked to override the express
  terms of the contract."
- *Cases applying the implied covenant with particular force to grants of sole discretion control.*
  They address the risk of self-dealing inherent in unqualified grants; these parties addressed that
  risk themselves with the unaffiliated-third-party requirement.

## Context

Miller is the practical end of the arc that begins with [[Elf Atochem North America v. Jaffari]].
Elf Atochem establishes that the LLC Act gives "maximum effect to the principle of freedom of
contract"; the [[Note on LLC default fiduciary duties]] records that default duties do exist after
the 2013 amendment to § 18-1104 and that members may contract out of them; and Miller shows what it
means to have done so. Glasscock states the whole progression in one passage: "under our law an LLC
itself implies default fiduciary duties; to eliminate such duties, the members must **explicitly
waive** them. They did so here, despite the presence of a controller with an incentive to take a
quick sale, and a Board with sole discretion to approve such a sale, with the single safeguard that
the sale must not be to an insider."

**The comparison the opinion invites is to [[In re Trados]]**, and it is the cleanest
entity-choice illustration in the course. "[I]f the parties had chosen to employ the **corporate
form** here, with its common-law fiduciary duties, this matter would be subject to **entire fairness
review**." The facts are nearly identical to Trados — a private equity controller, a waterfall that
pays the controller first, a board it dominates, a quick sale that leaves the junior classes with
nothing — and the outcome is opposite. Trados got a trial on entire fairness; Miller got dismissed on
the pleadings. The only difference is the entity and the waiver.

**The implied covenant's structure is the transferable content.** Step one is the **gap** question,
and it is usually dispositive. Step two, reached only if the contract is silent, asks what the
parties **would have agreed to** had they negotiated the point — not what is fair. Two refinements
are worth memorising: expectations are fixed **at the time of contracting**, and a **specified scope
of discretion leaves no gap**, which is why drafting "sole discretion, subject to X" is stronger than
"sole discretion" alone.

**The losses-lie-where-they-fall principle** is the normative justification and explains why the
result is not harsh. A waiver of fiduciary duties in a detailed governance scheme is itself a bargain
about risk allocation, and the OA "was drafted to attract capital investment, by allowing an exit on
terms favorable to the investors." Reading in an auction requirement "would deprive the Defendants of
a negotiated-for benefit" — the plaintiffs would be taking back with the covenant what they sold to
raise the money.

The reasoning parallels [[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]] from session 23 and points up the
contrast. Both let sophisticated investors reallocate fiduciary protection by contract. But Rich
preserves a floor — no covenant can exculpate **intentional** wrongdoing — while Miller finds no floor
to apply, because the implied covenant asks about the parties' bargain rather than about the
defendant's state of mind. The conduct alleged here is unattractive, and the court never needs to
evaluate it.

Note the one duty that could not be waived, and why it did not help: **6 Del. C. § 18-1101(c)** makes
the implied covenant mandatory, and the OA itself bound the managers to it. Mandatory status is not
the same as substantive protection.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Defendants' motion to dismiss under Court of Chancery Rule 12(b)(6).
- The complaint pleaded four counts: breach of the implied covenant against the HCP entities and the
  four HCP-affiliated managers; aiding and abetting that breach; tortious interference with contract;
  and civil conspiracy.
- Granted.
- The posture is what makes the case so useful. Every allegation about the sales process is assumed
  true — the undisclosed calls, the pressure tactics, the concession that a more open process should
  have been used — and it is all irrelevant, because the analysis never reaches conduct. That is the
  strongest possible demonstration of what a fiduciary waiver accomplishes.

### Facts

Christopher Miller and Franklin "Lani" Fritts founded Trumpet Search, LLC in 2008. Trumpet provides
clinical services to people with autism and other developmental disabilities, focused on helping
disabled children develop skills and cope with behavioural issues, and had benefited from favourable
economic and political trends.

In late 2014 two HCP entities purchased the majority of Trumpet's Class D interests, becoming its
largest members. HCP is a Chicago private equity firm that looks, per its website, for "high
potential value companies still flying under the radar." It had formed the two entities to hold the
interests and to appoint a majority of Trumpet's Board.

On May 5, 2016 the members executed the Second Amended and Restated Operating Agreement. It created
a new class of Class E units "primarily for HCP Investments," entitled to a first-in-line,
first-priority return of 200% of capital contributions, followed by Class D at 200%, then
participating Class A and B at 100%, then non-participating A and B, then non-participating C, then
$1,092,500 to four named management members, then $6,907,500 to all members pro rata, then a further
$1,092,500 to participating management members, and finally all members pro rata.

The arithmetic: HCP Investments bought 78.5% of the Class E units for $1,963,354, giving it a
first-position payout of $3,926,708; the two HCP entities held 87.5% of the Class D units,
representing $12,000,000 and a second-position payout of $24,000,000. So HCP took the bulk of the
first $30 million and then nothing further until several junior classes had been satisfied.

Sections 2.05 and 3.09 waived all fiduciary duties for members and Board members respectively.
Section 3.01(a) let HCP appoint a majority of the seven-person Board, which it did — Signoret,
Shafer, Russell and Maruri, against Miller, Fritts and Leslie Margolin. Section 8.06 obliged every
member to consent to a Board-approved sale to an independent third party, with the Board made
attorney-in-fact for any objector. Section 8.06(a) gave the Board "sole discretion" over "the manner
in which... an Approved Sale shall occur, whether as a sale of assets, merger, transfer of Membership
Interests or otherwise," and required the Board to notify members in writing of an approved sale with
reasonable details, terms and conditions.

Less than a year later HCP championed a sale to MTS Health Partners, which initially offered $31
million. The HCP-allied managers declined to run an open sales process and gave the non-affiliated
managers little time to find alternatives. Trumpet nonetheless managed an abbreviated process and
pressed MTS up to $41 million and then $43 million.

The process was contentious. When another party, FFL, indicated interest, the non-HCP members
insisted MTS be told; MTS threatened to revoke its offer and later threatened to sue over a purported
exclusivity provision the plaintiffs say did not exist, and falsely accused Miller of contacting FFL
— threats the plaintiffs trace to unauthorised calls Shafer and Signoret had made to MTS. When Fritts
pulled his support on March 2, the HCP managers "ostensibly" announced they would drop MTS and pursue
a more open-market approach, then re-engaged MTS anyway. By a March 7 meeting MTS had raised its
offer by about $1.6 million, its letter of intent had expired, and no other offers were on the table.
The HCP managers allegedly wore Fritts and Margolin down with litigation scare tactics and threats to
station HCP partners in Trumpet's offices weekly. Shafer admitted at that meeting that HCP likely
should have employed a more open-market process, but said the only option now was the $43 million
offer. The Board approved it.

At $43 million, participating Class A and B members receive almost nothing — about a third of their
investment if they waive claims against the Board — and the junior classes receive nothing. The
plaintiffs allege that at $53 million all preferred classes would have been paid in full, and that at
$60 million Miller and the Miller Trust together would have received at least $1 million.

### Issue

- Whether the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing supplies a term requiring the Board,
  once it decided to sell, to conduct an open-market process designed to achieve the highest value
  reasonably available for all members.
- Whether the OA contains a gap as to how Trumpet could be marketed and sold, given § 8.06(a)'s grant
  of sole discretion over the "manner" of a sale.
- Whether a contractual grant of sole discretion is itself a gap that the implied covenant fills.

### Holding

- **On the waiver: effective.** The LLC Act permits elimination of fiduciary duties, and the members
  eliminated them, leaving only the non-waivable implied covenant.
- **On the gap: none.** Section 8.06(a) explicitly vests the Board with sole discretion over the
  manner of a sale, subject only to the unaffiliated-third-party requirement, and the plaintiffs'
  narrower reading of "manner" is unreasonable.
- **On specified discretion: no gap either.** Where the scope of discretion is specified, there is no
  gap as to its scope, and the covenant cannot override express terms. The parties themselves filled
  the self-dealing gap that sole-discretion clauses usually leave.
- **On expectations, in the alternative: not frustrated.** The notice requirement in § 8.06(a) and the
  absence of any obligation to disclose an ongoing process both suggest the parties contemplated a
  privately negotiated sale.
- **Disposition:** motion to dismiss granted.

### Rule

- An LLC agreement may eliminate fiduciary duties owed by members or managers to one another.
- An LLC agreement may not eliminate the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, which
  inheres in every contract governed by Delaware law.
- The elements of an implied covenant claim are those of a breach of contract claim: a specific
  implied contractual obligation, breach, and resulting damage.
- The covenant applies only where a party acted arbitrarily or unreasonably, frustrating the fruits of
  the bargain the asserting party reasonably expected, measured as of the time of contracting.
- Implied terms must address developments or gaps that neither party anticipated.
- Good faith means faithfulness to the scope, purpose and terms of the contract; fair dealing means
  action consonant with the agreement and its purpose, not equitable behaviour.
- The first step is to determine whether the contract contains a gap; if it directly addresses the
  matter, existing terms control.
- Where a contract confers discretion, the covenant requires reasonable and good faith exercise — but
  where the scope of discretion is specified, there is no gap as to that scope.
- Courts should be all the more hesitant to resort to the covenant where an agreement eliminates
  fiduciary duties as part of a detailed governance scheme, because such an agreement implies that
  losses should remain where they fall.

### Reasoning

*(Glasscock, V.C.)*

- The opinion front-loads its answer in the introduction and then proves it, which tells you the
  reasoning is structural rather than fact-bound. The dispositive observation comes early: "**the
  incentive complained of is obvious on the face of the OA**."
- That observation converts the plaintiffs' best argument into their worst. A risk visible in the
  document at signing is not an unanticipated gap; it is a term. "It thus appears that the parties to
  the OA **did consider** the conditions under which a contractually permissible sale could take
  place."
- The gap inquiry is placed first deliberately, because it disposes of the case without any assessment
  of conduct. Everything alleged about the sales process is assumed true and never evaluated.
- The textual analysis of § 8.06(a) is done by asking what the clause **could have said** and did not
  — the drafters could have confined discretion to choosing among enumerated structures, and instead
  wrote language in which the structures are an illustration of a broader grant.
- On specified discretion the court distinguishes the sole-discretion cases by identifying the
  **gap they actually fill**: the risk that an unqualified grant will be abused for self-interested
  reasons. These parties addressed that risk themselves, so the judicial gap-filler has nothing to do.
- The alternative holding on expectations is inferred from the contract rather than from testimony,
  and the strongest point is a negative inference: an express notice requirement makes no sense if an
  open process was assumed, and there is no obligation to disclose an **ongoing** process at all.
- The closing justification is about incentives ex ante. The OA "was drafted to attract capital
  investment, by allowing an exit on terms favorable to the investors," so implying an auction
  requirement "would deprive the Defendants of a negotiated-for benefit." The plaintiffs would be
  reclaiming by litigation what they sold to raise money.
- The entity-choice sentence is the opinion's frame, and it is placed where nobody can miss it: had
  they used the corporate form, "this matter would be subject to entire fairness review."

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The result is doctrinally clean and leaves a real question about what the implied covenant is for.
The covenant is mandatory under § 18-1101(c) — it is the one protection the statute refuses to let
parties waive — and yet the court's own framework makes it nearly unreachable wherever the drafters
were careful: careful drafting specifies the scope of discretion, specified scope means no gap, and
no gap means no covenant. A mandatory term that a competent drafter can render inoperative is closer
to a default than the statute's language suggests. Glasscock's answer is implicit in the losses-lie-
where-they-fall principle — the covenant polices unanticipated gaps, not bad bargains — but it does
mean that in the LLC context the protection is strongest precisely where it is least needed.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the entity-choice sentence.** "[I]f the parties had chosen to employ the corporate form
  here, with its common-law fiduciary duties, this matter would be subject to entire fairness review."
  Then say what they chose instead and what it cost them.
- **Run the implied covenant analysis in order.** Is there a **gap**? If the contract addresses the
  subject, stop — existing terms control. Only if silent, ask what the parties **would have agreed to**
  had they negotiated the point, measured at the time of contracting.
- **Have the specified-discretion rule ready.** Sole discretion ordinarily invites the covenant to
  require reasonable and good faith exercise; but "if the scope of discretion is specified, there is no
  gap in the contract as to the scope of the discretion." Here the scope was specified by the
  unaffiliated-third-party limit.
- **Explain why the obvious incentive defeated the claim.** The waterfall's perverse incentive was
  visible on the face of the OA when it was signed. The covenant fills gaps "neither party
  anticipated," not risks both parties could see.
- **Define good faith and fair dealing as the court does.** Faithfulness to the scope, purpose and
  terms of the contract; and "fair" is "something of a misnomer" meaning consonant with the agreement,
  not equitable. The covenant is not "a free-floating requirement that a party act in some morally
  commendable sense."
- **Quote the restraint principle.** Where an agreement eliminates fiduciary duties as part of a
  detailed governance scheme, it "implies an agreement that **losses should remain where they fall**."
- **Be ready for "but look at what they did."** The undisclosed calls, the litigation threats, the
  admission that a more open process should have been used — all assumed true, all irrelevant, because
  the analysis never reaches conduct. That is the point of the case.
- **Know the one non-waivable duty and why it did not save them.** Section 18-1101(c) makes the implied
  covenant mandatory, and the OA itself bound the managers to it. Mandatory is not the same as
  protective.
- **Numbers worth holding.** Class E $1,963,354 at 200% = $3,926,708; Class D $12,000,000 at 200% =
  $24,000,000; HCP taking the bulk of the first $30 million and 0% of the next dollars; four of seven
  managers; MTS at $31 million, then $41 million, then $43 million; a $53 million break-even for the
  preferred classes.
