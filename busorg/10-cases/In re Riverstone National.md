## Rule

Where a merger **extinguishes** a derivative claim against the directors who approved it,
and the directors are thereby "relieved of potential liability," they have received "a
material benefit from the merger **not shared by the common stockholders**." A board
majority so benefited is **interested**, entire fairness applies, and the surviving claim
— that the merger price was unfair because it did not account for the lost claim — is
**direct**.

## Facts

- Riverstone was the nation's second largest privately owned apartment property management
  company. CAS Capital, controlled by Nicholas and Peter Gould, was the **majority
  stockholder**; the Goulds also sat on the five-member board alongside Pearson, Danner
  (CEO) and Wanich (COO).
- After 2008 Riverstone moved toward single-family rentals and did business with Invitation
  Homes, which became the largest single-family rental company in the country.
- **Riverstone never received an ownership interest in Invitation Homes. Many of its
  directors and officers did.** At an October 2012 special meeting the board "acknowledged
  that certain members of the Board, officers and executives... may elect to acquire
  ownership interests in Invitation Homes," and in approving the acquisitions "purported to
  **waive any actual or potential conflict of interest**."
- **20 May 2014** — the plaintiffs told Riverstone the directors and officers had usurped the
  Invitation Homes opportunity and demanded the interests be assigned to the company.
- **30 May 2014** — the plaintiffs filed a **§ 220** books-and-records action. **The same
  day**, Riverstone executed the merger agreement with Greystar. *The day before, CAS
  Capital had already delivered its written consent as majority stockholder.* **The timing
  is the case.**
- The merger converted all shares into the right to receive a pro rata share of a **$94
  million** purchase price, less adjustments.
- **The merger agreement provided that the acquirer would release the usurpation claims**,
  and the board, CAS Capital and Greystar were all aware of those claims.
- The directors received the same per-share consideration as everyone else. *Argued as
  proportionality and rejected — they also received something nobody else did.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The complaint adequately pleads five linked propositions: a **chose in action** existed
  pre-merger for usurpation of corporate opportunity; a derivative claim on that ground
  "would have withstood a motion to dismiss"; the claim was **threatened and known to the
  board** when the merger was negotiated; the implied liability was **material** to the
  directors threatened; and the merger both eliminated the derivative suit **by operation of
  law** and eliminated pursuit of the claim as a corporate asset **by contract**.
- Therefore "a majority of the Defendant directors received a material benefit from the
  merger not shared by the common stockholders."
- That majority being **interested**, they "must demonstrate that the merger was entirely
  fair to the stockholders, in light of a plausible allegation of unfair price."
- **The surviving claim is direct.** "Any potential derivative actions have been
  extinguished; what remains are the Plaintiffs' allegations that the Defendant directors
  were interested in the merger and that the price was unfair, a direct claim belonging to
  the Plaintiffs."
- Motions to dismiss largely denied.

**Rejected**

- *The directors received exactly what every other stockholder received, pro rata, so there
  was no disproportionate benefit.* True as to the merger consideration and beside the point.
  The additional benefit was **escape from liability**, which no other stockholder shared.
- *The plaintiffs' real complaint is the usurpation, which is a derivative claim they lost
  standing to bring when the merger closed.* Correct, and that is why the claim is reframed.
  The lost derivative claim is not the injury; the **unfair price** that failed to account
  for it is, and that injury belongs to the former stockholders directly.

## Context

[[Tooley v. Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette|Tooley]] applied to the hardest recurring pattern in
derivative litigation. A merger strips a stockholder of standing to sue derivatively, because
standing requires continuous ownership under
[[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test|Rule 23.1]]. If that were the end of it, a conflicted board could
extinguish claims against itself by selling the company — and the buyer, as Greystar did
here, could contract away the claim as part of the price.

Riverstone closes that gap by **reclassifying**. The corporate asset is gone, so the
derivative claim is gone; but the failure to value that asset in the merger consideration is
a harm to the stockholders that does not depend on showing corporate injury. That satisfies
Tooley's first question, and the recovery would flow to the former stockholders, which
satisfies the second.

The interestedness analysis borrows directly from
[[Sinclair Oil v. Levien|Sinclair]]: a benefit "not shared by the common stockholders" is the
same test as something received "to the exclusion of, and detriment to, the minority," and the
same idea now appears in post-SB 21 § 144(e)(3). What differs is the **form** of the benefit —
not cash or property, but relief from exposure.

Note the pleading burden the court imposes. It is not enough that a claim existed; the
plaintiff must plead that it **would have survived dismissal** and that the exposure was
**material** to the directors. A weak or trivial claim extinguished by a merger does not make
a board interested.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Two counts for breach of fiduciary duty by former minority stockholders, on the defendants'
  motions to dismiss.
- **Posture shapes the whole opinion.** Every finding is a plausibility determination — that
  a claim existed, that it would have survived dismissal, that it was material. Glasscock
  calls the motions "rather simple to resolve" once the framing is right, which is the tell:
  the work is characterisation, not fact-finding.
- Largely denied, meaning the directors must prove entire fairness going forward.

### Facts

Riverstone managed apartment properties out of Dallas and was, by 2008, the second largest
privately owned firm of its kind. The financial crisis pushed it toward single-family homes —
buying depressed houses, rehabilitating them, leasing them and managing the leases — and into
a commercial relationship with Invitation Homes, a Delaware limited partnership formed in 2012
that became the largest single-family rental business in the country.

The opportunity to invest in Invitation Homes went to Riverstone's directors and officers
personally rather than to Riverstone. The board minuted the fact, acknowledged that members
might acquire interests, and purported to waive the conflict.

Two years later the minority stockholders moved. On 20 May 2014 they demanded that all
Invitation Homes interests held by directors, officers and affiliates be assigned to the
company. On 30 May they filed a § 220 action to get the books. That same day Riverstone signed
a merger agreement with Greystar — the majority stockholder having already consented in
writing the day before — under which the company would be bought for $94 million and the
acquirer would release the usurpation claims.

The minority received cash for their shares. The directors received cash for theirs, and the
disappearance of a claim against them.

### Issue

- Whether directors who approve a merger that extinguishes a threatened derivative claim
  against them thereby receive a material benefit not shared with other stockholders, making
  them interested.
- Whether the former stockholders' surviving claim is direct or derivative.

### Holding

- **On interestedness: yes.** Relief from material, non-frivolous potential liability is a
  benefit not shared by common stockholders, and a board majority so benefited is interested
  — triggering entire fairness on a plausible allegation of unfair price.
- **On classification: direct.** The derivative claims are extinguished; what remains is the
  stockholders' own allegation that the price was unfair because it did not account for the
  extinguished asset, which they can prove without showing corporate injury and for which
  recovery would flow to them.
- **Disposition:** motions to dismiss largely denied.

### Rule

- A merger extinguishes derivative standing, because Rule 23.1 requires continuous ownership.
- Directors relieved by a merger of material potential liability receive a benefit **not
  shared by the common stockholders**.
- A board majority receiving such a benefit is interested, and must prove entire fairness where
  unfair price is plausibly alleged.
- To plead that benefit, a plaintiff must allege that the extinguished claim existed, that it
  **would have withstood a motion to dismiss**, that it was threatened and known to the board,
  and that the implied liability was **material** to the directors.
- The resulting attack on the merger's fairness is a **direct** claim belonging to the former
  stockholders.

### Reasoning

*(Glasscock, V.C.)*

- The opinion's method is to separate the asset from the injury. The chose in action was a
  corporate asset, and its loss was a corporate harm — but the plaintiffs are not suing for
  that. They are suing because the consideration they personally received failed to reflect it.
- The materiality and merits requirements are what keep the doctrine from swallowing every
  merger. Any board faces some theoretical exposure; only a threatened, viable, material claim
  makes directors interested.
- The dual elimination is treated as decisive: the merger killed the derivative suit **by
  operation of law**, and the merger agreement killed the corporate asset **by contract**.
  Either alone might be argued away; together they show the value went somewhere other than the
  stockholders.
- The conflict waiver at the October 2012 meeting does no work for the defendants. Purporting
  to waive a conflict is not the same as cleansing a transaction, and the court does not treat
  it as either.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None.
- The counter-position worth two lines: on this reasoning almost any merger approved by a board
  facing a credible threatened claim becomes an entire fairness transaction, which gives any
  disgruntled stockholder a way to convert a sale into fiduciary litigation simply by sending a
  demand letter first. The court's answer is the pleading burden — the claim must have been
  viable enough to survive dismissal and material enough to matter to the directors — but
  whether that filter is demanding enough is a fair question.

### Cold-call notes

- **The question to expect is what benefit the directors received**, since they took the same
  per-share price as everyone else. The answer is escape from liability, and the phrase to use
  is "a material benefit not shared by the common stockholders."
- **Have the four pleading requirements.** Claim existed; would have survived dismissal;
  threatened and known to the board; material to the directors. Without those the argument
  proves too much.
- **The timing is the memorable fact.** The § 220 action and the merger agreement were signed
  the same day, and the majority stockholder had consented the day before.
- **Be precise on classification.** The usurpation claim is derivative and is gone. The unfair
  price claim is direct and survives. Saying the plaintiffs are "still bringing the derivative
  claim" is the most likely wrong answer.
- **Connect it back to Sinclair.** "[N]ot shared by the common stockholders" is Sinclair's
  disproportion test applied to a benefit that takes the form of relief from exposure rather
  than of property.
- **Numbers:** five directors, $94 million purchase price, demand 20 May 2014, § 220 suit and
  merger agreement both 30 May 2014.
