## Rule

Whether a claim is direct or derivative "must be based **solely** on the following
questions: **Who suffered the alleged harm** — the corporation or the suing stockholder
individually — **and who would receive the benefit of the recovery** or other remedy?"

The concept of **"special injury" is disavowed**.

## Facts

The casebook excerpt carries the legal holding rather than the underlying dispute. The
operative facts are doctrinal:

- The lower court had applied a **"special injury"** requirement: a direct claim needed a
  wrong "separate and distinct from that suffered by other shareholders, ... or a wrong
  involving a contractual right of a shareholder, such as the right to vote, or to assert
  majority control, which exists independently of any right of the corporation."
  *Disavowed.*
- Chancellor Chandler had, in the contemporaneous *Agostino* decision, urged abandoning
  "special injury" and reframing the inquiry as whether the plaintiff "can prevail
  **without showing an injury to the corporation**." *Adopted.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The analysis rests on two questions and nothing else: who suffered the harm, and who
  would receive the benefit of the recovery.
- **"Special injury" is abandoned** as "amorphous and confusing." Cases had "complicated"
  a simple analysis by injecting it.
- The first prong is best framed as the Chancellor put it: "[l]ooking at the body of the
  complaint and considering the nature of the wrong alleged and the relief requested, has
  the plaintiff demonstrated that he or she can prevail **without showing an injury to the
  corporation**?" The second prong "should logically follow."
- The governing formulation remains that of *Grimes v. Donald*: "a court should look to the
  nature of the wrong and to whom the relief should go. The stockholder's claimed direct
  injury must be **independent** of any alleged injury to the corporation."
- The stockholder must show **both** that the duty breached was owed to the stockholder and
  that he or she can prevail without showing corporate injury.
- **Why a clear test matters**: the classification "may be outcome-determinative," so "it is
  necessary that a standard to distinguish such actions be clear, simple and consistently
  articulated and applied."

**Rejected**

- *A direct claim requires an injury separate and distinct from that suffered by other
  shareholders.* Disavowed. A harm shared by all stockholders can still be direct if it is
  independent of corporate injury — otherwise every widely shared wrong would be derivative
  by definition.

## Context

The gateway to the whole of derivative litigation, and the reason it is assigned first. The
classification carries three consequences the opinion lists: a derivative plaintiff must
**retain ownership** throughout the litigation, must **make presuit demand** on the board
(or plead futility), and must obtain **court approval of any settlement** — all under
[[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test|Rule 23.1]]. And "the recovery, if any, flows only to the corporation."

That last point is why the test is more consequential than it looks. A stockholder who wins
a derivative claim receives nothing directly; the corporation is made whole and the
stockholder benefits only pro rata through share value. A plaintiff with a direct claim
recovers personally and never faces demand futility at all.

[[In re Riverstone National]], assigned the same day, is Tooley applied to the hardest
recurring pattern: a merger that extinguishes a derivative claim, where the classification
determines whether anything survives at all.

Note what the test is **not**. It is not about how many stockholders were hurt, and it is not
about whether the defendant is a fiduciary. It is two questions about harm and remedy.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Delaware Supreme Court, on appeal from the Court of Chancery.
- The casebook presents the opinion as a statement of law rather than a fact-bound
  adjudication, which is how it functions in practice — it is cited for its two questions.

### Facts

The excerpt gives no sustained factual narrative. What matters is the doctrinal posture: the
Court of Chancery had been applying a "special injury" requirement drawn from earlier cases,
under which a direct claim needed a wrong distinct from that suffered by stockholders
generally, or a wrong to a contractual stockholder right such as voting or majority control.
Chancellor Chandler, in *Agostino*, had criticised the concept and proposed replacing it with
a question about independence from corporate injury. The Supreme Court took the opportunity
to settle the standard.

### Issue

- What test determines whether a stockholder's claim is direct or derivative.
- Whether "special injury" survives as an element of a direct claim.

### Holding

- **On the test: two questions.** Who suffered the harm — corporation or stockholder — and
  who would receive the benefit of the recovery.
- **On special injury: abandoned.** The concept is amorphous, confusing, and no part of the
  analysis.
- The stockholder must show the duty breached was owed to the stockholder and that he or she
  can prevail without showing injury to the corporation.

### Rule

- The direct/derivative analysis rests solely on who suffered the harm and who would receive
  the recovery.
- A stockholder's claimed direct injury must be **independent** of any injury to the
  corporation.
- The stockholder must demonstrate that the duty breached was owed to the stockholder.
- "Special injury" is not part of the test.
- A derivative suit is brought on the corporation's behalf and any recovery goes to the
  corporation; a direct suit recovers for the stockholder.
- A derivative plaintiff must retain share ownership throughout, make presuit demand, and
  obtain court approval of any settlement.

### Reasoning

*(Veasey, C.J.)*

- The move is one of simplification rather than of substance: the two questions were "well
  imbedded in our jurisprudence," and the opinion's work is removing an accretion.
- "Special injury" fails because it answers a different question — how many people were hurt
  — rather than the one that matters, whether the stockholder's injury depends on the
  corporation's.
- The Chancellor's reformulation is adopted because it is operational: ask whether the
  plaintiff can prevail **without** showing corporate injury, and the answer decides prong
  one.
- The justification is explicitly administrative. Because classification is often
  outcome-determinative and expensive to get wrong, the standard must be "clear, simple and
  consistently articulated."

### Dissent / concurrence

- None.
- The tension worth a line: dropping "special injury" makes the test cleaner but leaves the
  hardest cases — dilution, voting-power shifts, merger consideration — turning on a
  characterisation of "independence" that can be argued either way. *Gentile*-type dual-natured
  claims are the standing evidence that two questions do not always produce one answer.

### Cold-call notes

- **Have the two questions verbatim.** Who suffered the harm; who receives the benefit of
  recovery. Any answer that begins elsewhere is already off track.
- **The trap is "special injury."** It is the natural thing to say and it is precisely what
  Tooley abolishes. If a hypothetical invites you to ask whether this stockholder was hurt
  differently from the others, that is the wrong question.
- **Know why it matters practically.** Derivative means Rule 23.1 — continuous ownership,
  demand or futility, court-approved settlement — and recovery to the corporation. Direct
  means none of that.
- **Be ready for the hard middle.** Claims that look like both are the exam's favourite
  ground, and [[In re Riverstone National]] is the assigned example: a merger extinguishes
  the derivative claim, and what survives is a direct attack on price.
