## Rule

Enhanced scrutiny and the duty to seek **the best value reasonably available** attach
whenever a transaction will cause **a change in corporate control or a break-up of
the entity** — a break-up is not required — because once control passes from a fluid
aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders to a single holder, the public stockholders
lose forever the leverage to demand another control premium.

## Facts

- Before the deal, **Paramount's public stockholders in the aggregate owned a
  majority** of the voting stock, so "[c]ontrol of the corporation is not vested in a
  single person, entity, or group, but vested in the **fluid aggregation of
  unaffiliated stockholders**." Afterward, Redstone would control Viacom and therefore
  Paramount. That shift is the trigger.
- Redstone controlled roughly **85.2% of Viacom's voting Class A stock** through
  National Amusements, and Paramount holders would receive mostly **non-voting Class B**
  stock — a minority equity position with no votes, behind a controlling holder.
- The **Stock Option Agreement** gave Viacom an option on about **19.9% (23,699,000
  shares) at $69.14**, triggered by the same events as the termination fee, and was
  **not capped**. Two unusual features: Viacom could pay with "a senior subordinated
  note of questionable marketability" instead of the $1.6 billion in cash (the Note
  Feature), and could instead require Paramount to pay cash equal to the spread
  between exercise price and market (the Put Feature). It was worth over **$200
  million** when QVC launched and nearly **$500 million** at the $90 bid.
- The **No-Shop Provision** barred Paramount from soliciting, encouraging, discussing,
  negotiating or endorsing any competing transaction unless a third party made an
  unsolicited written bona fide proposal with no material financing contingencies and
  the board determined that talks were necessary to its fiduciary duties.
- A **$100 million termination fee**, which "whether or not unreasonable by itself,
  clearly made Paramount less attractive to other bidders, when coupled with the Stock
  Option Agreement."
- **The October 23–24 renegotiation is the pivotal fact.** QVC's hostile $80 bid had
  given Paramount "considerable leverage with Viacom," and the board used it to improve
  price — but "there is no evidence in the record that Paramount sought to use its
  newly-acquired leverage to eliminate or modify the No–Shop Provision, the Termination
  Fee, or the Stock Option Agreement." Change the answer to that question and the case
  comes out differently.
- Davis told Diller over lunch on July 21 that **Paramount was not for sale**, having
  just learned of QVC's potential interest.
- Redstone described the deal as a "marriage" that would "never be torn asunder" and
  said only a "nuclear attack" could break it, and called Diller and John Malone **to
  dissuade them from bidding**.
- On **November 12 QVC's offer exceeded Viacom's by over $1 billion** at then-current
  values. The board rejected it on November 15 as "excessively conditional," without
  ever contacting QVC about the conditions, believing the No-Shop forbade it — and
  after receiving an Oresman memorandum on "conditions and uncertainties" that one
  director said gave him "a very negative impression."
- The only quantitative comparison put before the board used **then-current market
  prices** rather than anticipated values at closing, even though those prices
  "constantly fluctuated depending upon which company was perceived to be the more
  likely to acquire Paramount."
- **Both** the Viacom and QVC tender offers were 51% cash with a securities back end,
  so **both were two-tiered, front-end loaded, and coercive** — a factor the board was
  obliged to weigh and did not.
- On November 19, three days after the injunction hearing, Diller wrote confirming QVC
  had **financing commitments and no antitrust obstacle**. *The conditionality the board
  relied on was resolvable by asking.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **A sale of control triggers enhanced scrutiny; a break-up is not required.** "[W]hen
  a corporation undertakes a transaction which will cause: (a) a change in corporate
  control; or (b) a break-up of the corporate entity, the directors' obligation is to
  seek the best value reasonably available to the stockholders."
- The reason is the permanent loss of leverage. When a majority of voting shares is
  acquired by one person or a cohesive group, "there is a significant diminution in
  the voting power of those who thereby become minority stockholders," who can then be
  cashed out and must rely solely on fiduciary duties. "The acquisition of majority
  status... come[s] at a price. That price is usually a **control premium**."
- "Once control has shifted, the current Paramount stockholders will have **no leverage
  in the future to demand another control premium**," so they "are entitled to receive,
  and should receive, a control premium and/or protective devices of significant
  value."
- Enhanced scrutiny here is mandated by three things: "(a) the threatened diminution
  of the current stockholders' voting power; (b) the fact that an asset belonging to
  public stockholders (a control premium) is being sold and may never be available
  again; and (c) the traditional concern of Delaware courts for actions which impair or
  impede stockholder voting rights."
- **The key features of enhanced scrutiny**: "(a) a judicial determination regarding
  the adequacy of the decisionmaking process employed by the directors, including the
  information on which the directors based their decision; and (b) a judicial
  examination of the reasonableness of the directors' action in light of the
  circumstances then existing. **The directors have the burden of proving that they
  were adequately informed and acted reasonably.**"
- The standard is **reasonableness, not perfection**: "a court applying enhanced
  judicial scrutiny should be deciding whether the directors made a reasonable
  decision, not a perfect decision. If a board selected one of several reasonable
  alternatives, a court should not second-guess that choice," but will determine
  whether the decision "was, on balance, within a range of reasonableness."
- In assessing best value a board "is not limited to considering only the amount of
  cash involved, and is not required to ignore totally its view of the future value of
  a strategic alliance," but "[w]here stock or other non-cash consideration is
  involved, the board should try to **quantify its value, if feasible**, to achieve an
  objective comparison." It may weigh "fairness and feasibility; the proposed or actual
  financing... questions of illegality;... the risk of non-consum[m]ation;... the
  bidder's identity, prior background and other business venture experiences; and the
  bidder's business plans."
- **Contractual provisions cannot limit fiduciary duties.** The No-Shop and similar
  provisions, "whether or not they are presumptively valid in the abstract, may not
  validly define or limit the directors' fiduciary duties under Delaware law or prevent
  the Paramount directors from carrying out their fiduciary duties. To the extent such
  provisions are inconsistent with those duties, they are **invalid and
  unenforceable**."
- **The breaches.** The board "clearly gave insufficient attention to the potential
  consequences of the defensive measures demanded by Viacom." The uncapped Stock Option
  Agreement had "unusual and potentially 'draconian' provisions"; the Termination Fee
  made Paramount less attractive when coupled with it; the No-Shop inhibited
  negotiation with QVC.
- Having decided to sell control, the board had a **continuing obligation** — at the
  October 24 meeting "and thereafter" — to evaluate whether the QVC offer was or would
  remain conditional, whether it could be improved, whether the Viacom terms could be
  improved, whether each offer was likely to close, what other information was
  available, whether other courses existed, and whether the timing could be managed.
- The board "made no effort to eliminate or modify these counterproductive devices, and
  instead continued to cling to its vision of a strategic alliance with Viacom." The
  over-$1 billion disparity "cannot be justified on the basis of the directors' vision
  of future strategy, primarily because the change of control would supplant the
  authority of the current Paramount Board to continue to hold and implement their
  strategic vision in any meaningful way. Moreover, their uninformed process had
  deprived their strategic vision of much of its credibility."
- On November 15 the directors "remained prisoners of their own misconceptions and
  missed opportunities to eliminate the restrictions they had imposed on themselves,"
  and "remained paralyzed by their uninformed belief that the QVC offer was 'illusory'."
- The process was not reasonable and the result was not reasonable. **Affirmed.**

**Rejected**

- *Revlon requires a break-up, and Time–Warner so held, so no enhanced scrutiny applies
  absent a corporate break-up.* An "erroneous interpretation" of both decisions. That
  a break-up had become a reality "which even the directors embraced" was only **one of
  the circumstances** in Revlon, and Revlon itself said the enhanced duties apply "when
  bidders make relatively similar offers, **or** dissolution of the company becomes
  inevitable." Requiring both a change of control and a break-up "would unduly restrict
  the application of Revlon... and has no basis in policy."
- *Time–Warner is controlling because a strategic combination was involved there too.*
  The facts "were quite different... and refute Paramount's position." In Time the
  Chancellor found no change of control precisely because Time "would be owned by a
  fluid aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders both before and after the merger" —
  the opposite of what the Viacom transaction would do to Paramount.
- *The board's considered judgment that a Viacom alliance offered greater long-term
  strategic value justifies accepting a bid over $1 billion lower.* The strategic
  vision could not survive the transaction that was supposed to implement it, since the
  change of control would supplant the board's authority to carry it out; and the
  board's uninformed process had drained the vision of credibility.
- *The No-Shop Provision legally prevented Paramount from talking to QVC.* Such
  provisions cannot define or limit fiduciary duties and are invalid and unenforceable
  to the extent they conflict with them. The court reserves whether a no-shop "could
  validly have operated here at an early stage solely to prevent Paramount from actively
  'shopping' the company," but it could not bar the directors from considering
  unsolicited bids or negotiating for best value.
- *Viacom had vested contract rights in the No-Shop and the Stock Option Agreement.*
  "Viacom's argument is that the Paramount directors could enter into an agreement in
  violation of their fiduciary duties and then render Paramount, and ultimately its
  stockholders, liable for failing to carry out an agreement in violation of those
  duties. Viacom's protestations about vested rights are without merit." A
  sophisticated party that knew of and demanded the unreasonable features "cannot be
  now heard to argue that it obtained vested contract rights."

## Context

QVC completes the Revlon trigger. [[Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes]] located the duty in
the inevitability of break-up; [[Paramount v. Time]] read that as a requirement; QVC
holds it is not, and supplies the modern disjunctive test — **change of control or
break-up**. The unifying rationale is not the form of the transaction but the loss of
something the public stockholders own: the ability to command a control premium in some
future deal, which exists only while control sits with a fluid aggregation of
unaffiliated holders and disappears the moment it does not.

That is also the cleanest way to reconcile the two Paramounts, which otherwise look
irreconcilable. Time keeps control dispersed and the board wins; Paramount hands control
to Redstone and the board loses. The board's subjective devotion to a strategic vision
is identical in both; what differs is whether the transaction destroys the stockholders'
option to sell control later.

The case is also the best single statement of **what enhanced scrutiny actually
requires**, and the passage is worth carrying whole: adequacy of the decision-making
process including the information relied on, reasonableness of the action in the
circumstances, with the burden on the directors — and a court deciding whether the
decision was reasonable, not perfect. That formulation is quoted in
[[In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation]] thirty years later as the content of
Revlon review.

On the deal protection side QVC supplies three propositions that run straight into
[[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] and
[[Deal protection devices - Unocal review of merger lock-ups]]. A contractual provision
cannot define or limit fiduciary duties. A sophisticated counterparty that demanded an
unreasonable provision takes no vested right in it. And an **uncapped** option is the
particular danger, because it grows with the competing bid — the Paramount option went
from $200 million to nearly $500 million precisely as QVC bid higher, so the device
punished exactly the outcome the stockholders wanted.

The most useful teaching fact is the **failure to renegotiate the protections**. The
board did use QVC's bid as leverage; it extracted a better price on October 24 and left
the Stock Option, Termination Fee and No-Shop untouched. Enhanced scrutiny is a
continuing obligation, and a board that reopens a deal must reopen the parts that
impede competing bids, not only the parts that raise its own price.

Footnote 119 is a small gem worth keeping: **both** tender offers were 51% cash with a
securities back end, so both "were two-tiered, front-end loaded, and coercive. Such
coercive offers are inherently problematic and should be expected to receive
particularly careful analysis by a target board." The structure Unocal condemned in
[[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] had by 1994 become the ordinary form of a friendly deal.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from the Court of Chancery's November 24, 1993 order preliminarily enjoining
  the defensive measures protecting the Paramount–Viacom transaction.
- Brought by QVC as a competing bidder and by Paramount stockholders.
- Affirmed.
- Posture is central twice over. This is a **pre-closing injunction** decided on an
  expedited record, which is exactly the setting Revlon and Unocal were built for; and
  because the remedy is injunctive, the court can invalidate contractual provisions
  rather than award damages.

### Facts

Paramount was a widely held Delaware corporation with about 118 million shares
outstanding, running motion picture and television studios, book publishers,
professional sports teams and amusement parks. Its board had fifteen members, four of
them officers — including Martin Davis, chairman and chief executive since 1983 — and
eleven outside directors who were present or former senior executives of public
companies or financial institutions.

Viacom was controlled by Sumner Redstone, who held indirectly about 85.2% of its voting
Class A stock and 69.2% of its non-voting Class B through National Amusements, an entity
91.7% owned by him. QVC, a televised shopping company, was led by Barry Diller and
backed by co-investors including BellSouth and Comcast.

Paramount had been looking for a combination since the late 1980s and had made an
unsuccessful run at Time in 1989. Talks with Viacom restarted at an April 20, 1993
dinner between Redstone and Davis and became serious in July, but broke down over price
— Viacom offering about $61 a share, Paramount wanting at least $70 — and over the stock
option. Two things then happened. Davis learned of QVC's potential interest and told
Diller over lunch on July 21 that Paramount was not for sale. And Viacom's Class B stock
rose from $46.875 on July 6 to $57.25 on August 20, which QVC attributed to open-market
buying by Redstone.

Talks resumed August 20 and the Paramount board unanimously approved the Original Merger
Agreement on September 12: each Paramount share would convert into 0.10 Viacom Class A
voting shares, 0.90 Class B non-voting shares, and $9.10 cash. The board amended its
poison pill to exempt the merger and agreed to three protective provisions — the
No-Shop, a $100 million Termination Fee, and the Stock Option Agreement on 19.9% of
Paramount's stock at $69.14, uncapped, with the Note Feature and the Put Feature.

The parties called the deal a certainty. Redstone described a "marriage" that would
"never be torn asunder," said only a "nuclear attack" could break it, and telephoned
Diller and John Malone of Tele-Communications to dissuade a competing bid.

Diller wrote anyway on September 20, proposing roughly $80 a share in stock and cash and
expressing eagerness to negotiate. At the September 27 board meeting Davis advised that
the Original Merger Agreement barred discussions with QVC unless certain conditions were
met, in particular evidence that QVC's proposal carried no financing contingencies. QVC
supplied that evidence on October 5; on October 11 the board authorised management to
meet with QVC and retained Booz-Allen to assess the incremental earnings potential of
each combination. Discussions moved slowly because Paramount delayed signing a
confidentiality agreement.

On October 21 QVC sued and announced an $80 cash tender offer for 51% of Paramount's
shares, with a 1.42857-share back end, conditioned among other things on invalidation of
the Stock Option Agreement, then worth over $200 million. Within hours Viacom opened
talks to raise its bid, and on October 24 the board approved an Amended Merger Agreement
— an $80 cash tender offer by Viacom for 51%, improved merger consideration, a right not
to exempt Viacom from the Rights Agreement if another offer was a "better alternative,"
and a right to terminate if the board withdrew its recommendation. **The three defensive
provisions were left untouched.**

Viacom's tender offer commenced October 25 and QVC's October 27. Diller asked to
negotiate; a November 1 meeting went nowhere after Paramount rejected QVC's proposed
"fair bidding process" guidelines as inappropriate and contrary to its obligations to
Viacom. Viacom raised to $85 on November 6 and the board recommended it. QVC raised to
$90 on November 12, exceeding Viacom by over $1 billion at then-current values.

Before the November 15 meeting Oresman circulated a memorandum summarising the
"conditions and uncertainties" of QVC's offer; one director testified it gave him a very
negative impression. The board found the QVC offer not in the stockholders' best
interests because it was excessively conditional, and did not contact QVC about the
status of those conditions because it believed the No-Shop forbade it. Several directors
testified they thought Viacom better for Paramount's future business prospects. The only
quantitative comparison used then-current market prices rather than anticipated values
at closing.

The injunction hearing was November 16. On November 19 Diller wrote that QVC had
financing commitments and no antitrust obstacle. Chancery enjoined on November 24.

### Issue

- Whether the Paramount–Viacom transaction, which would deliver control of Paramount to
  Redstone, triggers enhanced judicial scrutiny and the obligation to seek the best value
  reasonably available, absent any break-up of the corporation.
- Whether Time–Warner established that a break-up is a precondition to that obligation.
- Whether the Paramount board's process and result were reasonable, given the untouched
  deal protections and its refusal to engage with QVC.
- Whether a No-Shop Provision can bar directors from negotiating, and whether Viacom
  acquired vested contract rights in provisions obtained from a board acting in breach.

### Holding

- **On the trigger: a change of control suffices.** Either a change in corporate control
  or a break-up imposes the obligation to seek best value, because each is a fundamental
  and perhaps irrevocable change, and because the control premium is an asset of the
  public stockholders that will never be available again.
- **On Time–Warner: distinguishable, not controlling.** Time remained owned by a fluid
  aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders before and after; Paramount would not.
- **On process: unreasonable.** The board gave insufficient attention to the defensive
  measures, failed to use its October leverage to modify them, treated the No-Shop as a
  legal bar to talking to QVC, never tested whether QVC's conditions were resolvable, and
  compared the offers on fluctuating market prices.
- **On result: unreasonable.** Accepting a bid over $1 billion lower cannot be justified
  by a strategic vision the transaction itself would extinguish.
- **On the contractual provisions: unenforceable.** They cannot define or limit fiduciary
  duties, and Viacom, which demanded them, holds no vested rights in them.
- **Disposition:** affirmed.

### Rule

- Enhanced scrutiny applies in two circumstances relevant here: approval of a transaction
  resulting in a sale of control, and adoption of defensive measures in response to a
  threat to corporate control.
- When a transaction will cause a change in corporate control or a break-up of the
  entity, the directors' obligation is to seek the best value reasonably available to the
  stockholders.
- Directors must act reasonably to that end, focusing on one primary objective, and act in
  accordance with their fundamental duties of care and loyalty.
- The key features of enhanced scrutiny are judicial review of the adequacy of the
  decision-making process, including the information relied on, and of the reasonableness
  of the action in the circumstances; the directors bear the burden on both.
- A court decides whether the directors made a reasonable decision, not a perfect one,
  and will not second-guess a choice among several reasonable alternatives.
- In comparing alternatives the board is not limited to cash, may weigh future strategic
  value, should quantify non-cash consideration where feasible, and may assess fairness,
  financing, legality, risk of non-consummation, the bidder's background and its plans.
- Contractual provisions may not validly define or limit directors' fiduciary duties, and
  are invalid and unenforceable to the extent they are inconsistent with them.
- A counterparty that negotiated for and obtained such provisions from a board acting in
  violation of its duties acquires no vested contract rights in them.

### Reasoning

*(Veasey, C.J.)*

- The opinion grounds the trigger in **what the stockholders lose** rather than in the
  form of the deal. Voting power is the statutory mechanism for every fundamental
  corporate change — electing directors, amending the charter, merging, selling the
  assets, dissolving — so a transaction that converts a majority into a minority takes
  away the only leverage minority holders have.
- From that premise the control premium follows as an **asset**, not a windfall. It
  compensates for the loss of voting power, it can be sold once, and after the sale "the
  current Paramount stockholders will have no leverage in the future to demand another
  control premium."
- Time–Warner is handled by returning to what the Chancellor actually held: no change of
  control, because ownership stayed with a fluid aggregation. The break-up language is
  recharacterised as one circumstance in Revlon rather than a requirement.
- Enhanced scrutiny is then defined with unusual precision, and the court pairs it with an
  express limit — reasonable, not perfect — because it is about to overturn a board's
  considered judgment and wants to mark the boundary.
- The strategic-vision argument is answered with its own internal contradiction. A board
  cannot justify a lower price by the long-term plan it means to implement when the
  transaction that delivers the plan also removes the board's power to carry it out.
- The process failures are treated cumulatively, and the October 23–24 renegotiation is
  the fulcrum: leverage was obtained, used on price, and not used on the provisions that
  were suppressing the competing bid.
- On the contractual points the court reasons from priority rather than from
  interpretation. Fiduciary duties are not terms a board can bargain away, so a provision
  inconsistent with them is not a hard case about construction — it is simply
  unenforceable, and a counterparty that demanded it knew what it was getting.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion does leave a real practical tension for deal lawyers, and footnote 120 is
where it shows. The court expressly reserves whether a no-shop might validly operate "at
an early stage solely to prevent Paramount from actively 'shopping' the company," while
holding that it could not prevent the directors from considering unsolicited bids or
negotiating for the best value. Where the line falls between a permissible bar on
soliciting and an impermissible bar on responding is left for another case, and it is the
question every merger agreement since has had to answer by drafting.

### Cold-call notes

- **State the trigger disjunctively.** Change in corporate control **or** break-up. If you
  say Revlon requires a break-up, you are stating the argument QVC rejects.
- **Have the fluid-aggregation test ready, and use it both ways.** Control in a fluid
  aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders before and after means no trigger (Time);
  control passing to one holder means trigger (QVC). Then give the reason: the control
  premium is an asset that can only be sold once.
- **Quote the enhanced scrutiny definition.** Adequacy of the decision-making process
  including the information relied on, and reasonableness of the action in the
  circumstances, with the burden on the directors. Add the limit — reasonable, not
  perfect — because it is what separates this from de novo review.
- **Lead the breach analysis with the failure to renegotiate.** The board had leverage on
  October 24 and spent it entirely on price. Enhanced scrutiny is a continuing obligation,
  and the defensive provisions were the thing suppressing the higher bid.
- **Know why "excessively conditional" failed as a reason.** The board never asked QVC
  about the conditions, believing the No-Shop forbade it, and QVC confirmed financing and
  antitrust clearance three days after the hearing. A condition you never test is not a
  finding.
- **Be precise about why the option was draconian.** It was **uncapped**, so it grew with
  the competing bid — $200 million at QVC's $80, nearly $500 million at $90 — plus the
  Note Feature and the Put Feature. The device punished exactly the outcome stockholders
  wanted.
- **Have the two contract propositions ready.** A provision cannot define or limit
  fiduciary duties, and a sophisticated counterparty that demanded it takes no vested
  rights. Both reappear in [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]].
- **Know footnote 119.** Both offers were 51% cash with a securities back end, hence
  two-tiered, front-end loaded and coercive — the same structure Unocal condemned, now
  standard on both sides.
- **Numbers worth holding.** 118 million shares; 85.2% Redstone control of Viacom's voting
  stock; 19.9% option on 23,699,000 shares at $69.14, worth $200 million then nearly $500
  million; $100 million termination fee; QVC at $80, $90; Viacom at $80, $85; a
  $1 billion-plus gap on November 12.
