## Rule

A board acting in good faith, after reasonable investigation and on the advice of
outside advisors, may keep a poison pill in place indefinitely against a
**structurally non-coercive, all-cash, fully financed, all-shares** tender offer,
because inadequate price is itself a legally cognisable threat in the form of
**substantive coercion** — and a pill combined with a staggered board is **not
preclusive** so long as obtaining board control at some future annual meeting remains
realistically attainable.

## Facts

- Air Products' offer was the **antonym of structural coercion**: all shares, all
  cash, backed by secured financing, regulatory approval obtained, the front end and
  back end at the same price in the same currency, and no interest in owning less
  than 100%. "[I]f there were an antonym in the dictionary for 'structural coercion,'
  Air Products' offer might be it."
- The offer had been public for **sixteen months** and the $70 "best and final" for
  just over two, so the Airgas board had more time "than any litigated poison pill in
  Delaware history."
- **Air Products' own three nominees were elected to the Airgas board** at the
  September 15, 2010 annual meeting, on an express platform of taking a fresh look —
  and then **joined the board's rejection unanimously**. One of them, Clancey, said
  "We have to protect the pill." That reversal is the fact that carries the opinion.
- Those nominees obtained **their own independent counsel (Skadden)** and forced the
  board to retain a **third independent financial advisor (Credit Suisse)**, using a
  public letter that an evidentiary hearing revealed was "meant as leverage" — "It's
  like playing poker. We put our chips up on the table, everything we had."
- The board was **nine of ten directors independent**, advised by **three** outside
  financial advisors and two law firms. Prong one on process was "undeniable."
- The board believed in good faith that Airgas was worth **at least $78** in a sale;
  Air Products' best and final was **$70**, up $10 from its opening public bid.
- Airgas's defences were a **staggered board** (three classes, three directors elected
  annually), a **15%-trigger poison pill**, and a **charter supermajority provision**
  requiring 67% approval for a merger with a 20%-plus Interested Stockholder unless
  approved by disinterested directors or fair price and procedure requirements are met.
- The Airgas **stockholder base was sophisticated and well informed**, and both the
  directors and their advisors conceded it had all the information needed to decide.
  *That concession is what makes the finding of substantive coercion so uncomfortable,
  and the court says so.*
- Airgas showed **four quarters of improving financial results** during the delay, and
  the pill helped push Air Products up by **$10 per share**.
- Air Products chose to run nominees promising to "consider without any bias" the
  offer, rather than a slate promising to redeem the pill or running on shareholder
  choice. *Argued and used against it — "Air Products got what it wanted."*
- Airgas asserted many threats — coercion, opportunistic timing, a prisoner's dilemma,
  the need to protect merger arbitrageurs from themselves. **"[T]he only threat that
  the board discussed... is the inadequate price."** *The rest are rejected as
  litigation-generated.*
- Airgas argued Unocal should not apply once the bidder's own nominees agreed with the
  incumbents. *Argued and rejected — "simply an incorrect statement of the law."*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **A board cannot "just say no" to a tender offer.** It "must first pass through two
  prongs of exacting judicial scrutiny by a judge who will evaluate the actions taken
  by, and the motives of, the board."
- **Unocal, not the business judgment rule, applies**, and the agreement of the
  bidder's own nominees does not change that. "Because the Airgas board is taking
  defensive action in response to a pending takeover bid, the 'theoretical specter of
  disloyalty' does exist."
- **Prong one is not satisfied by process alone.** "[N]o matter how exemplary the
  board's process, or how independent the board, or how reasonable its investigation,
  to meet their burden under the first prong of Unocal defendants must actually
  articulate some legitimate threat to corporate policy and effectiveness." The
  process "has to lead to the finding of a threat."
- Airgas's process was unimpeachable — an independent board, three outside financial
  advisors, two law firms.
- **The offer is not structurally coercive**, and poses **no threat of opportunity
  loss**, since after sixteen months there is no alternative on the table and counsel
  conceded Airgas was not asking for more time to explore one.
- **Substantive coercion is a valid threat, and inadequate price is a form of it.**
  It is "the risk that [Airgas's] stockholders might accept [Air Products']
  inadequate Offer because of 'ignorance or mistaken belief' regarding the Board's
  assessment of the long-term value of [Airgas's] stock" — the concept the Supreme
  Court embraced in [[Paramount v. Time]].
- The Supreme Court "has unequivocally endorse[d the] conclusion that it is not a
  breach of faith for directors to determine that the present stock market price of
  shares is not representative of true value," and a board that in good faith believes
  an offer inadequate "may properly employ a poison pill as a proportionate defensive
  response to protect its stockholders from a 'low ball' bid."
- **Prong two, coercion:** the defences are not coercive, because Airgas is not
  cramming down a management-sponsored alternative but "simply wants to maintain the
  status quo and manage the company for the long term."
- **Prong two, preclusion:** bound by Versata v. Selectica, which held that "the
  combination of a classified board and a Rights Plan do not constitute a preclusive
  defense," and that making control "more difficult" does not make it "realistically
  unattainable." Waiting until the next annual meeting "delay[s]—but [does] not
  prevent—[Air Products] from obtaining control of the board."
- **Prong two, range of reasonableness:** the board is responding to a legitimately
  articulated threat, its view is bolstered by the bidder's own nominees joining it,
  and Air Products made a tactical choice in the slate it ran. "[D]irectors, when
  acting deliberately, in an informed way, and in the good faith pursuit of corporate
  interests, may follow a course designed to achieve long-term value even at the cost
  of immediate value maximization."
- "[I]n order to have any effectiveness, pills do not—and can not—have a set
  expiration date. To be clear, though, **this case does not endorse 'just say
  never.'**" Directors still owe duties to short-term as well as long-term holders,
  but "a board cannot be forced into [selling] mode any time a hostile bidder makes a
  tender offer that is at a premium to market value."
- Relief denied; all claims dismissed with prejudice.

**Rejected**

- *Unocal should not apply where the bidder's own nominees, after advice from a new
  investment banker, agree with the incumbent directors.* "That is simply an incorrect
  statement of the law." Defensive action in response to a pending bid always carries
  the theoretical spectre of disloyalty, which is the reason the intermediate standard
  exists.
- *Air Products' offer is coercive, opportunistically timed, presents a prisoner's
  dilemma, and endangers a minority that must be protected from merger arbitrageurs.*
  The only threat the board itself discussed was inadequate price. "In the end, it
  really is 'All About Value'."
- *An inadequate all-cash, all-shares offer with an equal back end is a continuing
  threat.* The court agrees with Justice Berger's contrary view and says it has "a hard
  time believing that inadequate price alone... poses any 'threat'," especially given
  the information available — but "under existing Delaware law, it apparently does."
- *The combination of a staggered board and a pill is preclusive, because no bidder has
  ever waged two successful proxy contests to remove one.* Selectica forecloses the
  argument. The court records the practical reality in a footnote and defers anyway.
- *Substantive coercion should be confined, as Chancellor Allen and Vice Chancellor
  Strine have argued, so that there is an end game at which stockholders are trusted to
  decide.* "[W]hile I agree theoretically with former-Chancellor Allen's and Vice
  Chancellor Strine's conception of substantive coercion and its appropriate
  application, the Supreme Court's dictum in Paramount... suggests that, unless and
  until the Supreme Court rules otherwise, that is not the current state of our law."

## Context

Airgas is where the takeover line arrives, and the interest of the opinion is that
**the judge disagrees with the result he reaches**. Chandler says he has "a hard time
believing that inadequate price alone... poses any 'threat'," that in his "personal
view, Airgas's poison pill has served its legitimate purpose," that there "seems to be
no threat here," and that he does "not share the Airgas board's confidence" — and then
holds for Airgas because "I am constrained to deny" relief. Footnote 105 states it
flatly: "I am bound by Delaware Supreme Court precedent that, in my opinion, drives the
result I reach."

Three precedents do the constraining, and each comes from this chunk.

**[[Paramount v. Time]]** supplies substantive coercion — stockholders tendering "in
ignorance or a mistaken belief" about the board's strategy — and the dictum that
directors "are not obliged to abandon a deliberately conceived corporate plan for a
short-term shareholder profit unless there is clearly no basis to sustain the corporate
strategy," which Chandler calls "powerful dictum." Time also holds that the selection of
a time frame "may not be delegated to the stockholders."

**[[Moran v. Household International]]** supplies the pill itself, and the assurance
that "there are numerous methods to successfully launch a hostile tender offer."
Footnote 106 is Chandler's answer to that assurance, and it is the sharpest sentence in
the chunk: "Our law would be more credible if the Supreme Court acknowledged that its
later rulings have modified Moran and have allowed a board acting in good faith (and
with a reasonable basis for believing that a tender offer is inadequate) to remit the
bidder to the election process as its only recourse. The tender offer is in fact
precluded and the only bypass of the pill is electing a new board. If that is the law,
it would be best to be honest and abandon the pretense that preclusive action is per se
unreasonable."

**Selectica** supplies the preclusion holding. A classified board plus a pill is not
preclusive, because control remains "realistically attainable" at some future meeting —
even though, as Chandler notes, "no bidder to my knowledge has ever successfully stuck
around for two years and waged two successful proxy contests to gain control of a
classified board in order to remove a pill."

The **threat taxonomy** the opinion uses is the most exam-useful thing in it, because it
is the only place the three categories are laid out and applied in sequence.
**Structural coercion** — the two-tier front-loaded offer of
[[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] — is absent here by design. **Opportunity loss**, a
time-based threat that the defence forecloses a better alternative, is absent after
sixteen months with nothing else on the table. **Substantive coercion** — the risk that
informed stockholders will disbelieve the board and accept an underpriced offer — is
what remains, and it is enough.

The limits of the holding are real and worth stating. A pill cannot have a dead hand,
no hand or slow hand, because a newly elected board must be able to redeem — Carmody v.
Toll Brothers and Mentor Graphics v. Quickturn. "[T]his case does not endorse 'just say
never'." And the whole structure depends on the staggered board: without one, a single
proxy contest replaces the board and the pill comes down in a year.

Two developments since bear on how much of this survives in practice. Institutional
pressure has largely dismantled classified boards at large public companies, which
removes the second half of the Airgas combination. And the casebook note that follows
the case records the other half of the story: the sharp distinction between tender
offers, weakly protected under Unocal, and the stockholder franchise, robustly protected
under Blasius, "appears to have collapsed" in **Coster v. UIP Companies**, assigned in
session 21, which folds Blasius into a Unocal-shaped inquiry. Chunk K picks that up.

Airgas is also half of a pair. It is the high-water mark for the pill against a real
bidder; [[The Williams Companies Stockholder Litigation]], assigned in session 22, is
where a pill dies on a clear day against nobody. Read them together and the variable is
not board sincerity — intact in both — but whether the threat was **particular**. Williams
also answers a question Airgas leaves hanging: whether Moran's coercive/preclusive screen
is the whole of prong two. It is not, once the pill is adopted for a purpose Moran never
contemplated.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Post-trial opinion in the Court of Chancery, Chandler, C., after an October trial and
  a January supplemental evidentiary hearing.
- Air Products and Airgas stockholder plaintiffs sought an order compelling Airgas to
  redeem its pill and drop its other defences so that stockholders could decide on the
  $70 offer themselves.
- Relief denied; all claims dismissed with prejudice.
- The posture matters because the case arrives **after** a full election cycle. Air
  Products had already won three board seats, so the court is not speculating about
  whether the bidder could reach the stockholders — it knows, and it knows what
  happened when the bidder's own nominees got inside the boardroom.

### Facts

John McGlade of Air Products approached Peter McCausland, founder and chief executive of
Airgas, privately in mid-October 2009 about a combination. Rebuffed, Air Products went
public in February 2010 with a tender offer for all outstanding Airgas shares.

Airgas was led by a nine-member staggered board in three classes, one class elected each
year, all independent but McCausland. Its defences were the classified board, a
shareholder rights plan with a 15% trigger, and a charter provision requiring 67%
approval of any merger with a holder of 20% or more, unless approved by a majority of
disinterested directors or unless fair price and procedure requirements were satisfied.
Airgas had about 83.6 million shares outstanding, and its stock over the relevant period
ranged from $41.64 to $71.28.

Air Products moved up in stages — a $60 all-stock proposal, a $62 cash-and-stock
proposal, a public $60 all-cash proposal, and then a $60 all-cash tender offer for all
shares, non-coercive, non-discriminatory and backed by secured financing. Airgas rejected
each, filing that the offer "grossly undervalues Airgas" and that the timing was
"extremely opportunistic... in light of the depressed value of the Airgas Common shares,"
supported by inadequacy opinions from Goldman Sachs and Bank of America Merrill Lynch.

Air Products also nominated three independent directors for the 2010 annual meeting and
ran a protracted, high-visibility proxy contest, telling Airgas stockholders that "the
election of the Air Products Nominees... will establish an Airgas Board that is more
likely to act in your best interests" and promising the nominees would "consider without
any bias" the offer and "be willing to be outspoken in the boardroom." On September 15,
2010 all three were elected, unseating McCausland, Brown and Ill. Airgas then expanded
the board to ten and reappointed McCausland to the new seat.

Negotiations continued and failed; Airgas wanted $78, and after one meeting the parties
announced that "no further meetings are planned." The board stated publicly and in court
that it was unanimous that Airgas was worth at least $78 in a sale. The three new
directors objected in a December 7 letter that they had never agreed $78 was "the price
of admission to having any discussions" or a minimum sale price, and asked for authority
to hire their own financial and legal advisors. The evidentiary hearing established that
the letter was "meant as leverage" — Clancey: "We wanted a financial advisor and we were
trying to induce [the other directors]. It's like playing poker. We put our chips up on
the table, everything we had."

It worked. On December 10 the nine independent directors unanimously retained Credit
Suisse as a third independent financial advisor for the full board, and the new directors
retained Skadden as their own counsel at the company's expense. By December 21 the Air
Products Nominees fully supported the view that Airgas was worth at least $78.

Air Products then made its $70 "best and final" offer. It was unanimously rejected, and
the Air Products Nominees were among the most vocal opponents: Clancey concluded the
offer "was not adequate," that even an increase well below $78 "was not going to 'move
the needle'," and told the board, "We have to protect the pill." On December 22 Airgas
filed an amended 14D-9 rejecting the $70 offer as "clearly inadequate" in two pages of
bullet points.

### Issue

- Whether a board may keep a poison pill in place against a structurally non-coercive,
  all-cash, fully financed, all-shares tender offer, after losing one election contest,
  a year after the offer became public, and with fully informed stockholders.
- Whether inadequate price alone, in that setting, is a legally cognisable threat under
  the first prong of Unocal.
- Whether a poison pill combined with a staggered board is preclusive under the second
  prong.
- Whether Unocal ceases to apply once the bidder's own elected nominees agree with the
  incumbent directors.

### Holding

- **On the standard: Unocal applies.** Defensive action against a pending bid always
  carries the spectre of disloyalty, and the agreement of the bidder's nominees does not
  convert the decision into an ordinary business judgment.
- **On prong one: a threat was articulated, though barely.** Process alone is
  insufficient and must produce a threat. The offer is not structurally coercive and
  poses no opportunity loss, but **substantive coercion** — the risk that stockholders
  will disbelieve the board's view of long-term value and tender into an inadequate
  offer — is a valid threat recognised by the Supreme Court, and inadequate price is a
  form of it.
- **On prong two: proportionate.** The defences are not coercive, because no
  management-sponsored alternative is being crammed down; not preclusive, because
  Selectica holds a classified board plus a pill leaves control realistically
  attainable; and within the range of reasonableness, given a good faith belief in a
  substantial price gap, three independent financial advisors, and the bidder's own
  nominees joining the rejection.
- **Disposition:** relief denied; claims dismissed with prejudice. But a board still
  cannot "just say no" without passing both prongs, and the case "does not endorse 'just
  say never'."

### Rule

- When a poison pill is maintained as a defensive measure and the board is asked to
  redeem the rights, Unocal enhanced scrutiny applies.
- Prong one is satisfied by good faith and reasonable investigation, materially enhanced
  by a majority-independent board — but the process must actually produce an articulated,
  legitimate threat to corporate policy and effectiveness.
- Prong two asks first whether the response was draconian, being either preclusive or
  coercive, and if not, whether it fell within a range of reasonable responses.
- A response is coercive if aimed at cramming down a management-sponsored alternative.
- A response is preclusive if it makes gaining control of the board realistically
  unattainable; delay, even significant delay, is not preclusion.
- The combination of a classified board and a rights plan is not preclusive.
- Substantive coercion — the risk that stockholders will accept an inadequate offer out
  of ignorance or mistaken belief about the board's assessment of long-term value — is a
  legally cognisable threat.
- Directors acting deliberately, in an informed way and in good faith pursuit of
  corporate interests may follow a course designed to achieve long-term value even at the
  cost of immediate value maximisation.
- Where defensive measures are inextricably related they are scrutinised collectively as
  a unitary response.

### Reasoning

*(Chandler, C.)*

- The opinion opens by framing the case as an allocation question — "when, if ever, will
  a board's duty to 'the corporation and its shareholders' require [the board] to abandon
  concerns for 'long term' values... and enter a current share value maximizing mode?"
  and "in the context of a hostile tender offer, who gets to decide when and if the
  corporation is for sale?" The answer given is the board.
- Chandler tightens prong one before applying it, holding that exemplary process is not
  enough and must yield an articulated threat. Having raised the bar, he then finds the
  threat anyway, which is what makes the opinion read as reluctant.
- The threat analysis proceeds by **elimination**, which is why it is so useful. Structural
  coercion is ruled out on the offer's own terms; opportunity loss is ruled out by sixteen
  months with no alternative and counsel's concession; substantive coercion is what is
  left, and the Supreme Court has blessed it.
- On preclusion he treats himself as bound and says so, recording in a footnote the
  empirical reality that a pill plus a classified board in fact precludes the tender offer
  and leaves only the election route — and that the law would be more credible if it said
  so.
- Within the range of reasonableness the **bidder's own nominees** carry the weight. Three
  directors elected on a promise to look afresh, given their own counsel and a third
  financial advisor, concluded the price was inadequate and that the pill should stay. It
  is difficult to call a rejection unreasonable when the challenger's own candidates
  reached it independently.
- The tactical point is the sting. Air Products could have run a slate promising to redeem
  the pill, or on shareholder choice; it ran one promising an unbiased look, and got one.
  The pill is thereby recast as something the stockholders had a real chance to remove and
  chose not to.
- The conclusion insists pills cannot have set expiration dates if they are to work at
  all, while denying that this endorses "just say never" — a distinction that rests
  entirely on the continued availability of the annual meeting.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

This is the rare opinion whose most interesting disagreement is internal. Chandler
believes there was no real threat, that the stockholders had everything they needed, that
the pill had already done its legitimate work, and that Allen's and Strine's narrower
conception of substantive coercion is the right one — and holds the other way because
Supreme Court precedent, and in particular Time's dictum, leaves him no room. Footnotes
105 and 106 are the two places where he says so directly. The result is that the case is
simultaneously the strongest statement of board power over tender offers and the most
persuasive brief against it.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the question the opinion asks.** In the context of a hostile tender offer,
  who decides when the company is for sale? The answer under current Delaware law is the
  board — but only after passing both Unocal prongs.
- **Run the threat taxonomy in order.** Structural coercion, absent. Opportunity loss,
  absent. Substantive coercion, present. That sequence is the single most transferable
  thing in the case, and it is the framework for any pill question.
- **Define substantive coercion precisely.** The risk that stockholders will accept an
  inadequate offer "because of 'ignorance or mistaken belief' regarding the Board's
  assessment of the long-term value" of the stock. Then say where it comes from: Time.
- **Know the two footnotes.** 105 — bound by precedent that drives a result he disagrees
  with. 106 — the law would be more credible if the Supreme Court admitted its later
  rulings have modified Moran, since the tender offer is in fact precluded. Quoting 106
  shows you understand both the doctrine and the gap between doctrine and reality.
- **Be exact on preclusion.** Selectica: a classified board plus a pill is not preclusive,
  because delay is not prevention. Add Chandler's observation that no bidder has ever
  actually won two consecutive proxy contests to remove a pill.
- **Have the tightened prong one ready.** Process alone does not satisfy it; the board
  must actually articulate a legitimate threat. That is a small but real doctrinal
  contribution, and it cuts against boards.
- **Use the Air Products Nominees when asked whether this is entrenchment.** The bidder's
  own three directors, with their own lawyers and a third banker, unanimously agreed the
  price was inadequate. That is the hardest fact for the plaintiff's side.
- **Name the outer limits, so the answer is not "boards always win."** No dead-hand,
  no-hand or slow-hand pills; the annual meeting must remain available; and the case "does
  not endorse 'just say never'."
- **Numbers worth holding.** $70 best and final against a board valuation of at least $78;
  sixteen months of pursuit; a $10 increase driven by the pill; three of nine seats lost
  and then regained in substance; 15% pill trigger; 67% supermajority provision against a
  20% Interested Stockholder; 83.6 million shares; four quarters of improving results.
