## Rule

Directors are not insurers. They are liable neither for an **honest mistake of
law** about the scope of their corporate powers, nor for an employee's fraud they
failed to detect, provided their examinations were conducted "in the usual manner
pursued in other companies by prudent men careful of their own concerns."

## Facts

- The National Insurance Company was chartered by special act of the New York
  legislature in 1815, capital not to exceed $500,000 in $100 shares, with the
  property and concerns placed under nineteen annually elected directors. *Actual
  capital was $301,800.*
- The charter authorised insurance and lending on bottomry and respondentia,
  required capital to be invested in public stocks, bank stock, or bonds and
  mortgages before any risk was assumed (§ XIV), and forbade dealing or trading in
  goods or stock except to invest, secure, or reinvest (§ XI).
- **The directors nonetheless discounted notes**, averaging $290,000 a year while
  Kane was secretary — bank business the charter did not authorise. *Held ultra
  vires, and held not to create liability.*
- The directors appointed Oliver Kane secretary in 1824. Over 1825–27 he abstracted
  roughly **$179,000**, spent it on gambling, and killed himself on 3 January 1828,
  which is how the losses came to light.
- **The method was forgery of checks.** The president's own control was that no
  money left except by check signed by him and countersigned by the secretary,
  filled out in the secretary's hand per industry usage. Kane retained checks drawn
  for $5, $7 and $9, paid those small bills in cash, then **altered the units to
  thousands**. *The fraud ran through the very control designed to prevent it.*
- Semi-annual examining committees of directors reviewed the books for three years
  and detected nothing. Kane prepared the books and statements for each
  examination, artfully contrived to deceive.
- **Detection was easy if attempted**: compare the deposit books against the bank's
  own records, or compare cancelled checks against the check book. *Argued and
  rejected — "There is a wide difference between" what the committees could have
  done and what it was their duty to do.*
- Stockholders received semi-annual dividends of six per cent or more, on
  statements disclosing bills receivable, and the complainant **bought more shares
  year after year**. *This acquiescence disposes of the ultra vires claim.*
- No fraud or wilful disregard of duty was alleged against the directors by the
  time of decision; the complainant conceded the evidence failed on that.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **On ultra vires**: discounting notes exceeded the charter. Specification of
  certain powers "operates to restrain a corporation to the object of such powers
  and is an implied prohibition of the exercise of other and distinct powers," and
  "investment" is a permanent, inactive disposition of money rather than active
  capital employed in banking (following *People v. Utica Insurance Co.*).
- **On liability for that excess: none.** The error was honest, on a question of
  nice construction. "No man who takes upon himself an office of trust or confidence
  ... contracts for any thing more than a diligent attention to its concerns and a
  faithful and honest discharge of the duty." Holding men liable for good-faith
  error "would put an end to all offices of trust — since no one who is capable or
  worthy could be found to accept of them."
- **On acquiescence**: the stockholders knew and participated, so directors and
  stockholders are *in pari delicto* and neither may charge the other for what grew
  out of the transactions.
- **On causation**: no loss flowed from the note discounting. Not one discounted
  note was lost; the money was taken from cash at the bank and would have been
  equally exposed however it got there. The connection is "but a remote one."
- **On hiring**: appointing Kane did not make the directors sureties for his
  fidelity. Liability attaches only where directors select someone "known to them
  to be unworthy of trust or notoriously of bad character."
- **On oversight**: the standard is whether the examinations "were conducted in the
  usual manner pursued in other companies by prudent men careful of their own
  concerns." Following *Manhattan Bank v. Lydig*: "It is not for the court to point
  out the mode banks are to pursue to detect frauds; but if they take the usual and
  uniform method adopted, not only by this but by other banks, they cannot be
  subject to a charge of negligence." The directors met it. Bill dismissed.

**Rejected**

- *The committees must have failed to examine properly, because the false entries
  were plain and later investigation found them at once.* The court calls the
  argument "strong and able," is openly troubled by it — "the mind is naturally
  startled and astonished" that Kane passed each committee "not only unharmed but
  unsuspected" — and still rejects it. Nothing had aroused suspicion, and Kane
  prepared the books for the occasion.
- *The directors failed to adopt by-laws, hold frequent meetings, and kept too
  little control over the secretary.* By-laws were proved unnecessary in such
  companies and dispensed with in many; more to the point, by-laws "would not
  prevent the commission of crime or the devices of a fraudulent secretary."
- *Kane was known to gamble and was otherwise of exceptionable character.* Denied
  on the answers and unproven. The director most instrumental in his election
  testified he would have personally signed a surety bond had one been customary.

## Context

The earliest ancestor in this course of what becomes the **oversight** line. Scott
v. Depeyster asks the identical question [[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]] asks — when do directors answer for wrongdoing
they did not commit and did not detect — and answers it with an industry-practice
benchmark rather than a good-faith one. Both opinions refuse to convert directors
into guarantors; the modern doctrine moves the inquiry from *diligence* to
*conscious disregard*, which is what makes oversight a **loyalty** problem today
rather than a care problem.

The case also supplies the deference half of the session title, a century and a
half before [[Shlensky v. Wrigley]]. The reasoning is explicitly institutional and
consequentialist: liability for good-faith error would empty the boardroom of
anyone capable or worthy. That is the same argument [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] answers
by requiring gross negligence rather than negligence.

On the charter side, this is the enumerated-powers world. Corporate power came from
a specific legislative grant, and the grant's specification was an implied
prohibition of everything else. The contrast with the modern enabling statute is
the point of the session — see [[Early corporate charters - enumerated powers]].

Note the acquiescence holding, which has no modern analogue in this form. The
directors escape the ultra vires claim partly because the stockholders watched,
took dividends, and re-elected them. Today that argument runs through ratification
and through [[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]] rather than through *in pari
delicto*.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Stockholder's bill in equity, on behalf of himself and all others contributing,
  against the president and directors **in their individual capacities**, to compel
  them personally to make good the company's losses.
- Heard by the Vice-Chancellor of New York on bill, answers, and testimony. The
  answers, where responsive to the bill's charges, count as evidence for the
  defendants — which does substantial work in the outcome.
- No objection was taken that the company itself was not joined.

### Facts

The National Insurance Company was one of many corporations created in the early
republic by a special act of a legislature rather than by filing under a general
statute. Its 1815 charter named its first directors, capped its capital, told it
what kinds of insurance it could write, told it where its capital had to be
invested before it assumed any risk, and forbade it to trade in goods or stock.

By the 1820s the company had drifted well outside that grant, discounting
promissory notes at an average of $290,000 a year — in substance doing a bank's
business under an insurer's charter. The court found no bad faith in it; the Utica
Insurance Company, chartered a year later with comparable powers, had made the same
mistake and had been corrected only by litigation, over a dissent from Justice
Spencer.

Oliver Kane became secretary in October 1824. The company's cash control was a
requirement that every payment go out on a check signed by the president and
countersigned by the secretary, the body filled in by the secretary's hand as
industry usage dictated. Kane wrote small checks for small bills — five, seven,
nine dollars — paid those creditors in cash instead, kept the signed checks, and
altered the figures into thousands. Semi-annual committees of directors examined
the books for three years and found nothing. Kane made up the statements those
committees reviewed.

He killed himself in January 1828. The investigation that followed put the loss at
about $179,000, against a paid-in capital of about $301,800 — well over half the
company's capital.

### Issue

- Whether directors are personally liable to stockholders for employing corporate
  funds beyond the powers granted by the charter, where the excess arose from an
  honest misconstruction of the charter.
- Whether directors are personally liable for a secretary's embezzlements that
  their semi-annual examinations failed, over three years, to detect.

### Holding

- **On the ultra vires claim: no liability.** The note discounting was beyond the
  charter, but an honest error of judgment on a question of doubtful construction
  does not charge a trustee. Independently, the stockholders acquiesced and took
  the dividends, leaving the parties *in pari delicto*; and independently again, no
  loss was caused by the discounting, which was at most a remote condition of the
  fraud.
- **On the oversight claim: no liability.** Appointing a secretary does not make
  directors sureties for his fidelity absent knowledge of bad character, and the
  examinations were conducted in the manner usual among similar companies by
  prudent men careful of their own concerns.
- **Disposition:** the directors are not chargeable with the loss.

### Rule

- A corporation's specified powers operate as an implied prohibition on the
  exercise of other and distinct powers.
- An officer of trust undertakes diligent attention and an honest discharge of
  duty, not infallibility; good-faith error of judgment on a doubtful construction
  is not chargeable.
- Stockholders who know of, acquiesce in, and take the benefit of a course of
  dealing cannot afterwards charge the directors for it.
- Directors do not become sureties for a subordinate's fidelity by appointing him,
  unless they appoint someone known to be unworthy or notoriously of bad character.
- The measure of oversight is the usual and uniform method adopted by similar
  institutions, performed as prudent men careful of their own concerns would.
- Courts will not prescribe the method an institution must use to detect fraud.
- What the directors *could have done* is not the test; the test is what it was
  their **duty** to do.

### Reasoning

*(The Vice-Chancellor)*

- The liability analysis is openly policy-driven: a rule charging trustees for
  honest error "would put an end to all offices of trust," and without such offices
  neither public nor private affairs could be conducted.
- Three independent grounds dispose of the ultra vires claim — honest error,
  stockholder acquiescence, and absence of causation — and the court reaches all
  three rather than resting on one.
- Causation is analysed with unusual care. The funds were taken from cash held at
  the bank, and would have sat there exposed whether they arrived via discounted
  notes or via deposits awaiting bottomry loans. The discounting is "after all, but
  a remote" cause.
- On oversight the court substitutes an external benchmark for its own judgment,
  citing *Manhattan Bank v. Lydig*, and is candid that this benchmark produces an
  uncomfortable result here.
- The industry-usage evidence cuts both ways and the court accepts it in both
  directions: usage excused the absence of a surety bond, the absence of by-laws,
  and the form of the examinations.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; single Vice-Chancellor.
- The genuine tension is internal to the opinion and worth two lines. The court
  concedes it is "almost incredible" that Kane escaped the 1826 and July 1827
  committees if those committees used ordinary diligence, and concedes that one
  trip to the bank would have exposed him. Having said that, it excuses them on
  industry usage — which means the standard is calibrated to what the industry
  actually does rather than to what would work. That is precisely the objection
  that eventually pushes oversight doctrine toward good faith.

### Cold-call notes

- **The question to expect** is what the directors should have done, and the honest
  answer is that the court says they had no duty to do it. One visit to the bank,
  or one comparison of cancelled checks against the check book, would have caught
  him. The opinion's sharpest sentence is the concession that this is true and
  irrelevant: "It is not, I repeat, what they had it in their power to do and might
  have done. There is a wide difference between this and what it was their duty to
  do."
- **Do not confuse the two claims.** Ultra vires investing and failure to detect
  embezzlement are separate, with separate reasons for the same result. Answers
  that blur them lose the causation point, which is where the real analysis is.
- **Be ready on acquiescence.** The complainant kept buying shares. The court treats
  that as evidence he knew and approved, which raises a question worth having an
  answer to: should a stockholder's silence really bar a claim about conduct the
  charter forbade?
- **Connect it forward.** This is the ancestor of Caremark. Same question — liability
  for undetected wrongdoing — different answer. Scott uses industry practice;
  Caremark asks about a reporting system and conscious disregard. Saying so shows
  the arc of the course.
- **Numbers:** chartered 1815, capital capped at $500,000, actual capital $301,800,
  nineteen directors, $290,000 a year in discounted notes, roughly $179,000 stolen,
  checks altered from five, seven and nine dollars into thousands, three years of
  undetected examinations, six per cent semi-annual dividends.
