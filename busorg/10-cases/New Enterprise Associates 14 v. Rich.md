## Rule

A **covenant not to sue for breach of fiduciary duty**, given by sophisticated stockholders in
a **stockholder-level agreement** and limited to a **specific transaction with specific
characteristics**, is not facially invalid — but it **cannot insulate a defendant from tort
liability for intentional wrongdoing**, and a broad waiver of any ability to assert fiduciary
claims "would be a non-starter."

## Facts

- The covenant was **specific, not general**. It applied only to a Drag-Along Sale meeting
  **eight enumerated criteria**. "The Funds did not broadly covenant not to assert any claims for
  breach of fiduciary duty. They agreed not to sue over a **specific transaction with specific
  characteristics**." That specificity is what makes the provision survive facial attack.
- The covenant sat in a **stockholder-level agreement** — the Voting Agreement — and concerned a
  **stockholder-level right**. "The stockholder-level agreement only binds its signatories and
  only affects how they exercise their rights."
- The plaintiffs were **sophisticated venture capital funds and repeat players** who "understood
  its implications."
- The covenant **tracked a model agreement sponsored by the National Venture Capital
  Association**, and one of the firms behind the Funds is an NVCA member.
- It was **bargained-for consideration**. The Funds "acknowledge... that it was an inducement for
  Rich to invest," and it "induced Rich to lead the Recapitalization, his fellow investors to
  participate, and Rich and his colleague to serve on the Board."
- **The Funds had alternatives and declined them all.** They "were the dominant incumbents in the
  cap table," and "could have blocked it, forced the Company to seek different terms, or funded
  the Company themselves," or joined the investor group. "They decided to pass, agreed to the
  Covenant, and let Rich and his investor group take the risk."
- **The Funds had themselves pushed for a liquidity event**, and the company's six-month sale
  process had failed before the recapitalisation was proposed.
- The recapitalisation **converted all existing preferred to common** and gave Rich's group a new
  class of preferred — the classic down-round structure that resets the waterfall.
- The complaint pleads facts "supporting an inference that the defendants could have acted
  **intentionally and in bad faith** to benefit themselves and harm the common stockholders."
  *This is the fact that defeats the covenant as applied.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Fiduciary duties can be tailored, even without statutory authorisation.** "At the heart of
  every fiduciary relationship is an obligation of loyalty that **cannot be eliminated without
  destroying its fiduciary character**. Parties can, however, **orient** the obligation by
  specifying a purpose for the relationship, and they can **authorize the fiduciary to take
  specific actions that otherwise would constitute a breach**." Trust law and agency law both
  exemplify that tailoring.
- Delaware corporate law follows those principles. A **purpose clause** orients the relationship:
  directors "must pursue the corporate purpose selflessly for the benefit of the corporation and
  its stockholders, but they are **limited to pursuing the corporation's purpose. They cannot pick
  another path simply because they prefer it**." The DGCL "allows more space for fiduciary
  tailoring and greater limits on fiduciary accountability than is widely understood."
- **Stockholder-level agreements can go further than charters or bylaws.** "[T]hrough a private
  agreement, stockholders can agree to more constraints on their ability to exercise
  stockholder-level rights than corporate planners can impose through the charter or bylaws."
- **The loyalty claim is not too big to waive.** "Delaware law permits individuals to waive
  significant liberty and property interests that are arguably weightier than a right appurtenant
  to a share."
- **The corporate brand argument fails.** The promise of standardised terms "should not be
  overstated, because Delaware's support for private ordering means that an investor cannot assume
  that one Delaware corporation is like another," and the promise of an immutable duty of loyalty
  "is also overstated, because the duty can be oriented and tailored." In any event a
  stockholder-level agreement "does not undermine the corporate brand, because **the underlying
  rights remain intact**."
- **It does not collapse the corporation into an LLC.** "[T]he fundamental differences between
  corporations and LLCs operate at the **basal level of their statutes and constitutive
  documents**," and a superficial similarity in investor-level contracting "does not turn
  corporations into LLCs."
- **Manti Holdings supports rather than defeats the covenant.** There a majority of the Supreme
  Court upheld a drag-along **appraisal waiver**, "stressing the contractual freedom that Delaware
  corporate law provides," though "they also emphasized that they were not upholding all waivers
  ... and they admonished that Delaware law might not permit a stockholder to waive other rights."
- **The Manti factors govern enforcement as applied**: "(i) the presence of a written contract,
  (ii) the clarity of the waiver, (iii) the stockholder's understanding of the waiver's
  implications, (iv) the stockholder's ability to reject the provision, (v) the existence of
  bargained-for consideration, and (vi) the stockholder's sophistication." **The proponent must
  establish that enforcement is reasonable.**
- This case "provides an **optimal scenario for enforcement**" on every factor.
- **A hard look is coming for others.** "A covenant not to sue resembles another powerful
  provision: the **covenant not to compete**... Parties should expect a similar hard look for
  covenants not to sue. A **broad waiver** of any ability to assert claims for breach of fiduciary
  duty would be a **non-starter**. Even a narrowly tailored provision would likely be unreasonable
  if it appeared in an agreement that purported to restrict the rights of **retail
  stockholders**."
- **The intentional-harm limit is where the covenant fails.** "Delaware law generally prohibits
  contractual provisions that purport to exculpate a party for tort liability resulting from
  **intentional or reckless harm**. Delaware corporate law is more permissive and treats
  recklessness as a form of gross negligence, thereby expanding the power to exculpate to encompass
  recklessness." The only case going further is Abry Partners, permitting a sophisticated party to
  disclaim reliance and thereby covenant not to sue for extracontractual fraud.
- The covenant "cannot insulate the defendants from tort liability based on **intentional
  wrongdoing**, but it can protect against other claims." Because the complaint supports an
  inference of intentional bad faith, **the motion to dismiss is denied**.

**Rejected**

- *Fiduciary relationships are creatures of equity, equity does not countenance limitations on
  fiduciary duties except as authorised by statute, the DGCL does not authorise this covenant, and
  it is therefore contrary to Delaware public policy.* The premise is wrong: fiduciary duties can
  be tailored without statutory authorisation, by orientation and by authorising specific actions
  that would otherwise be breaches — as trust law, agency law, the DGCL and Delaware common law all
  demonstrate.
- *A claim for breach of the duty of loyalty is too big to waive.* Delaware permits waiver of
  liberty and property interests weightier than a right appurtenant to a share.
- *Permitting such covenants conflicts with Delaware's corporate brand of standardised terms and an
  immutable duty of loyalty.* Both promises are overstated, and a stockholder-level agreement leaves
  the underlying rights intact for every stockholder.
- *Permitting them collapses the distinction between a corporation and an LLC.* The differences
  operate at the level of the statutes and constitutive documents, not at the level of investor
  contracting.
- *Manti's cautions show that this covenant goes too far.* Trust law, agency law, the DGCL and
  Delaware common law each authorise provisions letting fiduciaries engage in specific transactions
  that would otherwise be breaches, and this covenant is sufficiently specific because it reaches
  only a transaction meeting the eight Drag-Along criteria.

## Context

Rich is the **contractual sequel to [[In re Trados]]**, and the two should be read as a pair.
Trados holds that preferred stockholders cannot make the board prefer them — the board's
discretion belongs to the residual claimants, and the preferred get their contract and nothing
more. Rich asks what sophisticated preferred holders can therefore buy by contract instead, and
the answer is: a great deal, at the stockholder level, so long as the waiver is specific, the
counterparties are sophisticated, and nobody acts intentionally in bad faith.

The doctrinal move that makes it work is the distinction between **eliminating** and **tailoring**
a fiduciary duty. Loyalty cannot be eliminated "without destroying its fiduciary character," but it
can be **oriented** — a purpose clause channels what the fiduciary may pursue — and specific
authorisations can permit conduct that would otherwise breach it. That framing lets Laster uphold
the covenant without conceding that corporate fiduciary duties are contractual at their core.

The second move is the **level** at which the waiver operates. A charter or bylaw binds everyone
and defines the shares; a stockholder-level agreement binds only its signatories and only governs
how they exercise rights they still hold. That is why the covenant does not damage Delaware's
"corporate brand," and it is also why the opinion warns that the same provision "would likely be
unreasonable if it appeared in an agreement that purported to restrict the rights of **retail
stockholders**."

**The limits are the examinable part.** Three of them are stated flatly: a broad waiver of any
fiduciary claim is a non-starter; a narrowly tailored one is likely unreasonable against retail
holders; and no covenant can exculpate **intentional** wrongdoing. The last is what actually
decides the motion — the covenant is valid, and it still does not get the defendants out, because
the complaint pleads intentional bad faith.

The covenant-not-to-compete analogy is the practical signal to lawyers. Delaware will enforce
these where sophisticated parties use them to create value and will scrutinise them closely
everywhere else, which is exactly how it treats restrictive covenants.

Note the relationship to the rest of the course. The permissiveness here sits beside the rigidity
of [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co]] and [[Gorman v. Salamone]], which hold that
stockholders cannot use the charter or bylaws to take over the board's § 141(a) authority. The
reconciliation is the same level distinction: **you may constrain how you exercise your own
rights; you may not rearrange the board's**. See
[[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]]. It also anticipates
[[Miller v. HCP & Co]] in session 25, where the parties simply used an LLC and waived fiduciary
duties outright — and the court's answer there is that having chosen that structure, they cannot
recover the protection through the implied covenant.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Motion to dismiss Counts VI, VII and VIII of the complaint — the Sale Counts — on the ground
  that the plaintiffs had covenanted not to sue.
- The Funds **concede** that the covenant covers their claims and that it induced Rich to invest;
  they argue only that it is **facially invalid**.
- Motion denied.
- The posture explains the opinion's unusual shape. Because the plaintiffs stake everything on
  facial invalidity, the court must decide the general question — can stockholders covenant not to
  sue for breach of fiduciary duty — before reaching the narrow ground on which the motion actually
  fails.

### Facts

The plaintiffs are investment funds managed by sophisticated venture capital firms. They invested
in a startup called Fugue, Inc. and backed it for about six years, then encouraged management to
seek a liquidity event. The company spent six months looking for a buyer and no one expressed
interest. Having declared the sale process a failure, the company needed capital.

The Funds did not want to increase their commitment. Management represented that the only option
was a recapitalisation led by George Rich, who would commit only on three conditions: that all
existing preferred stock convert to common; that he and his fellow investors receive a new class of
preferred; and that the Funds and other significant investors execute a voting agreement. The Funds
accepted those terms. They were offered the chance to participate in the recapitalisation and
declined.

The Voting Agreement contained a drag-along right: if the board and the holders of a majority of
the new preferred approved a transaction meeting eight enumerated criteria, the signatories were
obliged to participate. Critically, the signatories also covenanted not to sue Rich or his
affiliates or associates over a Drag-Along Sale, including by asserting claims for breach of
fiduciary duty.

An opportunity to sell soon materialised, the company and the acquiror negotiated a Drag-Along
Sale, and that transaction closed. The Funds then sued, challenging the sale and asserting
fiduciary claims. The defendants moved to dismiss on the strength of the covenant.

### Issue

- Whether a covenant not to sue for breach of fiduciary duty is facially invalid under Delaware law
  as an unauthorised limitation on fiduciary duties.
- If not, whether this covenant is enforceable on these facts under the Manti factors.
- Whether such a covenant can reach claims of intentional wrongdoing.

### Holding

- **On facial validity: the covenant is valid.** Fiduciary duties can be oriented and tailored
  without statutory authorisation; a stockholder-level agreement about stockholder-level rights
  leaves the underlying rights intact; loyalty claims are not categorically unwaivable; and this
  covenant is sufficiently specific because it reaches only a transaction meeting eight enumerated
  criteria.
- **On enforcement as applied: reasonable.** Every Manti factor favours enforcement — a written
  contract, a clear and specific waiver, sophisticated repeat players who understood it, a real
  ability to reject it, bargained-for consideration, and a provision tracking an NVCA model.
- **On scope: it cannot reach intentional wrongdoing.** Delaware generally prohibits contractual
  exculpation for intentional or reckless harm, and corporate law's extension covers recklessness
  but not intent.
- **Disposition:** because the Sale Counts rest on facts supporting an inference of intentional bad
  faith, they cannot be dismissed at the pleading stage. Motion denied.

### Rule

- Loyalty is at the heart of every fiduciary relationship and cannot be eliminated without
  destroying the relationship's fiduciary character.
- Fiduciary duties may nonetheless be oriented by specifying a purpose, and fiduciaries may be
  authorised to take specific actions that would otherwise constitute a breach.
- Directors must pursue the corporate purpose selflessly, but are limited to pursuing that purpose
  and cannot pick another path simply because they prefer it.
- Stockholders may agree by private agreement to more constraints on their stockholder-level rights
  than corporate planners can impose through the charter or bylaws.
- A covenant not to sue for breach of fiduciary duty is not facially invalid where it is limited to
  a specific transaction with specific characteristics.
- Enforcement is tested by the Manti factors, with the burden on the proponent to establish that
  enforcement is reasonable.
- Delaware law prohibits contractual provisions exculpating a party for tort liability resulting
  from intentional harm; corporate law extends the power to exculpate to recklessness treated as
  gross negligence.
- A broad waiver of any ability to assert fiduciary claims is a non-starter, and a tailored one is
  likely unreasonable against retail stockholders.

### Reasoning

*(Laster, V.C.)*

- The opinion is built as a **debate**, stating the argument for facial invalidity in three
  sentences and then unspooling the answer at length, which is itself a signal about where the
  intuitions lie and how much work is needed to overcome them.
- The decisive conceptual distinction is between **eliminating** and **tailoring**. Once loyalty is
  understood as capable of orientation — by purpose clauses in the corporate setting, by the terms
  of a trust or an agency elsewhere — the plaintiffs' premise that only statute can limit fiduciary
  duties collapses.
- The **level** distinction does the rest. A charter defines the shares and binds everyone; a
  stockholder-level agreement binds only signatories exercising rights they continue to hold. Every
  stockholder "receives the underlying rights and can exercise them."
- The court meets the rhetorical objections on their own terms rather than dismissing them. The
  "too big to waive" argument is answered by comparison to liberty and property waivers; the
  corporate brand argument by pointing out that private ordering already means no two Delaware
  corporations are alike; the LLC-collapse argument by locating the real differences at the level of
  the statutes.
- Manti is handled honestly, including the caution and the dissent. Rather than claiming Manti
  controls, the court takes from it a **list of factors** and applies them, and concedes that the
  opinions "raise questions about whether a provision like the Covenant goes too far."
- The **specificity** requirement is drawn from the tailoring authorities themselves: trust law,
  agency law, the DGCL and common law all authorise provisions permitting **specific transactions**
  that would otherwise breach. Eight enumerated criteria is what satisfies that.
- The covenant-not-to-compete analogy is doing forward-looking work. It signals that validity in
  principle will not mean easy enforcement in practice, and it tells lower courts what posture to
  take.
- The disposition is the quiet lesson: the defendants win every argument they raise about the
  covenant and still lose the motion, because Delaware will not let anyone contract out of liability
  for intentional harm.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The result leaves a tension the opinion acknowledges without resolving. The court holds that the
covenant is valid, that it reaches these claims, and that enforcement would be reasonable — and
then lets the claims proceed because the complaint pleads intentional bad faith. Since a fiduciary
plaintiff attacking a conflicted transaction will almost always plead bad faith, the practical
value of such a covenant at the pleading stage may be smaller than its doctrinal endorsement
suggests. What the covenant plainly does accomplish is to strip away everything short of intent,
which in a case like this means the care and process claims that would otherwise require discovery.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the tailoring distinction.** Loyalty cannot be **eliminated** without destroying the
  fiduciary character of the relationship, but it can be **oriented** by a purpose clause and
  specific conduct can be **authorised** in advance. That is the move that answers the facial
  invalidity argument.
- **Then give the level distinction.** Stockholder-level agreement, stockholder-level rights, binds
  only signatories, underlying rights intact. Contrast the charter and bylaws, which bind everyone
  and cannot rearrange § 141(a) authority — Moelis and Gorman.
- **Know the three stated limits, because they are the holding's real content.** A broad waiver is a
  non-starter; a tailored one is likely unreasonable against retail stockholders; and no covenant
  reaches intentional wrongdoing.
- **Recite the Manti factors and say who bears the burden.** Written contract, clarity, understanding,
  ability to reject, bargained-for consideration, sophistication — and the **proponent** must show
  enforcement is reasonable.
- **Explain why the motion was still denied.** The covenant is valid and unenforceable as to
  intentional bad faith, which is what the Sale Counts plead. Getting this backwards is the easiest
  mistake in the case.
- **Have the covenant-not-to-compete analogy ready.** Useful to sophisticated parties, susceptible to
  abuse, and examined closely. It tells you how Delaware will police these.
- **Connect to Trados.** Trados denies preferred holders the board's discretion; Rich lets them buy a
  contractual substitute at the stockholder level. Together they describe what VC investors can and
  cannot obtain.
- **Numbers and specifics worth holding.** Eight Drag-Along criteria; a six-month failed sale process;
  all existing preferred converted to common with a new preferred class for Rich's group; an NVCA
  model provision; Manti's six factors; Abry Partners as the single precedent permitting a waiver
  touching intentional conduct.
