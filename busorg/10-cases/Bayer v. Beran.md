## Rule

The business judgment rule **yields to the rule of undivided loyalty**. Where a
transaction tends to produce a conflict between self-interest and fiduciary
obligation, it is examined "with the most scrupulous care," and the burden falls on
the director "not only to prove the good faith of the transaction but also to show
its inherent fairness from the viewpoint of the corporation."

Rigorous scrutiny is not a verdict. A conflicted transaction can survive it.

## Facts

- Celanese needed to expand advertising and the directors chose a radio programme of
  fine music, the "Celanese Hour." The choice of medium, the budget, and the
  programme format are all *conceded to be ordinary business judgment*.
- **Dr. Camille Dreyfus was president and a dominant director. His wife of twelve
  years, professionally Jean Tennyson, was a singer who took a paid part in the
  programme.** *This single fact is why the case is not disposed of summarily.*
- Dreyfus consulted his wife about the programme and had the advertising agency
  confer with her. **She suggested the artists and the conductor, all of whom the
  agency adopted, and offered her own services as a paid artist.**
- **The other directors did not know she would be a paid artist until after they had
  approved the campaign** and the general type of programme.
- The Dreyfus family held about 135,000 shares and the other directors about 10,000,
  out of 1,376,500 outstanding — so this is **not a closely held family corporation**.
- Her compensation was $500 an evening, $24,000 in 1942 and $20,500 in 1943.
  *Argued as the motive and rejected — "far-fetched to suggest" a company incurred
  large advertising expenditure to produce that sum.*
- **She was paid less than any other artist**, on a standard-form contract negotiated
  through her professional agent, at rates conforming to comparable work, with no
  special build-up despite appearing most regularly. All artists were subordinated to
  the advertisement of the company.
- The programme's popularity increased after launch, and its musical quality was
  never challenged.
- **The radio campaign was never taken up at a formal board meeting and no
  resolution approved it.** *Argued as illegality and rejected.* A July 1943
  resolution expressly ratified only the renewal.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- No breach of fiduciary duty. Complaint dismissed on the merits.
- On the advertising decisions themselves: "[i]t was for the directors to determine
  whether they would resort to radio advertising; it was for them to conclude how
  much to spend; it was for them to decide the kind of program." Substituting
  judicial judgment there "would be an unwarranted act of interference."
- On the wife's participation: because a close relative of the chief executive "takes
  a position closely associated with a new and expensive field of activity, the
  motives of the directors are likely to be questioned," and the board is placed
  where "selfish, personal interests might be in conflict with the duty it owed." The
  **entire transaction** must therefore be subjected to "the most rigorous scrutiny."
- Applying that scrutiny: no breach. Enhanced prestige for the singer is no ground
  for liability "as long as the advertising served a legitimate and a useful corporate
  purpose and the company received the full benefit thereof."
- On formality: directors acting separately cannot ordinarily bind the corporation,
  for two reasons — collective procedure permits deliberation and interchange of
  views, and directors have no power to act except as a board. **But failure to
  observe the formalities "is by no means fatal"** on these facts: a close working
  directorate in daily association, following its customary practice for projects of
  equal and greater magnitude, with all executive committee members available and
  consulted.
- On ratification: the July 1943 resolution "may be deemed a ratification of all prior
  action," and independently, "acceptance and retention of the benefits of the radio
  advertising, with full knowledge thereof, was as complete a ratification as would
  have resulted from any formal all-inclusive resolution."

**Rejected**

- *The programme was undertaken to "further, foster and subsidize" the career of the
  president's wife and to furnish a vehicle for her talents.* The evidence failed to
  show it. The programme served a real corporate purpose, the company received full
  benefit, and her compensation was modest against the campaign's cost.
- *Choosing classical music rather than variety shows the programme was not for the
  company's benefit.* Variety has wider appeal, but "it would be a very sad thing if
  the former were the only kind of radio programs to be used," and large industrial
  concerns have long maintained fine musical programmes.
- *The expenditures were illegal because no formal board meeting or resolution
  approved them.* Ultra vires rejected; the expenditures were approved and authorised
  by the members individually and later ratified.

## Context

The common-law backdrop against which **8 Del. C. § 144** operates. Bayer states the
default: an interested transaction is stripped of business judgment protection and
the fiduciary carries the burden of proving good faith **and** inherent fairness.
Section 144 is the statutory overlay that supplies routes out of that default — and
the casebook's note immediately after this case poses the question the whole session
turns on. See [[Interested director and officer transactions - 144(a)]].

The structure is the same one already seen in [[Detroit Lions v. Argovitz]]: conflict
identified, presumption lost, burden on the fiduciary. What differs is the outcome,
and the difference is instructive. Argovitz never called the competing bidder;
Dreyfus's wife was paid a market rate, less than her colleagues, on an agent-
negotiated standard form, for a programme that worked. **Rigorous scrutiny is a
standard of review, not a rule of invalidity.**

Two threads run forward. The scrutiny trigger here is a **familial** relationship
rather than a financial interest, which is now expressly captured by § 144(e)(8)'s
definition of a "material relationship" as one "familial, financial, professional,
employment, or other" that "would reasonably be expected to impair the objectivity of
the director's judgment." And the ratification-by-acceptance-of-benefits holding is a
much looser cousin of the doctrine later tightened in
[[In re Investors Bancorp]], where retention of benefits is precisely what does
**not** ratify a discretionary self-award.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Stockholders' derivative action alleging negligence and waste in the advertising
  campaign, tried on the merits in the New York Supreme Court.
- Not a pleading-stage decision. The court had a full record, which is why it can say
  the evidence "fails to show" the improper purpose rather than merely that it was
  inadequately pleaded.

### Facts

Celanese Corporation manufactured a product competing with rayon and concluded it
needed to expand advertising. The directors settled on sponsoring a radio programme
of classical and semi-classical music, the "Celanese Hour," and retained an
advertising agency to produce it.

Dr. Camille Dreyfus, the company's president and one of its dominant directors,
consulted his wife about the programme. She had been a professional singer for years
under the name Jean Tennyson. He also asked the advertising agency to confer with
her. She proposed the artists — all Metropolitan Opera stars — and the conductor, and
the agency adopted every one of her suggestions. She also offered her own services,
for pay.

She was engaged at $500 an evening, earning $24,000 in 1942 and $20,500 in 1943, on a
standard-form contract negotiated through her professional agent. The other directors
learned she would be a paid artist only after they had approved both the campaign and
the programme type.

The campaign was never put to a formal board meeting. The directorate was small,
composed largely of executive officers in daily contact, and had handled larger
projects the same informal way. In July 1943, with all directors present but two
resident in England, the board passed a resolution expressly ratifying the renewal of
the broadcasting contract — by which time the Celanese Hour had been on the air for
eighteen months.

### Issue

- Whether directors breach the duty of loyalty by approving a corporate advertising
  programme in which the president's wife is engaged as a paid performer.
- Whether the expenditures were unlawful because no formal board meeting or resolution
  authorised them.

### Holding

- **On loyalty: no breach.** The familial connection required the entire transaction
  to be examined under the most rigorous scrutiny, and the transaction survived it —
  the advertising served a legitimate corporate purpose, the company received full
  benefit, the compensation was at market and below that of her colleagues, and there
  was no evidence the programme was designed to advance her career.
- **On formality: no liability.** Directors ordinarily bind the corporation only as a
  board, but failure to observe that formality is not fatal where the practice was
  customary, the directors were in daily consultation, and the action was later
  ratified both expressly and by acceptance of benefits with full knowledge.
- **Disposition:** complaint dismissed on the merits.

### Rule

- Questions of policy, expediency of contracts, adequacy of consideration, and the
  appropriation of corporate funds to advance corporate interests are left to
  directors' "honest and unselfish decision," and may not be questioned even where
  the result proves unwise or inexpedient.
- Absent fraud, improper motive, or personal interest, it is "only in a most unusual
  and extraordinary case" that directors are liable for negligence.
- The business judgment rule **yields to the rule of undivided loyalty**, which reaches
  "every situation in which a trustee chooses to deal with another in such close
  relation with the trustee that possible advantage to such other person might
  influence, consciously or unconsciously, the judgment of the trustee."
- Where challenged, such transactions are examined with the most scrupulous care, and
  "if there is any evidence of improvidence or oppression, any indication of
  unfairness or undue advantage, the transactions will be voided."
- The burden is on the director to prove **both** the good faith of the transaction
  **and** its inherent fairness from the corporation's viewpoint.
- Directors acting separately rather than as a board cannot ordinarily bind the
  corporation, but failure to observe that formality is not fatal where the practice
  is customary and the action is ratified.
- Acceptance and retention of benefits with full knowledge can itself constitute
  ratification.

### Reasoning

*(Shientag, J.)*

- The opinion is built as two separate analyses, and keeping them apart is the whole
  craft of it. The advertising decisions get pure business judgment deference; the
  wife's engagement triggers loyalty scrutiny of the entire transaction.
- The trigger is **appearance and structural risk**, not proof of taint. The court
  says only that where a close relative takes such a position, "the motives of the
  directors are likely to be questioned" and the board "would be placed in a position
  where selfish, personal interests might be in conflict."
- Having raised the standard, the court then applies it concretely: market rate, paid
  less than the others, standard form, agent-negotiated, no special build-up, artists
  subordinated to the product, programme popularity rising. Each fact answers a
  specific inference of favouritism.
- The disproportion argument disposes of purpose: a company does not run a large
  advertising campaign to route $24,000 to a spouse.
- The closing note is a warning rather than a holding — the directors "have allowed
  themselves to be placed in a position where their motives concerning future
  decisions on radio advertising may be impugned," and "[t]he free mind should be ever
  jealous of its freedom."
- On formality, the court is candid that regular procedure "is something more than
  what has, at times, thoughtlessly been termed red tape," while declining to impose
  liability for its absence.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; single judge.
- The tension worth two lines: the court finds the other directors did not know the
  president's wife would be paid until after they approved the campaign, and treats
  that as exonerating. It is equally an argument the other way — the disinterested
  directors approved without the material fact, which is the precise defect
  § 144(a)(1) now addresses by requiring the material facts to be "disclosed or...
  known to **all** members of the board."

### Cold-call notes

- **The likely opening question** is what triggers the heightened scrutiny, and the
  answer is not self-dealing. Dreyfus took nothing. The trigger is a **close relation**
  whose advantage "might influence, consciously or unconsciously," the fiduciary's
  judgment. That is a structural test, and § 144(e)(8) now codifies it as "material
  relationship."
- **The trap** is treating rigorous scrutiny as a conclusion. The directors **win**.
  Any answer that stops at "conflict, therefore liability" has missed the case's
  actual work, which is showing what surviving the standard looks like.
- **Have the exonerating facts as a list**, because that list is the template for
  arguing fairness: market compensation, less than peers, standard form, negotiated at
  arm's length through an agent, no undue prominence, legitimate corporate purpose,
  full benefit received, success afterwards.
- **Be ready on the formality point.** Buccola may push on why sloppy process did not
  sink this. The answer is customary practice plus a close working directorate plus
  ratification — and note the court's own discomfort, which suggests a different board
  would get a different answer.
- **Connect it to the statute.** Bayer is the common-law default § 144 was written
  against: burden on the fiduciary to prove good faith and inherent fairness. Every
  safe harbour in § 144 is a way of not having to do what Dreyfus had to do here.
- **Numbers:** $500 an evening, $24,000 in 1942, $20,500 in 1943; Dreyfus family
  135,000 shares, other directors 10,000, out of 1,376,500 outstanding; eighteen
  months on air before the ratifying resolution.
