## Rule

"[I]t is the policy of [the LLC Act] to give the **maximum effect to the principle of freedom of
contract** and to the enforceability of limited liability company agreements." Members may
therefore contract away their right to sue in Delaware — including derivatively — and the LLC is
bound by an agreement it never signed, because "**[i]t is the members who are the real parties in
interest. The LLC is simply their joint business vehicle.**"

## Facts

- The **LLC agreement contained an arbitration clause** covering all disputes, seated in San
  Francisco, and a **California forum selection clause** for "any action on a claim arising out of,
  under or in connection with this Agreement or the transactions contemplated by this Agreement,
  provided such claim is not required to be arbitrated," plus consent to personal jurisdiction in
  California.
- **Malek LLC never signed the Agreement.** The Certificate of Formation was filed October 29,
  1996; the members signed the Agreement on November 4. *Argued and rejected — this is the fact
  the appeal was built on.*
- **The members did sign it.** Elf and Malek, Inc. "executed the Agreement to carry out the affairs
  and business of Malek LLC and to provide for arbitration and forum selection."
- The dispute-resolution provisions "**do not distinguish between direct and derivative claims.
  They simply state that the members may not initiate any claims outside of California.**"
- Elf contributed **$1 million for a 30% interest**; Malek, Inc. contributed its rights to the
  water-based maskant for **70%**. Jaffari was the manager under the Agreement and chief executive
  under a separate employment agreement.
- **The Distribution Agreement contained no forum selection or arbitration clause.** *Argued and
  rejected — those counts were nonetheless swept in.*
- Elf's allegations went to **Jaffari's conduct as manager**: breach of fiduciary duty, withdrawing
  funds for personal use, pushing the LLC toward insolvency, interfering with business
  opportunities, failing to disclose, and threatening to make poor quality maskant and violate
  environmental regulations. "[A]ll of plaintiff's claims revolve around Jaffari's conduct (or
  misconduct) as Malek's manager."
- Elf's own complaint alleged that "Jaffari... has totally disregarded his **obligations under the
  LLC Agreement**." *The court treats that allegation as support for characterising the claims as
  contractual.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Policy.** Section 18-1101(b), like § 17-1101(c) of the LP Act, provides that "it is the policy
  of [the Act] to give the maximum effect to the principle of freedom of contract and to the
  enforceability of limited liability company agreements." The Act is "a **flexible statute**"
  that "generally permits members to engage in private ordering with substantial freedom of
  contract to govern their relationship, provided they do not contravene any **mandatory
  provisions** of the Act."
- "The basic approach of the Delaware Act is to provide members with **broad discretion in drafting
  the Agreement** and to furnish **default provisions when the members' agreement is silent**. The
  Act is replete with fundamental provisions made subject to modification in the Agreement (e.g.
  'unless otherwise provided in a limited liability company agreement....')."
- "[O]nly where the agreement is **inconsistent with mandatory statutory provisions** will the
  members' agreement be invalidated. Such statutory provisions are likely to be those intended to
  **protect third parties**, not necessarily the contracting members."
- **The LLC is bound though it never signed.** Section 18-101(7) defines the limited liability
  company agreement as "**any agreement, written or oral, of the member or members** as to the
  affairs of a limited liability company and the conduct of its business," and the members executed
  this one to carry out the LLC's affairs. "It is the members who are the real parties in interest.
  The LLC is simply their joint business vehicle."
- **The derivative label is irrelevant.** Section 18-1001 permits a member to sue derivatively in
  the Court of Chancery, but "Elf **contracted away its right to bring such an action in
  Delaware**." Because the provisions do not distinguish direct from derivative claims, "all claims,
  whether derivative or direct, arose under, out of or in connection with the Agreement."
- **The Distributorship Agreement counts are swept in.** The forum clause reaches claims "arising
  out of" and "in connection with" the Agreement or transactions "contemplated by" or "related to"
  it, and "[t]his prohibition is so broad that it is dispositive" of counts purporting to arise under
  a separate document with no forum clause of its own.
- **No "special" Chancery jurisdiction survives contract.** Sections 18-110(a) and 18-111 vest
  jurisdiction in Chancery over removal of managers and over interpreting and enforcing LLC
  agreements, and § 18-1001 permits derivative actions there. "Such a grant of jurisdiction may have
  been constitutionally necessary if the claims do not fall within the traditional equity
  jurisdiction. Nevertheless, **for the purpose of designating a more convenient forum, we find no
  reason why the members cannot alter the default jurisdictional provisions of the statute and
  contract away their right to file suit in Delaware**."
- Affirmed; the amended complaint was properly dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

**Rejected**

- *Malek LLC did not sign the Agreement and so is not bound by its arbitration and forum selection
  clauses, which means the derivative claims brought on its behalf are not covered.* The statutory
  definition of an LLC agreement is an agreement "of the member or members," the members executed
  it, and the LLC is their vehicle rather than a separate party whose assent is required.
- *The claims are derivative, and the Court of Chancery should therefore have adjudicated them.* The
  characterisation is irrelevant because the provisions cover any action, and Elf gave up the
  Delaware forum for all of them.
- *Counts under the Distributorship Agreement, which has no forum clause, must stay in Delaware.* In
  reality they "are all subsumed under the rubric of the Agreement's forum selection clause," since
  every claim revolves around Jaffari's conduct as manager and the remedies sought "bear directly on
  Jaffari's duties and obligations under the LLC Agreement."
- *The Act gives the Court of Chancery special jurisdiction over LLC disputes that members cannot
  contract around.* Those grants set defaults. Members may designate a more convenient forum and
  contract away the right to sue in Delaware.

## Context

Elf Atochem is the **charter of Delaware LLC law**, and everything in session 25 follows from its
premise: the operating agreement is the entity's constitution, and the statute mostly supplies
defaults. The casebook frames the contrast plainly — a corporation "must have a board of directors,
the directors must be human individuals..., the board must meet regularly, and the fiduciary duties
of the directors are rather difficult to alter," whereas with an LLC, "[d]on't want a board? Fine.
Don't want to meet every quarter? No problem."

The case is formally about **forum**, and its reach is broader than that. The holding that members
may contract away the Delaware forum for derivative claims is a specific instance of a general
proposition — statutory rights in the LLC Act are defaults unless the provision is **mandatory**,
and mandatory provisions are mostly those "intended to protect **third parties**, not necessarily
the contracting members." That test is the one to carry into any LLC problem: ask who the provision
protects.

The second holding is the conceptual one. **The LLC is not a stranger to its own operating
agreement.** Section 18-101(7) defines the agreement as one "of the member or members," so the
entity is bound without signing, and the reason given is ontological rather than technical: "[i]t is
the members who are the real parties in interest. The LLC is simply their joint business vehicle."
That framing is why LLC law leans contractual where corporate law leans fiduciary.

**What Elf Atochem does not settle is default fiduciary duties**, and that question ran on for
another fifteen years. The [[Note on LLC default fiduciary duties]], assigned the same day, tells the
story: Chancery held for years that the defaults matched corporate duties, the Supreme Court in Gatz
Properties chastised the Chancellor for reaching the issue at all and declared his pronouncements
"dictum without any precedential value," and the General Assembly settled it in 2013 by amending
**6 Del. C. § 18-1104** to provide that "the rules of law and equity, **including the rules of law
and equity relating to fiduciary duties** and the law merchant, shall govern." So default duties
exist — and members remain free to contract out of them.

[[Miller v. HCP & Co]], assigned the same day, is what happens when they do. There the operating
agreement waived all fiduciary duties, gave the board sole discretion over the manner of a sale, and
the plaintiffs tried to recover protection through the implied covenant. Glasscock's answer is the
natural sequel to Elf Atochem: "the members forwent the suite of common-law protections available
with the corporate form, and instead chose to create an LLC," and the implied covenant "is meant to
enforce the intent of the parties, and not to modify that expressed intent where remorse has set in."

The one duty members cannot waive is the **implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing**, which
6 Del. C. § 18-1101(c) makes mandatory — a good illustration of the mandatory/default line the case
itself draws.

Read against the corporate side of the course, Elf Atochem explains [[Gorman v. Salamone]]'s footnote
exactly: "a Delaware corporation is a **board-centric entity**. Other governance structures can be
imposed on other entities, if that is what the stakeholders desire." If you want member control
without a board, you are not looking for a corporation.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from the Court of Chancery's dismissal of Elf's amended complaint for **lack of subject
  matter jurisdiction**.
- Elf sued Jaffari and Malek LLC, individually and derivatively on behalf of Malek LLC, in Chancery
  seeking equitable remedies.
- Affirmed.
- A **case of first impression** before the Delaware Supreme Court under the LLC Act, which is why
  the opinion spends so long on the Act's architecture and policy before reaching a narrow
  jurisdictional question.

### Facts

Elf Atochem North America, a Pennsylvania corporation, manufactures and distributes solvent-based
maskants to the aerospace and aviation industries worldwide. Cyrus Jaffari was president of Malek,
Inc., a California corporation, which had developed a water-based maskant. In the mid-1990s Elf
approached Jaffari proposing to invest in his product and assist in marketing it — an attractive
proposal to Jaffari, whose company had limited resources and little international sales expertise.

They agreed on a joint venture to be carried out through a limited liability company. On October 29,
1996 Malek, Inc. caused a Certificate of Formation to be filed with the Delaware Secretary of State,
forming Malek LLC. The certificate is "a relatively brief and formal document" that "does not
contain a comprehensive agreement among the parties," and the statute contemplates that it will be
complemented by an LLC agreement.

On November 4 Elf, Malek, Inc. and Jaffari executed the Agreement governing Malek LLC, which the LLC
itself did not sign. Elf contributed $1 million for a 30% interest; Malek, Inc. contributed its
rights to the water-based maskant for 70%. Jaffari was to be the manager. Two further agreements
followed: an Exclusive Distributorship Agreement between Elf and Malek LLC making Elf the worldwide
distributor, and an employment agreement making Jaffari chief executive of Malek LLC.

The Agreement required all disputes "arising out of this Agreement" to be arbitrated in San
Francisco, and selected California courts for any claim "arising out of, under or in connection with
this Agreement or the transactions contemplated by this Agreement" that was not required to be
arbitrated. The Distribution Agreement contained neither clause.

On April 27, 1998 Elf sued in Delaware, individually and derivatively, alleging among other things
that Jaffari breached his fiduciary duty to Malek LLC, withdrew funds for personal use and pushed the
company to the brink of insolvency, interfered with business opportunities, failed to make
disclosures to Elf, and threatened to make poor quality maskant and to violate environmental
regulations, along with breach of contract, tortious interference and fraud.

The Court of Chancery dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, holding that Elf's claims
arose under the Agreement or the transactions it contemplated and were directly related to Jaffari's
actions as manager, so that only a California court or arbitrator could decide them.

### Issue

- Whether an LLC that did not itself execute the LLC agreement defining its governance and operation
  is nevertheless bound by that agreement.
- Whether contractual provisions requiring all disputes to be resolved exclusively by arbitration or
  by court proceedings in California are valid under the Delaware LLC Act.
- Whether the derivative character of a claim, or the statutory grants of jurisdiction to the Court
  of Chancery, prevent members from contracting away the Delaware forum.

### Holding

- **On the unsigned agreement: the LLC is bound.** An LLC agreement is defined as an agreement "of
  the member or members," and the members executed this one to carry out the LLC's affairs; the LLC
  is their joint business vehicle and the members are the real parties in interest.
- **On the clauses: valid.** The Act's policy is to give maximum effect to freedom of contract, and
  an agreement is invalidated only where inconsistent with mandatory statutory provisions, which are
  chiefly those protecting third parties.
- **On the derivative label: irrelevant.** The provisions cover any action, so direct and derivative
  claims alike fall within them.
- **On the Distributorship Agreement counts: swept in.** The forum clause's breadth reaches claims
  arising out of, in connection with, contemplated by or related to the Agreement, and all of Elf's
  claims turn on Jaffari's conduct as manager.
- **On Chancery's statutory jurisdiction: alterable.** Sections 18-110(a), 18-111 and 18-1001 supply
  defaults, and members may designate a more convenient forum.
- **Disposition:** affirmed.

### Rule

- It is the policy of the LLC Act to give maximum effect to the principle of freedom of contract and
  to the enforceability of limited liability company agreements.
- The Act provides members broad discretion in drafting the agreement and furnishes default
  provisions only where the agreement is silent.
- A members' agreement is invalidated only where inconsistent with mandatory statutory provisions,
  which are likely to be those intended to protect third parties.
- A limited liability company agreement is any agreement, written or oral, of the member or members
  as to the affairs of the LLC and the conduct of its business.
- An LLC is bound by the agreement of its members notwithstanding that the LLC did not sign it.
- Members may contract away their right to file suit in Delaware, including as to derivative claims,
  and may alter the default jurisdictional provisions of the Act.

### Reasoning

*(Veasey, C.J.)*

- The opinion begins with the **architecture of the Act** rather than with the clauses, because the
  result follows from the statute's character. The court is candid that the Act is "prolix, sometimes
  oddly organized," and does "not always flow evenly," and then extracts the one organising
  principle — freedom of contract, defaults where the agreement is silent.
- The mandatory/default line is drawn by asking **whom a provision protects**. Provisions safeguarding
  third parties bind; provisions structuring the members' own relations do not. That is the test that
  makes the rest of the analysis possible.
- The unsigned-agreement problem is dissolved by **statutory definition** rather than by agency or
  estoppel. Because § 18-101(7) defines the agreement as one of the members, there is no gap between
  the members' assent and the entity's.
- The accompanying justification is conceptual and is the most quoted line in the case: the members
  are the real parties in interest and the LLC is "simply their joint business vehicle." It is an
  express rejection of the corporate instinct that the entity is a separate constituency whose claims
  belong to it alone.
- On the derivative point the court refuses to let a **characterisation** override a contract. The
  provisions say members may not initiate any claim outside California, and a plaintiff cannot
  relabel its way back into Delaware.
- The Distributorship Agreement counts are handled by looking at **what the claims are really about**.
  Elf's own pleading that Jaffari "totally disregarded his obligations under the LLC Agreement" is
  quoted back to it.
- The jurisdictional holding is carefully limited. The court acknowledges that the statutory grants
  "may have been constitutionally necessary," and then confines its ruling to forum — members may
  choose a more convenient one, which is a different thing from abolishing a right.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion leaves open how far the reasoning reaches. Saying that the LLC is bound because "the
members who are the real parties in interest" works cleanly for a two-member joint venture, where
every member signed. It works less obviously for an LLC with later-admitted members, creditors, or
managers who are not members — and the court's own mandatory/default test, keyed to the protection of
**third parties**, is the place where those problems would have to be worked out. Nothing in the
opinion addresses a member who never signed, and the answer would presumably turn on the Act's
provisions for admission rather than on this reasoning.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with § 18-1101(b).** Maximum effect to the principle of freedom of contract. Everything else
  in Delaware LLC law is a gloss on that sentence.
- **State the mandatory/default test.** An agreement is invalidated only where inconsistent with
  mandatory provisions, and those are chiefly the ones protecting **third parties**. Ask whom the
  provision protects.
- **Have the unsigned-agreement answer ready in two steps.** Section 18-101(7) defines an LLC
  agreement as an agreement "of the member or members"; and the members are the real parties in
  interest while "[t]he LLC is simply their joint business vehicle."
- **Explain why the derivative label did not help.** The clauses did not distinguish direct from
  derivative and barred any action outside California. Section 18-1001 permits derivative suits in
  Chancery, but that is a default the members contracted around.
- **Be precise about what was contracted away.** The **Delaware forum**, not the claims themselves.
  Elf could still pursue everything in California, by arbitration or in court.
- **Know what the case does not decide.** Default fiduciary duties. That took Gatz Properties, a
  rebuke to the Chancellor, and the 2013 amendment to § 18-1104 to resolve.
- **Name the one non-waivable duty.** The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, § 18-1101(c)
  — which is exactly what the plaintiffs reach for in Miller v. HCP.
- **Use the casebook's framing for the corporate contrast.** Corporations require a board of human
  directors meeting regularly with duties hard to alter; LLCs require whatever the operating agreement
  says. Gorman's footnote is the same point from the corporate side.
- **Numbers and dates worth holding.** Certificate filed October 29, 1996; Agreement signed November 4;
  $1 million for 30% against contributed technology for 70%; arbitration in San Francisco; California
  forum; suit filed April 27, 1998.
