## Rule

Demand futility is assessed **director by director**, on three questions:

1. whether the director **received a material personal benefit** from the alleged
   misconduct that is the subject of the demand;
2. whether the director **would face a substantial likelihood of liability** on any of the
   claims that are the subject of the demand; and
3. whether the director **lacks independence** from someone who received such a benefit or
   who faces such a likelihood of liability.

**Exculpated claims do not satisfy prong two**, because they do not expose directors to a
substantial likelihood of liability.

## Facts

- Zuckerberg took the **Giving Pledge** in December 2010 and by March 2015 was working on an
  accelerated plan to donate **$2–3 billion of Facebook stock annually**.
- Facebook's legal team warned he could sell only **$3–4 billion** before dropping below
  majority voting control, and proposed following the **"Google playbook"**: issue a new
  class of **non-voting** stock he could sell without diluting his votes.
- The legal team recommended a **special committee of independent directors** and noted that
  Google's own reclassification litigation had settled for **$522 million**. Zuckerberg told
  them to "start figuring out how to make this happen."
- The special committee — Desmond-Hellman (chair, then CEO of the Gates Foundation),
  Andreessen and Bowles — negotiated and recommended the reclassification; the board
  approved it.
- **Two of the nine demand-board directors, Chenault and Zients, were not even on the board
  when the reclassification was approved in 2016.** *Board turnover is one of the reasons the
  court reworks the test.*
- The complaint's independence allegations against **Bowles** were that Zuckerberg had waived
  a policy to let him stay on the board and that he had ties to the deal's financial
  advisors. *Rejected.* The complaint did not allege he "was expected to do anything in
  exchange for the waiver, or that remaining a director was financially or personally
  material" to him.
- Bowles had privately told Zuckerberg he was "proud to be a small part of [his] life."
  *Argued and rejected — this "suggest[s] that Zuckerberg and Bowles had a collegial
  relationship, which is not sufficient to compromise" independence.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **The three-part test is adopted**, applied "on a director-by-director basis," treating
  "*Rales* as the general demand futility test" while "draw[ing] upon *Aronson*-like
  principles when evaluating whether particular directors" face the relevant disqualification.
- **Why Aronson needed refinement.** When Aronson was decided, "raising a reasonable doubt
  that the business judgment standard of review would apply exposed directors to a substantial
  likelihood of liability for care violations." **§ 102(b)(7)** "and other developments in
  corporate law have weakened the connection between rebutting the business judgment standard
  and exposing directors to a risk that would sterilize their judgment." Aronson has also
  "proved difficult to apply in many contexts, such as where there is **turnover** on a
  corporation's board."
- **The refinement is not a change in law.** The test "is consistent with *Aronson*, *Rales*,
  and their progeny. Thus, cases properly applying those holdings remain good law."
- **Exculpated claims fail prong two.** Because a § 102(b)(7) provision eliminates monetary
  liability for care breaches, such claims "do not expose directors to a substantial
  likelihood of liability" and cannot excuse demand.
- The complaint failed to plead with particularity that a majority of the demand board lacked
  independence. Dismissal affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *Raising a reasonable doubt that the business judgment rule applies should excuse demand,
  as Aronson's second prong provided.* That equivalence held in 1984 and does not hold now.
  Exculpation severed the link between losing business judgment deference and facing personal
  exposure.
- *Bowles was beholden to Zuckerberg because Zuckerberg waived a policy to keep him on the
  board.* No allegation that anything was expected in return, or that the seat was materially
  important to him.
- *Bowles's expression of personal warmth toward Zuckerberg shows he could not be impartial.*
  A collegial relationship is not enough, and the existence of a "personal friendship" is
  insufficient to establish non-independence (*Beam*).

## Context

The modern statement of demand futility, and the reason the syllabus assigns it with
[[Tooley v. Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette|Tooley]] and
[[In re Riverstone National|Riverstone]]: Tooley decides **whether** Rule 23.1 applies at all,
and Zuckerberg decides **how** its demand requirement is satisfied once it does.

The doctrinal driver is worth naming because it recurs all term: **§ 102(b)(7) changed
everything downstream of it.** Aronson's second prong worked when losing business judgment
review meant care liability. Once exculpation made care damages unavailable, rebutting the
business judgment rule stopped implying personal exposure — which is also why plaintiffs
plead loyalty and bad faith wherever possible. See
[[Charter exculpation of director liability]] and
[[Oversight liability - Caremark and good faith]].

Read the independence holding directly against [[Marchand v. Barnhill]]. Marchand found
reasonable doubt where a director owed his entire career to the defendant's family and had a
building named after him through their fundraising. Zuckerberg finds none where a director was
collegial, grateful, and kept in his seat by a waiver. **The line is between a relationship
that creates a debt and one that creates warmth** — and locating a fact pattern on that line
is the recurring exam task.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Derivative action by Tri-State challenging Facebook's proposed stock reclassification,
  dismissed by the Court of Chancery under Rule 23.1 for failure to make demand or plead
  futility.
- **Posture matters in an unusual way**: the reclassification was abandoned before
  implementation, so the underlying transaction never happened. What survived was the fight
  over who may sue about it.
- Affirmed, with the Supreme Court taking the occasion to adopt the Chancery test.

### Facts

Zuckerberg wanted to give away most of his Facebook stock during his lifetime and to keep
control of Facebook while doing it. Those two goals are in direct tension, since every share
he donated carried votes. Facebook's lawyers told him he had roughly $3–4 billion of headroom
before he fell below majority voting power — against a plan to give away $2–3 billion a year.

The proposed solution was Google's: create a new non-voting class, distribute it as a
dividend, and let him sell the non-voting shares while his voting stock stayed put. The
lawyers flagged that Google's version of this had cost $522 million to settle and recommended
a special committee of independent directors.

The committee — chaired by Desmond-Hellman, with Andreessen and Bowles — negotiated with
Zuckerberg and recommended approval, and the board approved. Litigation followed, and Facebook
abandoned the reclassification before it took effect. By the time Tri-State's complaint was
filed the board had changed: Chenault and Zients had joined and had nothing to do with the
2016 vote.

### Issue

- What test governs demand futility under Court of Chancery Rule 23.1.
- Whether claims exculpated by a § 102(b)(7) charter provision can expose directors to a
  substantial likelihood of liability sufficient to excuse demand.
- Whether the complaint pleaded with particularity that a majority of the demand board lacked
  independence.

### Holding

- **On the test: the three-part, director-by-director inquiry is adopted** — material personal
  benefit, substantial likelihood of liability, or lack of independence from someone in either
  category.
- **On exculpated claims: no.** They cannot produce a substantial likelihood of liability and
  so cannot excuse demand.
- **On independence: not pleaded.** Collegiality, gratitude, a policy waiver without any
  quid pro quo, and professional ties to deal advisors do not raise reasonable doubt.
- **Disposition:** affirmed. The refined test is consistent with Aronson and Rales, which
  remain good law.

### Rule

- Demand futility is assessed director by director against the **demand board** — the board in
  place when the complaint is filed.
- The three questions: material personal benefit from the misconduct; substantial likelihood
  of liability on the claims; lack of independence from someone in either category.
- Exculpated claims do not create a substantial likelihood of liability.
- Rales operates as the general test, with Aronson-like principles informing the
  director-level inquiry.
- A collegial relationship or personal friendship is insufficient to compromise independence.
- Allegations must be pleaded **with particularity**.

### Reasoning

*(Montgomery-Reeves, J.)*

- The opinion's central insight is historical. Aronson's second prong was a proxy: in 1984,
  doubt about business judgment review implied care exposure. § 102(b)(7) broke the proxy, so
  the test now asks about exposure directly.
- Board turnover supplies the second reason. Aronson asks about the board that **made the
  decision**; Rales asks about the board that must **consider the demand**. With two
  directors who were not present for the vote, the Aronson frame does not fit, and the
  Chancery synthesis resolves it by making Rales the general test.
- The adoption is framed as continuity rather than change — "cases properly applying those
  holdings remain good law" — which preserves the accumulated case law while replacing the
  frame.
- On independence the court is exacting about what an allegation must contain: not that a
  relationship exists, but that something was expected in return, or that the benefit was
  material to the director.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; unanimous.
- The tension worth two lines: the court simultaneously tightens demand futility and preserves
  every prior case as good law, which leaves practitioners reconciling a new frame with
  precedent decided under the old one. And prong two now depends on the exculpation clause, so
  the same conduct excuses demand at a company without a § 102(b)(7) provision and does not at
  one with it.

### Cold-call notes

- **Have the three prongs verbatim and in order.** Material personal benefit; substantial
  likelihood of liability; lack of independence from someone in either category. Director by
  director, against the **demand board**.
- **The most likely follow-up is why Aronson needed fixing.** Two reasons: § 102(b)(7) severed
  the link between losing business judgment review and facing liability, and Aronson fails
  where the board has turned over.
- **Know the exculpation consequence.** A care claim at a company with a § 102(b)(7) provision
  cannot excuse demand. This is the same fact that pushes plaintiffs toward
  [[Oversight liability - Caremark and good faith|oversight]] and loyalty theories.
- **Be ready to line this up against Marchand.** Same question, opposite results. Rankin owed
  his career and a building to the Kruse family; Bowles was collegial and grateful. Debt
  versus warmth.
- **The memorable facts** are the $522 million Google settlement the lawyers flagged in
  advance, and Bowles telling Zuckerberg he was "proud to be a small part of [his] life."
- **Numbers:** $2–3 billion a year planned, $3–4 billion of headroom, $522 million Google
  settlement, nine-member demand board, two directors who joined after the vote.
