## Rule

**"[I]nequitable action does not become permissible simply because it is legally
possible."** Directors who use the corporate machinery and the DGCL to perpetuate
themselves in office, and to obstruct the legitimate efforts of dissident stockholders in
a proxy contest, act for inequitable purposes and their action will not be permitted to
stand.

## Facts

- Management advanced the annual meeting date from **January 11, 1972 to December 8,
  1971** — about a month — by amending the bylaw, using "a relatively new section of the
  Delaware Corporation Law."
- The amendment was added to the agenda of the **October 18 directors' meeting two days
  after** the stockholders committee filed its intention with the SEC, on **October 16**,
  to wage a proxy fight. The sequence is the entire case.
- Management had **disingenuously resisted producing a stockholder list** to the dissidents
  and had "otherwise turned a deaf ear to plaintiffs' demands about a change in management
  designed to lift defendant from its present business doldrums."
- Management **hired two established proxy solicitors**. Combined with the list refusal and
  the compressed timetable, the dissidents were left "little chance, because of the
  exigencies of time, including that required to clear material at the S.E.C., to wage a
  successful proxy fight."
- Management **complied strictly** with the DGCL and the bylaws in changing the date.
  *Argued and rejected — this is the fact that generates the holding.*
- Management **attempted to conceal its action as long as possible**; the stockholders
  learned of it unofficially on Wednesday, October 27 and sued the following Monday,
  November 1. *Argued and rejected as laches.*
- Management offered business reasons for the change, which the Chancery Court considered
  and **rejected on the facts**. *The inequitable-purpose finding is a factual one the
  Supreme Court reads out of the lower court's own findings.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The Chancery Court's findings "amount to a finding that management has attempted to
  utilize the corporate machinery and the Delaware Law **for the purpose of perpetuating
  itself in office**; and, to that end, for the purpose of **obstructing the legitimate
  efforts of dissident stockholders** in the exercise of their rights to undertake a proxy
  contest against management."
- "These are **inequitable purposes, contrary to established principles of corporate
  democracy**. The advancement by directors of the by-law date of a stockholders' meeting,
  for such purposes, may not be permitted to stand."
- "When the by-laws of a corporation designate the date of the annual meeting of
  stockholders, **it is to be expected** that those who intend to contest the reelection of
  incumbent management will gear their campaign to the by-law date. It is not to be
  expected that management will attempt to advance the date in order to obtain an
  **inequitable advantage** in the contest."
- The answer to strict statutory compliance "of course, is that **inequitable action does
  not become permissible simply because it is legally possible**."
- The suit was **not tardy**. "Until management changed the date of the meeting, the
  stockholders had no need of judicial assistance," there was no prior warning, and
  management tried to conceal the action.
- "[S]tockholders **may not be charged with the duty of anticipating inequitable action** by
  management, and of seeking anticipatory injunctive relief to foreclose such action, simply
  because the new Delaware Corporation Law makes such inequitable action legally possible."
- **Reversed and remanded**, with instructions to nullify December 8, reinstate January 11
  as the sole date of the next annual meeting, and address the stock record closing date and
  related matters.

**Rejected**

- *Management complied strictly with the provisions of the new Delaware Corporation Law in
  changing the bylaw date.* Legality is not the question. Inequitable action does not become
  permissible simply because it is legally possible.
- *American Hardware establishes that a duly established meeting date will not be enlarged
  by judicial interference at the request of dissident stockholders merely because a proxy
  contest is under way.* The rule is agreed **"in the absence of fraud or inequitable
  conduct,"** and the case is inapposite twice over: it involved stockholders seeking to
  **enlarge** the contest period rather than management seeking to shorten it, and there was
  **no finding of inequitable action** by management.
- *The application for injunctive relief came too late.* The stockholders learned of the
  change unofficially on October 27 and filed on November 1, there was no prior warning, and
  management had attempted concealment.

## Context

Schnell is two sentences of law and a very short opinion, and it is quoted more often than
almost anything else in Delaware corporate law. The operative principle — **equity polices
the exercise of legal power** — is old, but Schnell is where it is applied to the
stockholder franchise, and it opens the chapter on directors, stockholders, voting and
bylaws for exactly that reason.

The casebook frames the chapter around a division of realms: "[t]he realm of the Board of
Directors is the day-to-day management of the corporation... the realm of the shareholder is
to elect directors and vote on certain other decisions," and "Delaware courts emphasize the
importance of the shareholder right to vote and are **suspicious of any direct interference**
with that right." The reading invites you to ask what justifies this formalist approach —
why directors keep enormous discretion over business decisions and almost none over the
mechanics of an election.

The answer supplied the following year, in [[Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp]], is
legitimacy: "[t]he shareholder franchise is the ideological underpinning upon which the
legitimacy of directorial power rests." Blasius also does the work Schnell leaves undone. As
[[Coster v. UIP Companies]] explains, Schnell handles the case where the board acts within
its legal power but is **motivated for selfish reasons** to interfere with the franchise;
Blasius takes up the harder case where the board acts in **good faith** and still thwarts a
vote.

Three limits are worth carrying forward.

**Schnell is narrow by design.** Later Delaware cases confine it to "those instances that
threaten the fabric of the law, or which by an improper manipulation of the law, would
deprive a person of a clear right," and "[a]lmost all of the post-Schnell decisions involved
situations where boards of directors deliberately employed various legal strategies either to
frustrate or completely disenfranchise a shareholder vote." Coster endorses cabining it that
way while calling the "fabric of the law" phrase "a bit hyperbolic."

**Schnell does not reach good-faith interference.** Coster holds that the Court of Chancery
"was correct in this case to cabin Schnell and its equitable review to those cases where the
board acts within its legal power, but is **motivated for selfish reasons**."

**Schnell is now one branch of a unified test.** Coster folds Schnell and Blasius into a
Unocal-shaped enhanced scrutiny: the board must face a real, non-pretextual threat with
proper and non-selfish motivations, and its response must be reasonable in relation to the
threat and neither preclusive nor coercive. Schnell's contribution survives inside the first
half of that inquiry, as the requirement that the motivation be proper.

The same principle recurs outside the franchise. [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] carries Schnell
forward as the caveat that "inequitable action may not be taken under the guise of law," and
the casebook pairs Schnell with Espinoza v. Zuckerberg, excerpted immediately after, where
the controller's informal approval could not substitute for the formality of a stockholder
vote — the mirror image, in which observing the form matters because the form is what
protects the franchise.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from the Court of Chancery's **denial** of dissident stockholders' petition for an
  injunction against the advanced meeting date.
- Reversed and remanded with specific instructions to reinstate the original date.
- The posture is unusual and is why the opinion is so short. The Supreme Court does not
  disturb any factual finding; it **recharacterises** findings the Chancery Court had
  already made — about the list refusal, the deaf ear, the timing of the bylaw amendment and
  the proxy solicitors — as amounting to a finding of inequitable purpose, and then supplies
  the legal consequence the lower court had withheld.

### Facts

Dissident stockholders of Chris-Craft Industries had for some time been pressing management
about a change in management designed, as they saw it, to lift the company out of its
business doldrums. Management turned a deaf ear and disingenuously resisted producing a list
of stockholders to the plaintiffs or their confederates.

On October 16, 1971 the stockholders committee filed with the SEC its intention to wage a
proxy fight. Two days later, at a directors' meeting scheduled for October 18, management
enlarged the meeting's agenda to include a bylaw amendment advancing the annual meeting from
January 11, 1972 to December 8, 1971. It did so under a relatively new section of the
Delaware Corporation Law, and in strict compliance with it.

Management also hired two established proxy solicitors. The combined effect of the refused
stockholder list, the professional solicitation and roughly a month less time — including the
time required to clear proxy material at the SEC — was, as the Chancery Court found, to leave
the plaintiffs little chance of waging a successful proxy fight before December 8.

Management attempted to conceal the change as long as possible. The stockholders learned of
it unofficially on Wednesday, October 27, and filed suit on Monday, November 1. The Court of
Chancery considered all of management's stated business reasons for the change and rejected
them, but denied relief, holding among other things that the application came too late.

### Issue

- Whether directors may advance the annual meeting date, in strict compliance with the DGCL
  and the bylaws, where the purpose is to shorten the time available to dissident
  stockholders for a proxy contest.
- Whether stockholders who sue five days after learning of the change are barred by laches
  for failing to seek anticipatory relief.

### Holding

- **On the merits: the advancement cannot stand.** The Chancery Court's own findings amount
  to a finding that management used the corporate machinery and Delaware law to perpetuate
  itself in office and to obstruct a legitimate proxy contest, which are inequitable purposes
  contrary to established principles of corporate democracy.
- **On the statutory compliance defence: irrelevant.** Inequitable action does not become
  permissible simply because it is legally possible.
- **On laches: no bar.** There was no need for judicial assistance until the date changed, no
  warning was given, concealment was attempted, and stockholders cannot be charged with a duty
  to anticipate inequitable action.
- **Disposition:** reversed and remanded; December 8 nullified and January 11 reinstated as
  the sole date of the next annual meeting.

### Rule

- Directors may not use the corporate machinery and the Delaware corporation law for the
  purpose of perpetuating themselves in office or obstructing the legitimate efforts of
  dissident stockholders to wage a proxy contest.
- Inequitable action does not become permissible simply because it is legally possible.
- Where a bylaw designates the annual meeting date, contestants are entitled to gear their
  campaign to that date, and management may not advance it to gain an inequitable advantage.
- In the absence of fraud or inequitable conduct, a duly established meeting date will not be
  enlarged by judicial interference at the request of dissident stockholders merely because a
  proxy contest is under way.
- Stockholders are not charged with a duty to anticipate inequitable action by management and
  to seek anticipatory injunctive relief.

### Reasoning

*(Herrmann, J.)*

- The opinion works backwards from the Chancery Court's own findings. Those findings
  described the refused stockholder list, the deaf ear to the dissidents, the bylaw amendment
  slipped onto an agenda two days after the SEC filing, and the retention of two proxy
  solicitors — and the Supreme Court simply names what they add up to.
- The legal move is to **separate authority from equity**. The board undoubtedly had the
  power to change the date; the question is whether it may exercise that power for this
  purpose, and equity answers no.
- The expectations passage supplies the underlying wrong. A bylaw date is a **published rule
  of the game**, and contestants are entitled to rely on it; shifting the date mid-contest is
  not a business decision but a change in the rules made by one of the players.
- American Hardware is distinguished on both axes — who was asking, and whether inequitable
  conduct was found — which lets the court preserve the sensible rule that courts do not
  routinely extend meeting dates for the convenience of insurgents.
- The laches holding follows from the same principle and is not a throwaway. If stockholders
  had to sue before the wrong occurred, a board could exploit the interval between an
  announced contest and any possible injunction, and the duty to anticipate would fall on the
  party with less information.

### Dissent / concurrence

*(Wolcott, C.J., dissenting)*

- The plaintiff stockholders had "for a considerable period of time, sought to obtain control
  of the defendant corporation," and "[t]hese attempts took various forms."
- In view of the length of time leading up to the immediate events, the application for
  injunctive relief came too late.
- He would affirm on the Vice Chancellor's opinion.

Wolcott's objection is narrower than it looks and is worth taking seriously. He does not
defend the bylaw change on the merits; he says the plaintiffs had been at this for a long time
and should not be heard at the last minute. The majority's answer is that the clock does not
start until the wrong occurs — but the dissent identifies the real tension, which is that
"inequitable purpose" is a standard with no notice value, and a board cannot know in advance
which legally authorised acts equity will undo.

### Cold-call notes

- **Have the sentence verbatim.** "[I]nequitable action does not become permissible simply
  because it is legally possible." It is the most quoted line in the course and the whole
  holding.
- **Say what made the purpose inequitable, with the timeline.** SEC filing on October 16;
  bylaw amendment added to the October 18 agenda; meeting moved from January 11 to December 8;
  stockholder list refused; two proxy solicitors retained. The purpose is inferred from the
  sequence, not from any admission.
- **Do not overstate the holding.** Schnell does not forbid moving a meeting date. It forbids
  moving it **for that purpose**, and American Hardware survives: absent fraud or inequitable
  conduct, courts will not adjust meeting dates just because a proxy contest is on.
- **Know how narrow Schnell has become.** Later cases confine it to deliberate legal
  strategies used to frustrate or disenfranchise a vote, and Coster approves cabining it to
  cases where the board acts within its legal power but is **motivated for selfish reasons**.
- **Explain what Schnell leaves open, because that is why Blasius exists.** A board acting in
  **good faith** that nonetheless thwarts a vote is outside Schnell, and that is precisely
  Blasius's case.
- **Have the laches point ready.** Stockholders are not charged with anticipating inequitable
  action. Then note the dissent, which is entirely about timing and not about the merits.
- **Connect to Unocal.** The caveat that "inequitable action may not be taken under the guise
  of law" travels into Unocal's proportionality analysis, and Coster eventually folds Schnell
  into Unocal-style enhanced scrutiny altogether.
