## Rule

- A pill adopted on a clear day against **stockholder activism as such** fails Unocal
  prong one: treating all activism as a threat is "an extreme manifestation of the
  proscribed **we-know-better** justification for interfering with the franchise."
- **Short-termism** and **distraction**, asserted in the abstract with no specific
  activist play afoot, are "mere euphemisms for stereotypes of stockholder activism
  generally" and so are not cognisable threats either — the court expressly leaves
  open whether a *particularised* short-termism threat could be.
- Even assuming a legitimate **gap-filling** objective (detecting a lightning-strike
  accumulation that the § 13(d) ten-day window hides), the response must still be
  proportional; a **5% trigger** combined with a **synthetic-equity beneficial
  ownership definition**, an **acting-in-concert provision reaching parallel conduct
  plus a daisy chain**, and a **narrow passive-investor exemption** is not within the
  range of reasonable responses and the pill is enjoined.
- Prong two is not exhausted by Moran's coercive/preclusive screen. "[T]he
  proportionality analysis is tied to a pill's purpose, and **with new purposes come
  new considerations**." A pill that leaves a proxy contest formally possible can still
  be disproportionate if it chills the communications that precede one.

## Facts

Williams is a Delaware natural gas pipeline company, roughly 1.2 billion shares
outstanding and a market capitalisation between about $11.2 and $27.5 billion over the
relevant period. Ownership is institutional and concentrated: about fifty per cent held
by around twenty institutions, with Blackrock, Vanguard and State Street together
holding almost a quarter.

The board had lived through activism before. In late 2011 Soroban (Mandelblatt) and
Corvex (Meister) each took slightly under five per cent and, by a February 2014
standstill, joined the board. They pressed for the Energy Transfer merger, and when it
died they and four other directors tried to remove chief executive Armstrong; when that
failed the six resigned and Meister threatened a full-slate proxy fight, standing down
only when Williams added three new independent directors. Only two of the Director
Defendants, Armstrong and Smith, were on the board then; Smith thought the Soroban and
Corvex campaign had been short-termist and harmful.

In early 2020 COVID-19 and the Saudi–Russia oil price war sent the stock from a $24.04
high to lows comparable to 2010 and 2016, despite earnings twenty-five per cent higher
and materially less debt. At a March 2 meeting the board considered a buyback and chose
instead to preserve liquidity. Outside director **Cogut** — a retired M&A partner who
had put in perhaps a dozen rights plans since the 1980s and who called the pill "the
nuclear weapon of corporate governance" — proposed a rights plan instead. He was not
worried about a takeover or about NOLs; he thought the pandemic warranted "a different
type of pill" to insulate management from activists "trying to influence the control of
the company." An "urgent" March 19 meeting adopted it.

The Plan ran one year and had four features: (i) a **5% trigger**; (ii) a beneficial
ownership definition capturing **derivatives** such as warrants and options; (iii) the
**AIC Provision**, an acting-in-concert definition reaching **parallel conduct** and
carrying a **daisy-chain** concept that aggregates stockholders who need not know the
others exist; and (iv) a **narrow passive-investor exemption** excluding anyone seeking
to direct corporate policies. Features (ii) and (iii) go beyond the federal § 13(d)
baselines for beneficial ownership and group conduct.

Nobody was actually buying. The board identified no specific activist and was not
protecting an NOL; it acted "pre-emptively to interdict hypothetical future threats."

## Court Ruling

- **Threshold.** Under **Selectica**, Unocal governs the adoption of a pill even with
  no imminent takeover in sight. Blasius is not applied.
- **Actual reasons, not papered reasons.** The court makes findings about *why* the
  directors acted, because "[t]hey cannot justify their conduct based on threats that
  they never identified or beliefs they did not hold." The resolution, the March 20
  press release and the March 30 proxy supplement all recited takeover deterrence —
  and all were wrong. The lawyer-drafted record did not reflect the board's intent;
  testimony and contemporaneous email showed the falling stock price was the catalyst
  and activism the target. **This is the case to cite for the proposition that the
  board's articulated rationale is a question of fact.**
- **Prong one, process.** Defendants satisfied the good-faith-reasonable-investigation
  half: nearly all independent outside directors, two meetings, outside legal and
  financial advisors on hand, genuine deliberation. The court notes the second meeting
  had "the impression of window dressing" and that "aspects of the process were less
  than perfect," but "nothing about the process jumps out as unreasonable." **Process
  was not the problem.**
- **Prong one, threat 1 — activism generally.** Rejected outright. Activism reaches the
  board through the franchise, because the proxy fight is the main lever of a holder
  who lacks a majority. Directors "cannot justify their actions by arguing that
  'without their intervention, the stockholders would vote erroneously out of ignorance
  or mistaken belief'"; "[t]he notion that directors know better than the stockholders
  about who should be on the board is no justification at all." Categorically treating
  activism as a threat runs "directly contrary to the ideological underpinnings of
  Delaware law." The court preserves room for *specific, identifiable* activist conduct
  to be a threat.
- **Prong one, threat 2 — short-termism and distraction.** The court declines to decide
  whether particularised short-termism or Cheff-style operational disruption can be
  cognisable. "Reasonable minds can dispute" it. But hypothetical versions cannot be:
  with no "specific, immediate" activist play, these labels "amount to mere euphemisms
  for stereotypes of stockholder activism generally." The doctrinal reason is that the
  short-termism argument "just particularizes the concern that shareholders will cast
  votes in a mistaken assessment of their own best interests" — the we-know-better
  objection again.
- **Prong one, threat 3 — the lightning strike.** Also left undecided, and **assumed
  legitimate for the sake of argument**. The gap is real: § 13(d) requires disclosure
  only after crossing five per cent and allows ten days to file, during which buying
  may continue, and it does not capture informal **wolf pack** coordination. The worry
  about recognising it is that a gap-filling purpose would be "omnipresent" — every
  Delaware corporation subject to the federal regime would have "a ready-made basis for
  adopting a pill," which would "constitute a dramatic turn in Delaware law."
- **Prong two — proportionality; the holding.** Plaintiffs did not argue coercion or
  preclusion, so the question is the range of reasonableness. Comparatively the Plan is
  an outlier: Morgan Stanley told the board that only two per cent of plans had a
  trigger under ten per cent; among non-NOL pills ever, only nine used a five per cent
  trigger and only two of those at Delaware corporations, the other adopted against an
  activist already holding seven per cent; of twenty-one pills adopted between March 13
  and April 6, 2020, only Williams' had a five per cent trigger, and thirteen of those
  twenty-one companies faced live campaigns. Independently of comparison, the features
  "are likely to chill a wide range of anodyne stockholder communications."
- **The AIC Provision is "the primary offender," not the trigger.** A five per cent
  limit still lets an activist take a larger dollar toehold in Williams than most pills
  with higher triggers would allow elsewhere. The AIC language sweeps in communications
  "relating to changing or influencing the control of the Company" with plus factors as
  innocuous as "exchanging information, attending meetings, [or] conducting
  discussions" — routine investor-conference behaviour — and then daisy-chains
  strangers together.
- **Defendants' two answers, both rejected.** (1) The Plan does not preclude a proxy
  contest and anything short of one is irrelevant under Unocal — this "misunderstands
  the proportionality inquiry," which tracks the pill's purpose, and in any event
  cutting off the private soundings by which holders "take the temperature" of each
  other before committing to a contest impedes the contest itself. (2) The "relating to
  changing or influencing control" limit and the other guardrails — but "relating to"
  and "influencing" are "nebulous and broad," as Kahan and Rock argued, and Cogut
  conceded at trial that plainly ordinary conduct would fall inside the AIC Provision.
- **Result.** Defendants "bear the burden to show their actions were reasonable" and
  failed to show that "this extreme, unprecedented collection of features bears a
  reasonable relationship to their stated corporate objective." The Plan is invalid and
  enjoined.

## Context

Williams is the answer to the question Airgas leaves open: is there *any* pill Delaware
will strike? [[Air Products v. Airgas]] shows the pill surviving indefinitely against a
structurally non-coercive, fully financed, all-shares, all-cash offer. Williams shows
one dying on a clear day against nobody. The difference is not judicial mood — it is
that Airgas had an identified bidder at an identified price and Williams had a
hypothesis.

The pill's whole authority chain, [[Moran v. Household International]] on §§ 157, 151(g)
and 141(a), was built for the front-end-loaded two-tier tender offer. Williams is the
case about **mission creep**: flip-over became flip-in, twenty per cent triggers became
fifteen and then ten, takeover defence became NOL protection, and NOL protection became
anti-activist insulation. The court's response is that the Unocal proportionality
inquiry is *purpose-relative* — the Moran coercive/preclusive screen was calibrated to
the takeover purpose and does not travel to a new one unchanged. That sentence is the
most transportable thing in the opinion.

Doctrinally Williams sits on the seam between the takeover line and the franchise line.
[[Schnell v. Chris-Craft Industries]] and [[Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp]] protect
the vote; [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] protects against threats. Williams imports the
*franchise* principle — no we-know-better justification — into *Unocal prong one*, and
does so because activism operates through the proxy fight. After
[[Coster v. UIP Companies]] the two lines are formally merged into one reasonableness
inquiry, and the casebook note on **In re AMC Entertainment** (Del. Ch. Aug. 2023),
which reads Coster to leave Blasius alive in name for board action touching votes that
do not implicate control, treats that survival as "window dressing" — the substance
being, in both branches, a legitimate reason plus a reasonable response.

Practically, Williams also tells you where the *defence* bar landed: the opinion
approvingly notes that "prominent defense firms recommended against the reflexive
adoption of a pill in response to COVID-19," requiring "[c]ompany-specific circumstances
as well as indicia of emerging or present threats." Delaware's caution has created "an
appropriate culture of caution in the board room."

Session 22 also assigns § 13 and Rule 13d-1 and § 14 and Rule 14a-19 — the disclosure
machinery whose gaps the Williams board said it was filling, and the universal proxy rule
that makes a short-slate contest cheaper. See
[[Federal disclosure and the universal proxy - Rules 13d-1 and 14a-19]]. The pill and the
federal rules move in opposite directions on the same problem: how easily an activist can
assemble a position and a slate before the board knows.

Two dates matter for reading the opinion today. Williams describes a **ten-day** § 13(d)
window; the SEC's **2023 amendments** cut the initial Schedule 13D deadline to **five
business days**, so the delay gap the board invoked is roughly half what it was. And
**Rule 14a-19**, the universal proxy, took effect in 2022 — the year after this
decision — making the short-slate campaign the board feared substantially cheaper to run.
The **wolf-pack gap** under § 13(d)(3) is the one that survives both changes.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Post-trial opinion, Court of Chancery, **McCormick, V.C.** (Feb. 26, 2021), affirmed
  by the Delaware Supreme Court in November 2021.
- Stockholder plaintiffs sued to invalidate the rights plan itself; they did **not**
  plead coercion or preclusion, which narrows prong two to the range-of-reasonableness
  question and is why the trigger comparisons and the AIC chilling evidence carry the
  analysis.
- Under **Unocal** the **defendants bear the burden**, so a failure of proof is a
  defendants' loss. That allocation does real work here: on threats two and three the
  court declines to decide the doctrinal question and rules against the board anyway,
  because it was the board's job to establish reasonableness.
- Relief: the Plan enjoined. Note what is *not* at issue — no damages, no personal
  liability for the directors, and no finding of bad faith. Williams is about the
  validity of a defensive measure, not about director culpability.

### Facts

The Plan's four features again, because the exam question is usually "which feature
kills it":

1. **5% trigger.** Unprecedented outside the NOL context; only nine such pills ever,
   two in Delaware, the other adopted against a holder already at seven per cent.
2. **Beneficial ownership including derivatives** — warrants, options, synthetic equity
   — beyond the federal default.
3. **AIC Provision** — "acting in concert" extended to **parallel conduct** with a
   **daisy chain**, so A and C are aggregated through B even if A has never heard of C.
   Beyond § 13(d)'s express-agreement group standard.
4. **Narrow passive-investor exemption** — excludes anyone who seeks to direct corporate
   policies, which is most of what an engaged institution does.

The board's ownership context matters to the chilling story: half the company is held by
about twenty institutions, and the three index giants hold nearly a quarter between
them. A provision that penalises "exchanging information, attending meetings, [or]
conducting discussions" among such holders operates on the ordinary texture of that
shareholder base.

### Issue

1. Does Unocal apply to the adoption of a rights plan on a clear day, with no takeover
   pending? (Yes, under Selectica.)
2. Did the board identify a legitimate threat — activism as such, hypothetical
   short-termism or distraction, or hypothetical rapid accumulation past five per cent?
3. If some threat is assumed legitimate, was this particular combination of features a
   proportional response to it?

### Holding

- Unocal applies. Good faith and reasonable investigation established.
- Activism as a category is **not** a cognisable threat.
- Hypothetical short-termism and distraction are **not** cognisable threats; whether
  particularised versions could be is left open.
- Whether gap filling is a legitimate objective is left open and **assumed** in
  defendants' favour.
- The Plan is **not proportional** to that assumed threat. Invalid; enjoined.

### Rule

Enhanced scrutiny under [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]], as applied to a rights plan under
[[Moran v. Household International]] and extended to clear-day adoptions by Selectica:

- **Prong one** has two parts — (a) good faith and reasonable investigation, and (b) a
  *legitimate* threat. Williams is the case where a board wins (a) and loses (b), which
  is why "the process was fine" is never a complete answer.
- The threat must be the one the board **actually** identified, established as a matter
  of fact, not the one recited in the resolution or press release.
- A justification that reduces to "the stockholders would vote wrongly" is not a threat,
  whatever it is called.
- **Prong two** asks whether the response falls within a **range of reasonableness**,
  and where coercion and preclusion are not alleged that range is assessed against the
  pill's own stated purpose, market practice, and the breadth of the operative
  definitions — including effects on conduct short of a proxy contest.

### Reasoning

The engine of the opinion is the **franchise premise**. Activists without a majority
influence boards through the threat of a proxy fight; therefore anti-activist measures
are franchise measures; therefore the anti-entrenchment principle of the voting cases
governs what may count as a threat. Once that move is made, the first two asserted
threats collapse into the forbidden we-know-better rationale, and the court can dispose
of them without weighing the policy merits of short-termism at all.

On the third threat the court chooses *assumption over decision*, twice, and this is a
deliberate craft choice worth noticing. Deciding that gap filling is legitimate would
hand every Delaware corporation a standing justification — "an omnipresent
justification" — and convert a situational defence into a general licence, undoing the
fact-intensive treatment these "nuclear weapon[s] of corporate governance" have always
received. Deciding that it is *not* legitimate would foreclose a genuine regulatory gap
the scholarship takes seriously. Assuming it, then losing on proportionality, leaves the
doctrine where it was while still striking the pill.

Proportionality is then argued on two independent tracks. **Comparative**: the numbers
above, which establish the Plan as a market outlier several times over. **Intrinsic**:
the AIC Provision's actual operation, supported by the proxy solicitor's testimony about
chilling and by Cogut's own concession that ordinary conduct falls inside it. The
intrinsic track is what defeats the "you can still run a proxy contest" answer —
proportionality is measured against the purpose asserted, and the conduct this pill
reaches is the pre-contest conversation, not the contest.

Note the evidentiary texture: Morgan Stanley's own board presentation supplies the
comparative statistics used against the board, and the director who conceived the pill
supplies the concession that sinks the AIC guardrails. The board's own record is the
plaintiffs' case.

### Dissent / concurrence

None — trial opinion. The Delaware Supreme Court affirmed. The nearest thing to a
counter-voice is the court's own repeated "reasonable minds can dispute," which flags
two live questions it refuses to resolve: whether *particularised* short-termism or
disruption is ever a cognisable threat, and whether a *true* gap-filling pill — narrower
than this one, tracking the § 13(d) window rather than sweeping in parallel conduct —
would survive.

### Cold-call notes

- **What was the threat?** Three of them, all hypothetical, and that is the whole case.
  The board admitted it knew of no activist play afoot.
- **Why didn't the good process save them?** Because prong one has two halves. The court
  says so expressly: "The real problem is not the process that Defendants followed, but
  the threats they identified."
- **Which feature was fatal?** Not the 5% trigger — the **AIC Provision**, "the primary
  offender." Be ready to say why: the trigger limits size, the AIC Provision limits
  speech.
- **What does Moran not answer?** Whether a pill adopted for a *new* purpose is
  proportional. "[W]ith new purposes come new considerations."
- **What did the court refuse to decide?** Whether gap filling is a legitimate objective
  and whether particularised short-termism is a threat. Do not overclaim Williams as
  holding that anti-activist pills are per se invalid.
- **Where does the board's stated rationale come from?** Findings of fact. The
  resolution, press release and proxy supplement said takeover deterrence and the court
  found that false. Good line for any Unocal question with a papered record.
- **Contrast with [[Cheff v. Mathes]]:** there the disruption concern was concrete and
  attached to an identified buyer; here "disruption" was an abstraction.
- **Contrast with [[Air Products v. Airgas]]:** identified bidder, identified price,
  pill upheld; no bidder, no price, pill enjoined.
