## Rule

A board's **"utter failure to attempt to assure a reasonable information and reporting
system exists"** is bad faith and a breach of the duty of **loyalty**. Where a company's
central compliance risk is obvious — for a monoline food manufacturer, food safety — the
board must have *some* board-level system for monitoring it. Management-level controls
and regulatory compliance are not a substitute for board-level oversight.

Separately: deep and longstanding personal and professional ties can defeat a director's
independence for demand-futility purposes, and independence is assessed **against the
decision actually at issue** — suing someone is not the same as voting against them.

## Facts

**On oversight**

- Blue Bell is a **monoline** company making a single product. "Blue Bell can only thrive
  if its consumers enjoyed its products and were confident that its products were safe to
  eat. That is, one of Blue Bell's central compliance issues is **food safety**." *This
  framing is the case.*
- **2009–2014, a continuous regulatory record**: FDA condensation dripping into cartons
  and wafers (2009, reported directly to CEO Paul Kruse); Alabama equipment on the floor
  and a ceiling in disrepair (2010); FDA ten violations including the **same persisting
  condensation drip**, ripped ingredient containers, inconsistent handwashing, "a spider
  and its web near the ingredients" (2010); Alabama drips, standing water, open tank lids
  (2011); Oklahoma failure to control contamination (2012); repeated Alabama cleaning and
  repair orders (2012, 2013, 2014).
- **Five positive listeria tests in 2013. Ten in 2014**, including repeated positives on
  consecutive samples "evidencing the inadequacy of the Company's remedial methods."
- **The board minutes are bereft.** January, February, March and April 2014 meetings
  reflect no report or discussion of listeria. The April minutes record that plant
  operations "were discussed briefly" and that Bridges reported "a good report from the
  TCEQ."
- Three pleaded structural failures: **no board committee** charged with monitoring food
  safety; **no full-board process** devoting part of any meeting to food safety compliance;
  and **no protocol or expectation** that management would deliver food safety reports.
  "[I]t is inferable that there was no expectation of reporting to the board of any kind."
- When the crisis broke in February 2015, the board still was not told. It met on 19
  February — after positive Texas tests on the 13th — with **no listeria discussion**.
- After the first recall, the board's recorded response was a resolution **"express[ing]
  support for Blue Bell's CEO, management, and employees."** *Instead of emergency
  meetings, "Blue Bell's board left the company's response to management."*
- Consequences: total product recall, three plants shut, **over a third of the workforce**
  laid off, a liquidity crisis forcing a **dilutive private equity investment**, and eight
  people sickened — **three of the five Kansas victims died**.

**On Rankin's independence**

- Rankin was hired by **Ed Kruse**, the current CEO's father, as his administrative
  assistant in 1981, promoted to CFO five years later, served 28 years, and was elected to
  the board in 2004.
- **The Kruse family spearheaded a campaign raising over $450,000** to name a Blinn College
  agricultural facility after him.
- Rankin had voted differently from Paul Kruse on separating the CEO and Chairman roles.
  *Relied on below and rejected — the two decisions are not comparable.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Reversed on both grounds.**
- **On independence:** the pled facts "support a reasonable inference that there are very
  warm and thick personal ties of respect, loyalty, and affection between Rankin and the
  Kruse family," creating reasonable doubt he could impartially decide whether to sue Paul
  Kruse. Rankin "owes an important debt of gratitude and friendship" for his first job, his
  progression from entry level to top manager and director, and the building named for him.
- **Independence is decision-specific.** The Court of Chancery "ignored that the decision
  whether to sue someone is materially different and more important than the decision
  whether to part company with that person on a vote about corporate governance," and "the
  nature of the decision at issue must be considered."
- Delaware law "has recognized that deep and longstanding friendships are meaningful to
  human beings," and must not rest "on a reductionist view of human nature" (*Oracle*).
  Social acquaintance alone is not enough; ties of this depth are.
- **On the Caremark claim:** under Caremark and *Stone v. Ritter* a director must make a
  good faith effort to oversee operations; failing to do so **breaches the duty of loyalty**
  and requires showing the fiduciary acted in bad faith, "the state of mind traditionally
  used to define the mindset of a disloyal director."
- The complaint pleads particularized facts supporting a reasonable inference that the board
  **"failed to implement any system to monitor Blue Bell's food safety performance or
  compliance"**, and that during a crucial period when yellow and red flags reached
  management, "there was no equivalent reporting to the board."

**Rejected**

- *The plaintiff is really challenging the **effectiveness** of controls in particular
  instances, which is not a valid Caremark theory.* This was Chancery's ground and it
  misreads the complaint. The allegation is not that a system worked badly but that at
  board level **no system existed at all**.
- *Blue Bell had management-level food safety protocols and was subject to comprehensive
  FDA and state regulation, so compliance was addressed.* Operational compliance by
  management is not board-level oversight. The question is whether **the board** made a good
  faith effort to have information reach it.
- *Rankin's vote against Paul Kruse on the chairman question shows independence.*
  Governance disagreement does not predict willingness to sue, and "[c]ausing a lawsuit to
  be brought against another person is no small matter, and is the sort of thing that might
  plausibly endanger a relationship" (*Sandys v. Pincus*).

## Context

The case that shows what [[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]]
prong one actually requires, and it is the rare complaint to clear it. Put the three cases
of this session side by side and the standard becomes legible:

- **Caremark** — extensive compliance apparatus, good faith effort, claims "extremely weak."
- **Marchand** — no board committee, no board process, no reporting protocol, for the single
  risk that could destroy the company. Bar cleared.
- **[[Segway Inc. v. Cai]]** — a functioning finance function and disappointing numbers, with
  no wrongdoing alleged at all. Nowhere near.

The doctrinal addition is **mission criticality**. Caremark asks whether a system exists;
Marchand asks *for what*. A monoline food company that monitors everything except food
safety has not made a good faith effort, however much else it monitors. That is what makes
this case citable far beyond ice cream.

Two forward links. Because *Stone v. Ritter* locates oversight in the duty of **loyalty**,
the claim survives an exculpatory charter provision — see
[[Charter exculpation of director liability]] — which is why plaintiffs press it and why
[[Segway Inc. v. Cai]] polices the boundary. And the independence holding is demand-futility
law, which the syllabus takes up directly with [[United Food (Tri-State) Pension Fund v. Zuckerberg]] and [[In re Oracle Corp. Derivative Litigation]].

Read against [[Scott v. Depeyster]] for the long arc: 1832 asked whether the examinations
matched industry practice, and excused directors who could easily have caught the fraud.
Marchand asks whether the board tried at all, and does not excuse directors who never looked.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Stockholder derivative suit against two executives (CEO Paul Kruse and VP of Operations
  Greg Bridges) for care and loyalty, and against the directors under Caremark.
- Dismissed below for failure to plead **demand futility**. On the management claims the
  plaintiff "needed eight directors for a majority, but only had seven" — the entire appeal
  on that branch turns on **one director**.
- **Posture is decisive throughout.** Every fact is an allegation; the Court is "bound to
  draw all fair inferences in the plaintiff's favor from the well-pled facts." The holding
  is that a complaint survives, not that the directors are liable.

### Facts

Blue Bell Creameries, founded in Brenham, Texas in 1907, is one of the country's largest ice
cream manufacturers and a Delaware corporation. It made one kind of thing, in three plants
across Texas, Oklahoma and Alabama, in a heavily regulated industry subject to FDA rules and
to each state's own food handling regulations.

From 2009 onward inspectors kept finding the same category of problem: condensation dripping
onto or near product, standing water, equipment and ceilings in disrepair, open tanks,
unprotected utensils, inconsistent hygiene. Some findings were reported directly to CEO Paul
Kruse. In 2013 the company's own tests returned five positives for listeria; in 2014, ten,
including consecutive positives on the same samples showing remediation was not working.

None of it reached the board. The minutes record briefly discussed plant operations and a
good report from an environmental agency. In early 2015 listeria moved from the plants into
the products. Texas health authorities, alerted by South Carolina, confirmed positives in
Blue Bell samples on 13 February; the board met on the 19th and did not discuss it. A limited
recall followed on the 23rd. The board met two days later and was told the FDA was working
with Texas inspectors and more would be known "within the next days or weeks."

In March the CDC connected Kansas infections to Blue Bell's Texas plant. The board's recorded
action was a resolution of support for management. By 20 April the company recalled
everything it made. Five adults in Kansas and three in Texas were sickened; three of the
Kansas five died.

### Issue

- Whether the complaint pleads particularized facts raising a reasonable doubt that director
  Rankin could impartially consider a demand to sue the CEO, given his career-long ties to
  the CEO's family.
- Whether a complaint alleging that a board had no committee, no process and no reporting
  protocol for the company's central compliance risk states a Caremark claim, or merely
  challenges the effectiveness of existing controls.

### Holding

- **On independence: reasonable doubt pleaded.** Ties of this depth — first job, mentorship,
  three decades of promotion, board seat, and a building named for him through the family's
  fundraising — support a pleading-stage inference of non-independence. A contrary vote on a
  governance question does not answer it, because independence is measured against the
  decision at issue, and deciding whether to sue is different in kind.
- **On Caremark: claim stated.** The complaint alleges not ineffective monitoring but the
  absence of any board-level system for the company's most important compliance risk, plus
  a record of flags reaching management and never the board. That supports a reasonable
  inference the directors consciously failed to attempt to assure a reporting system existed.
- **Disposition:** reversed on both holdings and remanded.

### Rule

- Directors have a duty "to exercise oversight" and to monitor the corporation's operational
  viability, legal compliance, and financial performance.
- A board's "utter failure to attempt to assure a reasonable information and reporting system
  exists" is an act of **bad faith** in breach of the duty of **loyalty**.
- A plaintiff must show the fiduciary acted in bad faith — "the state of mind traditionally
  used to define the mindset of a disloyal director."
- Where a company has an obvious **central compliance risk**, board-level monitoring of that
  risk is required; management-level controls and external regulation do not substitute.
- A challenge to the **existence** of board-level monitoring is a valid Caremark theory; a
  challenge to the **effectiveness** of existing controls is not.
- Independence is assessed in light of **the particular decision** at issue.
- Deep and longstanding personal or professional relationships can defeat independence at the
  pleading stage; mere social acquaintance cannot.

### Reasoning

*(Strine, C.J.)*

- The oversight analysis is built from the company outward. Because Blue Bell makes one
  product that people eat, food safety is not one compliance topic among many — it is the
  compliance topic, and a board that monitors nothing else must monitor that.
- The Court then moves from absence of structure to inference of state of mind. No committee,
  no process, no protocol, plus two years of flags stopping at management, supports the
  inference that the directors *consciously* failed to attempt a system. That inferential
  step is what converts a structural gap into bad faith.
- The Chancery court's "effectiveness versus existence" distinction is accepted as good law
  and then held inapplicable — a narrower and more durable move than rejecting it.
- On independence, the Court reasons from ordinary human experience rather than from formal
  criteria, quoting *Oracle*'s warning against "a reductionist view of human nature." The
  $450,000 building is treated as the capstone rather than the core; the core is a career.
- The decision-specificity point is the most transferable holding in the case: suing someone
  "might plausibly endanger a relationship" in a way a governance vote does not.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; unanimous.
- The pressure point worth two lines: nearly every fact supporting the oversight inference is
  an **absence** — no committee, no protocol, no minutes entry. Proving a negative at the
  pleading stage is easy and proving it false is hard, so the decision gives plaintiffs a
  template that any board without a dedicated committee for its main risk will struggle to
  answer. The Court's answer is mission criticality, which limits the template to risks
  central to the business rather than to every risk a company faces.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect to be asked what distinguishes this from Caremark**, and answer structurally, not
  by degree. Caremark's board had a compliance committee, an audit plan, an outside auditor,
  a hotline and a compliance officer. Blue Bell's board had **nothing** on the one risk that
  mattered. Existence versus effectiveness is the vocabulary.
- **Lead with "monoline."** The word does the work. A diversified manufacturer with a
  comparable record might come out differently, because food safety would be one risk among
  many rather than the business itself.
- **The board's resolution of support is the most quotable fact.** Two days after learning
  life-threatening bacteria was in its products, the board's recorded action was to encourage
  management. If asked what a good faith board would have done, start there.
- **Have the independence holding separately.** It is fifty percent of the case and easy to
  skip. Key move: independence is measured against *the decision at issue*, and a governance
  disagreement does not show willingness to sue a benefactor.
- **Know why it is a loyalty claim.** *Stone v. Ritter* put oversight inside loyalty via good
  faith, which is what keeps it outside § 102(b)(7) exculpation. That is the reason this
  theory is worth pleading at all.
- **Numbers:** flags from 2009 through 2014; five positive listeria tests in 2013, ten in
  2014; three deaths; over a third of the workforce laid off; $450,000 raised to name the
  building; the plaintiff needed eight directors and had seven.
