## Rule

A director's obligation includes "a duty to attempt in good faith to assure that a
corporate information and reporting system, which the board concludes is adequate,
exists." Liability for failing to monitor requires **"a sustained or systematic
failure of the board to exercise oversight — such as an utter failure to attempt to
assure a reasonable information and reporting system exists"**, which establishes the
lack of good faith that is a necessary condition to liability.

## Facts

- Caremark had roughly **7,000 employees and ninety branch operations** under a
  **decentralised** management structure. *Scale and decentralisation are why the
  board could not know, and why the question is systems rather than knowledge.*
- The Anti-Referral Payments Law forbade paying remuneration to induce Medicare or
  Medicaid referrals. Caremark contracted with physicians for consulting and research,
  some of whom referred patients — lawful on their face, but "obviously raised a
  possibility of unlawful 'kickbacks.'"
- **The law was genuinely unclear.** Few decisions construed the ARPL; HHS safe-harbour
  regulations in 1991 were "narrowly drawn" and left many agreements outside them.
  Caremark publicly disclosed the uncertainty, and inside and outside counsel advised
  the contracts were lawful.
- **The board did a great deal.** A "Guide to Contractual Relationships" reviewed
  annually by lawyers and revised five times; Zone President approval for every
  physician contract; an internal audit plan; Price Waterhouse as outside auditor,
  which in February 1993 found **no material weaknesses**; a new internal audit charter;
  an ethics manual with a **confidential toll-free hotline**; mandatory training; home
  office approval for disbursements; and the CFO appointed compliance officer.
- A 1994 federal indictment charged that **over $1.1 million** went to one physician
  to induce distribution of a growth hormone drug, some "in the guise of research
  grants," where he "performed virtually none of the consulting functions described."
- Caremark pleaded guilty to **a single felony count of mail fraud** and paid, in all,
  **approximately $250 million**. *Argued as proof of failure and rejected — magnitude
  of loss is not a breach.*
- **No senior officer or director was charged**, and at sentencing the United States
  **stipulated** that no senior executive "participated in, condoned, or was willfully
  ignorant of wrongdoing."
- The settlement's consideration was modest: a Compliance and Ethics Committee of four
  directors meeting quarterly, semi-annual board discussion of regulatory changes,
  written disclosure of financial relationships to patients, and compliance officers
  reporting semi-annually.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Two distinct contexts for inattention liability.** First, a board **decision** that
  proves ill-advised — reviewed under the business judgment rule, and here Allen is
  categorical: compliance with the duty of care "can never appropriately be judicially
  determined by reference to the **content** of the board decision," apart from the good
  faith or rationality of the process. Substantive second-guessing by "ill-equipped
  judges or juries" would injure investors. Second, **unconsidered inaction** — the
  oversight claim.
- **Graham v. Allis-Chalmers is read narrowly.** Its line that "absent cause for
  suspicion there is no duty upon the directors to install and operate a corporate
  system of espionage" cannot mean a board has no responsibility to assure information
  systems exist. Graham stands only for the proposition that, absent grounds to suspect
  deception, boards and senior officers cannot be charged "simply for assuming the
  integrity of employees."
- **Three reasons the broader reading fails in 1996**: Delaware's increasing seriousness
  about the board's role; "relevant and timely information is an essential predicate for
  satisfaction of the board's supervisory and monitoring role under **Section 141**";
  and the **federal Organizational Sentencing Guidelines**, which create powerful
  incentives to have compliance programmes in place.
- **The duty, stated.** The board must "exercise a good faith judgment that the
  corporation's information and reporting system is **in concept and design adequate**."
  The level of detail "is a question of business judgment," and no system "will remove
  the possibility that the corporation will violate laws."
- **The liability standard** is "quite high," and deliberately so — a demanding test
  "makes board service by qualified persons more likely, while continuing to act as a
  stimulus to good faith performance."
- **The elements of the claim**: that directors (1) knew or (2) should have known
  violations were occurring; (3) took no steps in a good faith effort to prevent or
  remedy; and (4) that such failure proximately caused the losses.
- Applied: essentially no evidence of sustained failure. The information systems "appear
  to have represented a good faith attempt to be informed." Claims "extremely weak."
  Settlement approved as fair and reasonable.

**Rejected**

- *A $250 million loss from criminal conduct shows the board failed to monitor.* "The
  liability that eventuated in this instance was huge. But the fact that it resulted from
  a violation of criminal law alone does not create a breach of fiduciary duty."
- *The directors should have predicted the consequences of the company's practices.*
  Neither the failure to predict, "nor the scale of the liability, gives rise to an
  inference of breach."
- *The board did not know the detail in the indictments.* "[T]he duty to act in good
  faith to be informed cannot be thought to require directors to possess detailed
  information about all aspects of the operation of the enterprise" — a requirement
  "inconsistent with the scale and scope of efficient organization size."

## Context

The modern answer to the question [[Scott v. Depeyster]] asked in 1832 — when do
directors answer for wrongdoing they neither committed nor detected? Scott used an
**industry-practice** benchmark; Caremark asks instead whether the board made a good
faith effort to have a **system**. The reframing matters enormously downstream: because
the standard is good faith rather than diligence, *Stone v. Ritter* later locates
oversight in the **duty of loyalty**, which puts it outside
[[Charter exculpation of director liability|§ 102(b)(7)]] and makes it one of the few
routes to director damages that exculpation cannot close.

That is also why plaintiffs plead oversight so often and win so rarely. The casebook's
demand-futility note makes the mechanics explicit: with no interested transaction, a
plaintiff must show with particularity a **substantial likelihood of liability** to
excuse demand, and the substantive standard is "possibly the most difficult theory in
corporation law upon which a plaintiff might hope to win a judgment."

Allen's two-context framing maps the whole course. The decision branch is
[[Duty of care - informed decision]] and [[Business judgment rule - scope of protection]]; the inaction branch is this module. Which branch a fact pattern sits in
determines the standard, the exculpation analysis, and the pleading burden.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Consolidated derivative actions, on a Rule 23.1 motion to approve a **settlement** as
  fair and reasonable — not a merits adjudication and not a motion to dismiss.
- **Posture does unusual work here.** Allen says the court "does not determine contested
  facts," is "constrained by the absence of a truly adversarial process" since both
  sides support the settlement, and that the recited facts "do not deserve the respect
  that judicial findings after trial are customarily accorded."
- Which means the most influential oversight standard in Delaware law was announced in a
  settlement-approval opinion where nothing was decided. It became law because the
  Supreme Court adopted it in *Stone v. Ritter*, not because Caremark held anything.

### Facts

Caremark was spun off from Baxter International in 1992 and listed on the NYSE; the
practices that caused the problem predated the spin-off. It provided alternative-site
health care — infusion therapy, growth hormone therapy, HIV/AIDS treatment, hemophilia
therapy — with most revenue coming from third-party payers including Medicare and
Medicaid.

The legal risk was structural rather than hidden. Caremark paid physicians under
consulting agreements and research grants; some of those physicians referred patients.
The ARPL prohibited remuneration to induce referrals, but almost no case law construed
it, and the 1991 HHS safe harbours were narrow enough that most of Caremark's
arrangements sat outside them without clearly violating anything. Counsel said the
contracts were lawful. The company told its shareholders in the 1992 annual report that
no assurance could be given its interpretation would prevail.

The investigation ran four years across HHS, DOJ, and multiple federal and state
agencies. In August 1994 a Minnesota grand jury returned a forty-seven-page indictment.
Caremark ultimately pleaded guilty to one count of mail fraud and paid roughly $250
million in criminal fines, civil damages and reimbursements — while the government
stipulated that no senior executive had participated in, condoned, or been wilfully
ignorant of the conduct.

### Issue

- What standard governs a claim that directors are liable for corporate losses caused by
  employee misconduct the board neither knew of nor authorised.
- Whether the Caremark board's conduct met that standard, for purposes of valuing the
  claims being released by settlement.

### Holding

- **On the standard: a good-faith duty to assure a system.** Directors must attempt in
  good faith to assure that an information and reporting system, which the board
  concludes is adequate, exists. Liability requires a sustained or systematic failure —
  an **utter failure to attempt** — which establishes lack of good faith.
- **On the facts: no breach.** The record shows active consideration by management and
  the board of the very structures at issue, and no knowing or intentional violation.
  The claims are "extremely weak."
- **Disposition:** settlement approved, the modest consideration being adequate given
  how weak the released claims were.

### Rule

- Director liability for inattention arises in two contexts: an ill-advised **decision**,
  and **unconsidered inaction**.
- For decisions, the business judgment rule is process-oriented; the content of the
  decision is not a ground for liability however wrong it proves, provided the process
  was rational or employed in a good faith effort to advance corporate interests.
- Boards must assure that information and reporting systems exist that are "reasonably
  designed to provide to senior management and to the board itself timely, accurate
  information" on both legal compliance and business performance.
- The **level of detail** of such a system is a question of business judgment.
- Liability requires a sustained or systematic failure to exercise oversight, such as an
  utter failure to attempt to assure a reasonable system exists, evidencing lack of good
  faith.
- The plaintiff must show the directors knew or should have known of violations, took no
  good faith steps to prevent or remedy them, and that the failure proximately caused the
  loss.
- Neither the scale of a loss nor the fact that it arose from criminal conduct creates a
  breach.

### Reasoning

*(Allen, Ch.)*

- The opinion's method is to reason from institutional consequence rather than from
  precedent. A rule permitting objective review of decisions "would expose directors to
  substantive second guessing by ill-equipped judges or juries, which would, in the
  long-run, be injurious to investor interests."
- Learned Hand in *Barnes v. Andrews* supplies the core: "it is the same corporation that
  chose him that now seeks to charge him... Must a director guarantee that his judgment
  is good?" Allen extracts from it "the core element of any corporate law duty of care
  inquiry: whether there was good faith effort to be informed and exercise judgment."
- Graham is confined rather than overruled, and the three grounds for confining it are
  all external to corporate doctrine — the Supreme Court's evolving seriousness about
  boards, § 141's structural need for information, and federal sentencing policy.
- The standard's height is defended openly as a policy choice: a demanding test "is
  probably beneficial to corporate shareholders as a class."
- Note what the opinion does **not** do. It never says the system must work. It requires a
  good faith judgment that the system is "in concept and design adequate," and concedes
  no system will prevent all violations.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; single Chancellor, on settlement approval.
- The tension worth noting: the standard is announced in an opinion that decides no facts
  and approves a settlement Allen himself calls "very modest," delivering changes that
  "do not impress one as very significant" because the board already had a compliance
  committee. The most consequential passage in modern Delaware oversight law is,
  strictly, dicta in a fairness hearing.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect to be asked what the Caremark board did right.** Have the list: the Guide
  revised five times under legal review, Zone President approval of every contract, an
  internal audit plan, a Big Six outside auditor finding no material weaknesses, an
  ethics manual, a confidential hotline, mandatory training, and a designated compliance
  officer. The case is a picture of adequacy, which is why it is assigned before
  [[Marchand v. Barnhill]].
- **The trap** is arguing from the $250 million. Allen addresses it directly and it earns
  nothing. Magnitude of loss is not evidence of breach.
- **Keep the two branches apart.** Decision versus inaction. Naming which branch a fact
  pattern occupies before analysing it is the single most valuable move here, because it
  selects the standard.
- **Know that the word "utter" is doing the work.** Not "inadequate," not "ineffective" —
  an *utter failure to attempt*. [[Marchand v. Barnhill]] is what clearing that bar
  actually requires, and [[Segway Inc. v. Cai]] is what failing it looks like.
- **Be ready on posture.** If pressed on why a settlement opinion became the governing
  standard, the answer is that the Supreme Court adopted it in *Stone v. Ritter*, which
  also relocated oversight into the duty of loyalty.
- **Numbers:** 7,000 employees, 90 branches, ~$250 million total payments, one felony
  count of mail fraud, $1.1 million to a single physician, a 47-page indictment, four
  years of investigation.
