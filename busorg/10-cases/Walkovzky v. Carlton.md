## Rule

Limited liability holds unless the plaintiff pleads that the shareholder was
**conducting the business in his individual capacity**. Undercapitalisation and
fragmentation of a business across many thinly financed corporations do not, by
themselves, state a claim against the shareholder.

## Facts

- Carlton was a stockholder in ten corporations, each holding **two taxicabs** and
  each carrying only the statutory minimum liability insurance of $10,000.
- The ten corporations were alleged to be operated "as a single entity, unit and
  enterprise" as to financing, supplies, repairs, employees and garaging.
- Walkovzky was severely injured by a cab owned by Seon Cab Corporation and
  negligently driven by Marchese.
- The complaint alleged the corporations were undercapitalised and their assets
  intermingled. *Alleged and held insufficient.*
- **The complaint did not allege that Carlton was doing business in his individual
  capacity** — shuttling personal funds in and out of the corporations without
  regard to formality. *This omission decides the case.*
- The theory actually pleaded was **fraud on the public**, not agency. *Argued and
  rejected — the choice of theory is what sinks the complaint.*
- Every operating corporation's sole assets were the cabs themselves, apparently
  subject to mortgages, with income continually drained out. *These are the
  dissent's facts; the majority does not dispute them.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The complaint fails to state a cause of action against Carlton individually.
- Courts pierce the veil "whenever necessary to prevent fraud or to achieve
  equity," guided by **general rules of agency**: whoever uses control of the
  corporation to further his own rather than the corporation's business is liable
  for its acts on respondeat superior principles, in tort as well as contract.
- Two different allegations produce two different results, and the complaint made
  the wrong one:
  - that the corporation is **a fragment of a larger corporate combine** which
    actually conducts the business → only the larger corporate entity is liable;
  - that the corporation is **a dummy for individual stockholders** carrying on the
    business in their personal capacities for personal ends → the stockholder is
    personally liable.
- Deliberately splitting fleet ownership among many corporations "does not ease the
  plaintiff's burden." The analysis is the same as it would be against a single
  corporation.
- The corporate form may not be disregarded merely because corporate assets plus
  mandatory insurance are insufficient to cover the claim.
- If statutory minimum insurance is inadequate to protect the public, "the remedy
  lies not with the courts but with the Legislature."
- **The court expressly leaves the door open**: "This is not to say that it is
  impossible for the plaintiff to state a valid cause of action against the
  defendant Carlton. However, the simple fact is that the plaintiff has just not
  done so here."

**Rejected**

- *The scheme is a fraud on the public, because it is designed so that injured
  people cannot recover.* The principle pleaded was fraud rather than agency, and
  the fraud theory "cannot withstand analysis": if it is not fraudulent for a
  single owner-operator to carry only the statutory minimum, the enterprise "does
  not become either illicit or fraudulent merely because it consists of many such
  corporations."
- *Undercapitalisation plus intermingled assets is enough.* Those allegations were
  made and were still "barren of any sufficiently particularized statements" that
  Carlton did business personally.
- *A holding for Carlton lets a known abuse continue.* A contrary rule "would apply
  equally to the thousands of cabs which are owned by their individual drivers who
  conduct their businesses through corporations" and carry the statutory minimum.

## Context

Assigned with [[Meinhard v. Salmon]] under "Incidents of Partnership and
Incorporation," and the pairing marks the boundary the whole course sits on.
Meinhard shows how demanding the law is **inside** a fiduciary relationship;
Walkovzky shows how little the law gives an outsider trying to reach through the
corporate form. The casebook's framing note makes the related point that internal
affairs are governed almost exclusively by the law of the state of incorporation,
which is why Delaware dominates and why this New York case appears as a seminal
outsider.

The mechanism is worth holding precisely: veil piercing runs on **agency**
principles, not on a free-floating fairness inquiry. That is why the majority
separates the enterprise-entity theory from the dummy-corporation theory — they
identify different principals, so they produce different defendants.

The modern Delaware standard quoted in the casebook's note requires pleading facts
supporting an inference that the corporation "has created a sham entity designed to
defraud investors and creditors," with courts weighing adequacy of capitalisation,
solvency, observance of corporate formalities, siphoning of funds by the dominant
shareholder, and whether the company functioned as a facade. Piercing "generally
results not from a single factor, but rather some combination of them," and "an
overall element of injustice or unfairness must always be present." Actual
piercing is uncommon and rarer still for large corporations.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Personal injury action; Carlton moved to dismiss for failure to state a cause of
  action against him individually.
- Special Term granted the motion; the Appellate Division reversed by a divided
  vote, holding a valid cause of action stated; Carlton appealed by leave on a
  certified question.
- **Posture is doing the work here.** The Court of Appeals is reviewing a pleading,
  which is why the opinion reads as a drafting lesson rather than a merits ruling —
  and why the court can say the claim may yet be stated. Most of what the dissent
  wants to rely on is either unpleaded or pleaded without particularity.

### Facts

Vesting a taxi fleet in many corporations of one or two cabs each was, as the
court puts it, "a rather common practice in the taxicab industry." Carlton was a
stockholder in ten such corporations, one of which was Seon Cab Corporation. Each
carried the minimum automobile liability insurance the law required, $10,000.

Walkovzky was run down by a Seon cab driven negligently by Marchese. He sued the
driver, Seon, the other nine corporations, and Carlton personally, alleging that
the corporations were operated as a single enterprise for financing, supplies,
repairs, employees and garaging, and that the multiple corporate structure was an
unlawful attempt to defraud members of the public who might be injured by the cabs.

On the dissent's account of the same facts, the corporations were intentionally
undercapitalised from inception for the purpose of avoiding responsibility for
accidents certain to occur with cabs on the street around the clock, their only
assets were mortgaged vehicles, and income was continually drained out.

### Issue

- Whether a complaint states a cause of action against a shareholder personally
  where it alleges that a business was deliberately fragmented among ten
  undercapitalised corporations each carrying minimum insurance, but does not
  allege that the shareholder conducted the business in his individual capacity.

### Holding

- **No cause of action stated.** The allegations establish at most that Seon was a
  fragment of a larger corporate combine, which would reach the combine and not the
  shareholder. Personal liability requires allegations that Carlton was carrying on
  the business personally, for personal ends, disregarding corporate formalities.
- **The fraud theory fails independently.** Carrying the statutory minimum is not
  fraudulent for one owner-operator, and multiplying the structure does not make it
  so.
- **Disposition:** order of the Appellate Division reversed; the complaint falls
  short, without prejudice to the possibility of a properly pleaded claim.

### Rule

- Incorporation exists precisely to let proprietors escape personal liability, but
  the privilege has limits.
- Courts disregard the corporate form whenever necessary to prevent fraud or
  achieve equity, guided by general rules of agency.
- Whoever uses control of a corporation to further his own rather than the
  corporation's business is liable for the corporation's acts, in contract and in
  tort, on respondeat superior principles.
- An allegation that the corporation is a fragment of a larger **corporate**
  enterprise reaches the larger entity only.
- An allegation that the corporation is a **dummy for individual stockholders**
  conducting business personally reaches the stockholder.
- Inadequacy of corporate assets plus mandatory insurance is not itself a ground to
  disregard the form.
- Conditions on the privilege of incorporation, including insurance minimums, are
  for the legislature.

### Reasoning

*(Fuld, J.)*

- The opinion routes veil piercing through agency, which forces the plaintiff to
  identify **whose** business was really being conducted. That identification
  determines the defendant, and the complaint never makes it.
- The court tests the rule by generalising it: thousands of individual
  owner-drivers incorporate under the Business Corporation Law and carry the
  statutory minimum. A rule reaching Carlton on these allegations reaches all of
  them.
- Institutional competence closes the argument. The Legislature set $10,000 and has
  the constitutional responsibility for conditions on incorporation; the court will
  not raise the figure by decision.

### Dissent / concurrence

*(Keating, J.)*

- Frames the question as whether the policy of limited liability is "so strong that
  it will permit that privilege to continue no matter how much it is abused, no
  matter how irresponsibly the corporation is operated, no matter what the cost to
  the public."
- Relies on the undercapitalisation principle: shareholders who put at risk capital
  "illusory or trifling compared with the business to be done and the risks of
  loss" abuse the separate entity, and that abuse is ground for denying the
  privilege.
- Rejects the statutory-minimum defence. The Legislature set a floor for those
  unable to accumulate assets, not a shield for those who organised corporations
  specifically to avoid responsibility while earning profits sufficient to buy more
  insurance.
- Rejects legislative acquiescence: approval of a scheme with such consequences "is
  not to be so lightly inferred" from a failure to amend.
- Offers a narrow holding, which is the strongest form of his position: a
  participating shareholder of **a corporation vested with a public interest**,
  organised with capital insufficient to meet liabilities certain to arise in the
  ordinary course, may be held personally responsible. Where income cannot cover
  higher premiums, or adequate finances later dwindle, no liability follows.

### Cold-call notes

- **The likeliest question is how to redraft the complaint.** Allege that Carlton
  shuttled personal funds in and out of the corporations without regard to
  formality and to suit his immediate convenience — that he was doing business
  personally. The majority hands you the language, which is a strong signal that
  the drafting point is the lesson.
- **Do not argue the equities.** The majority does not dispute that the structure
  exists to defeat recovery. It holds that the remedy belongs to the Legislature.
  An answer that leans on unfairness is answering the dissent.
- **Keep the two theories apart.** Enterprise entity reaches the combine; dummy
  corporation reaches the stockholder. Mixing them is the commonest error on this
  case, and the majority's whole analysis is that the plaintiff pleaded facts fitting
  the first while asking for the remedy available under the second.
- **Be ready on the dissent's limiting principle.** Keating is not proposing that
  undercapitalisation always pierces — only for a corporation vested with a public
  interest, undercapitalised against liabilities certain to arise, where income
  could have covered more insurance. Stating that narrow version shows the
  counter-position at its strongest.
- **Connect it forward.** The modern Delaware factors in the note — capitalisation,
  solvency, formalities, siphoning, facade, plus an overall element of injustice —
  are the same inquiry with more boxes, and piercing remains rare.
- **Numbers:** ten corporations, two cabs each, $10,000 statutory minimum.
