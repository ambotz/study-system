## What it is

The constitutive documents of an **Illinois non-stock, not-for-profit corporation** — the
University of Chicago, originally incorporated September 10, 1890 — consisting of Restated Articles
of Incorporation filed in 2010 and Bylaws last amended in 2022.

The reading closes the course by removing the thing every other session assumed. There are no
shares, no stockholders, and no residual claimants, so every doctrine built on the equity holder —
the vote, the derivative suit, the appraisal remedy, the duty to maximise value for the residual —
has nothing to attach to. What remains is a **self-perpetuating board** and a **purpose**.

Read it against the [[NVIDIA Certificate of Incorporation]] and [[NVIDIA Bylaws]] from earlier in
the term. Same two-instrument architecture; entirely different allocation of power.

## What to notice

### The three provisions that change everything

- **"The corporation shall have no members."** Four words in Article 3, and they are the whole
  reading. Delaware's non-stock provisions contemplate members who function roughly as stockholders
  do — § 228(b), for instance, lets members act by written consent. This corporation has none, which
  makes the board answerable to no internal constituency at all.
- **"The management of the corporation shall be vested in a Board of Trustees... The acts of the
  Board of Trustees shall be the acts of the corporation for all purposes."** The Illinois analogue
  of 8 Del. C. § 141(a), stated even more absolutely. Compare
  [[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]] and [[Gorman v. Salamone]] — there is no
  one here who could even attempt a constraining bylaw.
- **"Trustees shall be... elected by the Board of Trustees."** The board elects itself. This is the
  structural fact that distinguishes a non-stock corporation from every entity in the course. In a
  stock corporation the franchise is "the ideological underpinning upon which the legitimacy of
  directorial power rests" ([[Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp]]); here there is no franchise, and
  legitimacy has to come from somewhere else.

### The purpose clause does the work the residual claimant does elsewhere

Article 4 runs to a full page of enumerated objects — to provide higher education "to persons of
both sexes on equal terms," to establish and maintain schools and a university "in which may be
taught all branches of higher learning," to prescribe courses of study, employ faculty, fix tuition,
receive and invest gifts, act as trustee for donors, grant degrees, and "generally to pursue and
promote all or any of the objects above named."

That enumeration is the closest thing to an accountability mechanism the documents contain, and it
is exactly the device [[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]] describes: a purpose clause
**orients** a fiduciary relationship, so that fiduciaries "must pursue the corporate purpose
selflessly... but they are **limited to pursuing the corporation's purpose. They cannot pick another
path simply because they prefer it**." In a non-stock corporation, that is nearly the entire content
of the duty of loyalty.

Article 4 is also the historical artefact worth noticing — the **enumerated powers** form of the
1890s, which is precisely the structure of the
[[An Act to Incorporate the National Insurance Company|1815 National Insurance charter]] from early
in the term and of [[Early corporate charters - enumerated powers]]. Modern Delaware charters say
"any lawful act or activity." A purpose-limited charter is now the exception, and a non-stock
corporation is where it survives.

### The 1890 anti-sectarian covenant

"**No religious test or particular religious professions shall ever be held as a requisite** for
elections to said Board or for admission to said University... or for election to any
professorship, or any place of honor or emolument." Drafted into a charter funded by John D.
Rockefeller through the American Baptist Education Society, and preserved verbatim through the 2010
restatement. A charter provision as a permanent commitment device — the thing a board cannot undo
by ordinary action, and the non-stock analogue of an entrenched charter term.

### Board composition and self-perpetuation

- **Forty-seven trustees** at the 2010 restatement; the Bylaws cap the number at **fifty-five**,
  including the President.
- **Five-year staggered terms**, "as equal as is feasible" across five groups — the same
  classification logic as 8 Del. C. § 141(d), but adopted by a board answerable only to itself, and
  serving continuity rather than takeover defence.
- **Mandatory retirement** after the Annual Meeting following a trustee's seventy-fifth birthday,
  waivable for one-year terms on the Trusteeship and Governance Committee's recommendation and a
  majority vote.
- **Removal with or without cause by a majority of the Trustees** — the board can remove its own
  members, which in a stock corporation is a stockholder power under § 141(k).
- **Vacancies filled by the Board at any meeting.**
- **Honorary Trustees and Trustees Emeriti** are expressly **not members of the Board**, do not vote,
  hold no elective office, and are not counted for quorum. A carefully drawn honorific that confers
  no governance rights.

### Meeting and voting mechanics, and where they differ from the DGCL defaults

- **Three regular meetings a year**, the last of which is the Annual Meeting.
- **Quorum is one-third of the Board** — the same floor 8 Del. C. § 216 sets for *stockholder*
  quorums, here applied to directors, and far below the majority a Delaware board needs under
  § 141(b).
- **The act of a majority of those present at a quorum is the act of the Board**, except that four
  matters require **the affirmative vote of a majority of all Trustees**: amending the Articles;
  electing Trustees and elected officers; final action on the annual budget; and designating
  committees to exercise Board authority in management.
- **Notice** of five days for regular meetings, two for special. Special meetings may be called by
  the Chair, a Vice Chair, **any three Trustees**, or the President.

### Amendment — the absence of any external check

- **Bylaws** may be amended or repealed at any regular meeting on five days' notice, **provided the
  amendment is recommended by the Executive Committee** — though that recommendation "shall not
  prevent modification of the proposed amendment at the meeting and its adoption as so modified."
- **The Articles of Incorporation may be amended at any regular meeting of the Board by the
  affirmative vote of a majority of the Trustees**, on five days' notice.

This is the single most important contrast with the stock corporation. Under 8 Del. C. § 242 a
charter amendment requires board approval **and a stockholder vote**; the charter is the one
instrument the board cannot change alone, which is why so much of this course turns on what must be
in the charter rather than the bylaws — [[Modern charter and bylaws - what each instrument carries]].
Here the board amends both. The only asymmetry left is procedural: a bylaw amendment needs an
Executive Committee recommendation, while a charter amendment needs a majority of all trustees.

### Committees, and the Executive Committee in particular

Nine standing committees, of which the **Executive Committee** is the significant one: it exercises
"the authority of the Board of Trustees in the management of the University," except as precluded
by law, as the Bylaws require a majority vote of all Trustees, or as the Board otherwise resolves.
A committee of the whole board's power, in a board that meets three times a year. Compare the
delegation limits in 8 Del. C. § 141(c).

Note also that designating such a committee is one of the four supermajority matters, that each
committee must adopt a **charter** reviewed and approved by the Board, and that "[i]n the event of a
conflict between these Bylaws and any Charter, these Bylaws shall control."

### Officers and indemnification

- Officers are **elected annually by a majority of all Trustees** and serve "at the pleasure of the
  Board."
- Indemnification runs "to the fullest extent authorized by the **General Not For Profit Corporation
  Act of the State of Illinois**" — the analogue of 8 Del. C. § 145.
- **Advisory Councils** for divisions and schools, appointed by the Board on the President's
  recommendation, "charged with providing advice, advocacy, and philanthropy." Influence without
  governance rights, which is the pattern throughout: everyone other than the trustees gets a voice
  and no vote.

### The question the document poses

If there are no residual claimants, **for whose benefit do the trustees manage?** The corporate
answer — maximise value for the residual claimants, "not for the benefit of its contractual
claimants" ([[In re Trados]]) — has no referent. The answer the documents supply is the **purpose
clause**, enforced in practice by the Illinois Attorney General, donor restrictions, accreditors and
tax law rather than by any private plaintiff with standing. Worth having a view on before the exam,
because it is the natural closing question for a course that has spent thirteen weeks on who gets to
sue whom.
