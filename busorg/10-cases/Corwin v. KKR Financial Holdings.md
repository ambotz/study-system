## Rule

Where a transaction is **not** subject to entire fairness, approval by a fully
informed, uncoerced majority of the disinterested stockholders invokes the
business judgment rule in a post-closing damages action, and does so even if
Revlon would otherwise have applied.

## Facts

- KKR owned **less than 1%** of Financial Holdings' stock, had no right to appoint
  any directors, and had no contractual right to veto any board action. Those
  three absences defeat the controller allegation and therefore keep entire
  fairness out of the case.
- Financial Holdings had real assets controlled by its independent board and
  could pursue any path its directors chose. Control of the assets, not the
  contractual relationship, is what the controller inquiry turns on.
- Financial Holdings' primary business was financing KKR's leveraged buyout
  activity, and it had no employees running day-to-day operations — it was managed
  by KKR Financial Advisors, a KKR affiliate, under a management agreement
  terminable only on payment of a fee. *Argued and rejected — economic dependence
  plus an externally managed structure is not effective control of the board.*
- The stockholder vote was **fully informed**: all of the objective facts about
  the board's interests, KKR's interests, and the negotiation process were
  disclosed. This is the fact the holding turns on, and the one a plaintiff must
  attack to escape the rule.
- The vote was **uncoerced** and was cast by **disinterested** stockholders.
- Financial Holdings had an exculpatory charter provision and the merger was
  approved by an independent board majority. *Raised as an alternative ground; the
  court decides the case on the vote instead.*
- The suit sought **post-closing money damages**, not a pre-closing injunction.
  The remedy sought is what makes the cleansing doctrine available at all.
- Financial Holdings was an **LLC**, not a corporation, and the transaction was a
  stock-for-stock merger with KKR. *Background — the court applies corporate
  controller doctrine without remarking on the entity form.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The complaint did not plead facts supporting an inference that KKR was a
  controlling stockholder. Where a party lacks majority voting control, Delaware
  courts look for "a combination of potent voting power and management control
  such that the stockholder could be deemed to have effective control of the board
  without actually owning a majority of stock," and that combination was absent.
- Because entire fairness therefore did not apply, the effect of the uncoerced,
  informed stockholder vote is **outcome-determinative**, and the court expressly
  declines to decide whether Revlon governed the merger: "it does not matter."
- When a transaction not subject to entire fairness is approved by a fully
  informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders, the business
  judgment rule applies, and the complaint is properly dismissed.
- Unocal and Revlon are "primarily designed to give stockholders and the Court of
  Chancery the tool of **injunctive relief** to address important M&A decisions in
  real time, **before closing**." They were not designed for post-closing damages
  claims, their standards do not match Van Gorkom's gross negligence test for due
  care liability, and with exculpatory charter provisions prevalent, due care
  liability is rarely even available.
- The doctrine applies **only** to fully informed, uncoerced votes. If troubling
  facts about director behaviour were not disclosed and would have been material
  to a voting stockholder, the business judgment rule is not invoked.
- The policy rationale is that where the disinterested equity owners can protect
  themselves at the ballot box by voting no, a litigation-intrusive standard
  promises more cost in litigation rents and inhibited risk-taking than benefit,
  and judges are poorly positioned to second-guess impartial decision-makers who
  have better information or an actual economic stake.
- Affirmed on the basis of the Court of Chancery's reasoning.

**Rejected**

- *KKR was a controlling stockholder because Financial Holdings existed to finance
  KKR's deals and was managed by a KKR affiliate under a fee-protected management
  agreement.* Economic dependence and external management do not establish
  effective control where the stockholder holds under 1% of the equity, appoints
  no directors, holds no veto, and the independent board controls real assets and
  can choose any path.
- *Even without a controller, plaintiffs pleaded a Revlon claim that should have
  survived.* The argument was not fairly presented below, and in any event it
  fails on the merits because the fully informed vote restores business judgment
  review whether or not Revlon applied.
- *Cleansing by stockholder vote will impair the operation of Unocal and Revlon
  and expose stockholders to unfair director conduct.* Those standards remain
  fully available for their intended purpose, real-time injunctive relief before
  closing, and the cleansing doctrine is unavailable wherever the vote was
  uninformed or coerced.

## Context

Corwin is a short opinion doing two separable pieces of work, and keeping them
apart is the whole discipline of using the case. Part I is a **controller
definition** case: absent majority voting power, a stockholder is a controller
only on a combination of potent voting power and management control amounting to
effective control of the board, and neither an externally managed structure nor
business dependence supplies it. Part II is a **cleansing** case: given no
controller, an informed and uncoerced disinterested vote restores business
judgment review post-closing.

The sequencing point is the one to carry into any exam answer. Corwin operates
only where entire fairness does not, so the first question on any fact pattern is
whether a controller stands on the other side of the deal. If one does, the route
back to business judgment is [[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide|MFW]], assigned the same day,
and now § 144(b) and (c). If none does, Corwin is available. Getting these in the
wrong order produces an answer that applies a cleansing doctrine to a transaction
it cannot reach.

The doctrine is narrower than its reputation, and the limits are all in the
opinion. Corwin reaches **post-closing damages only**, so it leaves pre-closing
injunctive relief untouched — which is exactly the remedy Revlon and Unocal were
built for, as [[Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes]] and
[[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] both illustrate. It requires a **fully informed**
vote, which makes disclosure the entire battleground and which is why the
materiality standard from [[In re Trulia]], assigned the day before, does so much
work here. It requires an **uncoerced** vote, which is where Omnicare's fait
accompli analysis reappears: a vote whose outcome was predetermined cleanses
nothing. And it leaves untouched claims of waste and of bad faith, since a fully
informed vote cannot ratify what no rational person would approve.

The relationship to [[Charter exculpation of director liability|§ 102(b)(7)]] is
worth stating explicitly because the opinion assumes it. Corwin notes that with
exculpatory provisions prevalent, due care liability is rarely available anyway.
The two doctrines therefore stack: exculpation removes the care claim and Corwin
removes what remains of the enhanced-scrutiny claim, which is why post-closing
merger damages suits against non-controller boards so rarely survive.

The statutory sequel is [[Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)]].
Post-SB 21, § 144(b)(2) gives a controller transaction a statutory safe harbour on
an informed, uncoerced vote of a majority of the votes cast by disinterested
stockholders — cleansing by vote, promoted from common law to statute and extended
into the controller context that Corwin itself could never reach.
[[In re Investors Bancorp]] already records the same idea at work in the
ratification setting.

Two neighbouring opinions frame the excerpt. Lyondell Chemical v. Ryan, excerpted
immediately before, holds that the question under Revlon is not whether
disinterested directors did everything they arguably should have to get the best
price but whether they "utterly failed to attempt to obtain the best sale price" —
the bad faith framing that survives exculpation. In re GGP, excerpted immediately
after, opens on the rhythm that Corwin created: plaintiffs pleading blockholders
who act like controllers and votes that were coerced or uninformed, defendants
pleading passive blockholders and fully informed stockholders. That is the
litigation Corwin generates, and it is where the doctrine actually lives.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from the Court of Chancery's dismissal of the complaint on the pleadings.
- The action sought post-closing money damages; the merger had already closed, so
  no injunction was available and none was sought.
- The posture is load-bearing twice over. The controller question is decided on
  whether the complaint pleaded facts supporting an inference of control, and the
  cleansing holding is expressly confined to post-closing damages actions.

### Facts

KKR Financial Holdings LLC was a publicly traded vehicle whose primary business
was financing KKR's leveraged buyout activity. It had no employees managing its
day-to-day operations. Instead it was managed by KKR Financial Advisors, an
affiliate of KKR, under a contractual management agreement that Financial Holdings
could terminate only by paying a termination fee. KKR itself held less than 1% of
Financial Holdings' equity, had no right to appoint directors, and had no
contractual veto over board action.

In 2013 KKR and Financial Holdings agreed to a stock-for-stock merger. Financial
Holdings' stockholders approved it. Stockholder plaintiffs then sued for damages,
arguing principally that the structural relationship made KKR a controlling
stockholder, which would have made entire fairness the presumptive standard and
kept the case alive through discovery.

The Chancellor rejected that premise. He emphasised that Financial Holdings had
real assets that its independent board controlled and that the board had the
option of pursuing any path its directors chose, and he applied the settled test
for control short of majority ownership: a combination of potent voting power and
management control amounting to effective control of the board. On appeal the
Supreme Court says it sees no reason to repeat his "lucid analysis" of that
question.

The plaintiffs' fallback on appeal was that if entire fairness did not apply,
Revlon did, and that they had pleaded a Revlon claim. The defendants answered that
Financial Holdings had an exculpatory charter provision, that an independent board
majority approved the merger, and that a fully informed and uncoerced stockholder
vote had approved it, so no non-exculpated claim was stated. The court takes the
third of those and disposes of the appeal with it.

### Issue

- Whether the complaint pleaded facts supporting an inference that KKR was a
  controlling stockholder of Financial Holdings, which would make entire fairness
  the presumptive standard of review.
- Whether a fully informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders
  invokes the business judgment rule in a post-closing damages action where entire
  fairness does not apply.
- Whether that result holds even if Revlon would otherwise have governed the
  merger.

### Holding

- **On control: not pleaded.** KKR held under 1% of the stock, appointed no
  directors and held no veto, and an independent board controlled real assets, so
  the combination of potent voting power and management control that defines
  effective control was absent.
- **On cleansing: the vote invokes business judgment review.** With entire fairness
  out of the case, an informed and uncoerced disinterested stockholder vote makes
  the business judgment rule the standard for a post-closing damages claim, and
  the complaint was properly dismissed.
- **On Revlon: it does not matter.** Because the vote is outcome-determinative, the
  court declines to decide whether Revlon applied to the merger at all.
- **Disposition:** affirmed on the basis of the Court of Chancery's opinion.

### Rule

- A stockholder without majority voting power is a controller only where there is
  a combination of potent voting power and management control such that the
  stockholder effectively controls the board.
- Delaware has long been reluctant to second-guess the judgment of a disinterested
  stockholder majority that a transaction with a party **other than a controlling
  stockholder** is in their best interests.
- When a transaction not subject to entire fairness is approved by a fully
  informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders, the business
  judgment rule is the standard of review.
- The doctrine applies only to fully informed and uncoerced votes: a material
  undisclosed fact about director behaviour defeats it.
- Unocal and Revlon are tools of real-time injunctive relief before closing, not
  standards designed for post-closing damages claims.

### Reasoning

*(Strine, C.J.)*

- On control, the court refuses to convert a commercial and managerial
  relationship into board control. What matters is whether the independent board
  could choose its own path with the company's own assets, and it could.
- The cleansing holding is presented as an application of long-standing policy
  rather than as an innovation, and the court repeatedly locates the rationale in
  who is best positioned to decide: judges are poorly placed to evaluate business
  decisions, while informed disinterested stockholders have an actual economic
  stake in the outcome.
- The court separates the **function** of a standard from its **label**. Unocal and
  Revlon exist to let Chancery intervene before a deal closes; after closing, the
  question is whether anyone with authority to decide was misled or compelled, and
  if not, the standards designed for injunctions have no remaining work.
- The mismatch argument reinforces the point: Revlon's reasonableness inquiry does
  not line up with Van Gorkom's gross negligence liability standard, and
  exculpatory charters have made due care damages rare in any event, so applying
  Revlon post-closing produces litigation without a corresponding remedy.
- The court supplies its own limiting principle rather than leaving it to later
  cases, stating twice that everything turns on the vote being fully informed and
  uncoerced, and noting that here all objective facts about the board's interests,
  KKR's interests and the negotiation were disclosed.
- The cost-benefit framing is explicit and unusual for a standard-of-review
  holding: litigation rents and inhibitions on risk-taking are weighed directly
  against the protective value of judicial review.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

One tension the opinion does not resolve is the gap between the stockholders'
decision and the claim being extinguished. A stockholder voting on a merger is
deciding whether to accept the consideration offered, not adjudicating whether the
board ran a reasonable sale process — and a rational holder may well vote yes on a
price she considers acceptable while believing a better one was available. Corwin
treats the first decision as an answer to the second. The court's response is
embedded in the informed-vote condition: if the process defects were disclosed,
the holder had the information and chose anyway, and if they were not, there is no
cleansing.

### Cold-call notes

- **Say the sequence out loud before analysing anything.** Controller on both
  sides? If yes, entire fairness, and MFW or § 144 is the only way back. If no,
  ask whether the vote was fully informed and uncoerced, and if it was, business
  judgment review applies and the analysis ends there.
- **Know the four limits cold.** Post-closing damages only; fully informed;
  uncoerced; and no application where entire fairness governs. Waste claims also
  survive, since a vote cannot ratify what no rational person would approve.
- **Be ready for "so is Revlon dead?"** The answer is that Revlon is alive as an
  injunction standard and largely dead as a damages theory against a
  non-controller board whose stockholders were properly informed. Quote the "real
  time, before closing" line, which is the sentence doing the work.
- **Expect to be pushed on the controller test.** The strongest plaintiff argument
  here is that Financial Holdings was an empty vehicle existing to serve KKR and
  managed by KKR's own affiliate. The court's answer is ownership and board
  composition: under 1%, no appointment right, no veto, real assets, independent
  board. The honest observation is that the test asks about formal levers and is
  therefore hard to satisfy through economic dependence alone.
- **Do not overstate the holding as ratification.** The vote does not approve the
  breach; the vote changes the **standard of review**. A plaintiff can still win
  under business judgment review by pleading waste or bad faith, which is
  difficult but not conceptually foreclosed.
- **Connect to Trulia the day before.** Because everything turns on whether the
  vote was fully informed, disclosure litigation is where Corwin cases are won and
  lost, and Trulia's materiality standard supplies the test.
- **Have the § 144(b)(2) comparison ready.** SB 21 turned cleansing by vote into a
  statutory safe harbour and extended it to controller transactions, which is the
  one place Corwin itself could never operate.
