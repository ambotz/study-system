## Rule

A stockholder investigating wrongdoing under § 220 need show only a **credible
basis** from which the court can infer possible mismanagement warranting further
investigation; corporate records are defined by content rather than by medium or
custodian, so email and personal-account documents are reachable; and the court may
condition production on the entire production being **incorporated by reference**
in any derivative complaint the stockholder later files.

## Facts

- Mayer withheld de Castro's **name, position and qualifications** from the
  Compensation Committee while seeking approval for a package the Committee's own
  consultant, George Paulin, called "generally more than the data supported for a
  number two executive in peer companies." The Committee authorised negotiations
  three separate times before learning who the candidate was.
- At the October 13 meeting Mayer **described the previously approved terms
  inaccurately**, telling the Committee that concessions de Castro was seeking were
  already in the agreement, and the Committee approved a change on that basis.
- After that meeting Mayer prepared the Final Offer Letter with **further changes
  she never presented to the Committee** that materially increased de Castro's
  compensation. Unilateral enlargement of a package outside the approval process is
  the fact that supports both the fiduciary duty theory and the waste theory.
- The Committee "did not receive any materials that illustrated" how the
  interlocking vesting provisions, the six-month tail and the Specified Percentage
  would pay out under different departure scenarios — at any of the meetings. The
  compensation consequences "were not self-evident," so the absence of the
  modelling is what makes the approval uninformed.
- The Committee approved the without-cause termination **by written consent
  without meeting**, on a resolution whose only recitation was that management had
  "discussed with the Committee the termination without cause." There is no
  evidence the Committee evaluated a for-cause alternative or saw a calculation of
  the severance.
- The Committee did not discuss the **reasons** for the termination until February
  27, six weeks after approving it.
- A for-cause termination was potentially available: advertising revenue declined
  in every quarter after de Castro started, Mayer took his advertising team away
  from him within a year, and she later told the Committee he "still failed to
  correct or improve his performance." The without-cause characterisation cost
  $59.96 million.
- Yahoo's production was limited to **board-level materials** and excluded
  documents Mayer reviewed that never went to the Board or a committee. The gap
  between what the fiduciary saw and what the board saw is the whole dispute about
  scope.
- Amalgamated's demand attached account statements dated **three days before** the
  demand. *Argued and rejected — Section 220 requires documentation sufficiently
  proximate in time, not a same-day record.*
- Yahoo has a § 102(b)(7) exculpatory provision. *Argued and rejected — exculpation
  does not foreclose an otherwise proper purpose.*
- Holders of 71% of outstanding shares, and 92% of shares present, approved Yahoo's
  executive compensation on the advisory **say-on-pay** vote. *Background — an
  advisory vote is not raised as a bar and does not appear in the analysis.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Form and manner: satisfied.** "Delaware courts require strict adherence to the
  section 220 inspection demand procedural requirements," and Amalgamated met them
  by stating the Funds' ownership under oath, attaching documentary evidence, and
  averring the evidence was what it purported to be.
- Account statements dated three days before the demand suffice. Section 220 "must
  be applied with some appreciation of the practical considerations surrounding its
  use," and requiring same-day documentation "would be an unreasonable reading of
  the statute." What is required is documentation "sufficiently proximate in time
  to the date of the demand as to be consistent with and corroborate the averment
  of stock ownership."
- Using Section 220 carries an **implicit obligation** to advise the company if the
  demanding party loses stockholder status or its authority to act, and a court
  might impose a remedy on a party that continued under false pretences.
- **Proper purpose: established on both theories.** Investigating wrongdoing or
  mismanagement is a proper purpose, and the stockholder "need only show... a
  credible basis from which the Court of Chancery can infer there is possible
  mismanagement that would warrant further investigation." That standard "sets the
  lowest possible burden of proof" and may rest on documents, logic, testimony,
  circumstantial evidence, or sufficiently reliable hearsay.
- There is a credible basis to suspect **breach of fiduciary duty** in both the
  hiring and the firing. On hiring, Mayer withheld the candidate's identity,
  described the approved terms inaccurately, and then enlarged the package
  unilaterally; the directors' involvement "appears to have been tangential and
  episodic," and they "seem to have accepted Mayer's statements uncritically."
- "A board cannot mindlessly swallow information, particularly in the area of
  executive compensation," which "is not one of those instances" where deference to
  officers' judgments is appropriate. "Directors who choose not to ask questions
  take the risk that they may have to provide explanations later, or at least
  produce explanatory books and records as part of a Section 220 investigation."
- On firing, the issue is "why Yahoo's fiduciaries agreed to a without-cause
  termination when a for-cause alternative was potentially available." The
  Committee "rubberstamped what Mayer had done through a quick email exchange of
  written consents," which "suggests ostrich-like conduct warranting further
  investigation."
- There is also a credible basis to suspect **waste** — a transaction "on terms
  that no person of ordinary, sound business judgment could conclude represent a
  fair exchange" — because Mayer increased the compensation without approval and
  because a for-cause termination could have avoided severance entirely.
- **Investigating director disinterestedness and independence** is an independently
  proper purpose, and may extend to the nomination process, management's influence
  over it, procedural safeguards, and minutes showing patterns in how directors
  handled management proposals.
- **Exculpation does not cut off the purpose.** Stockholders may use the
  information to seek an audience with the board, prepare a resolution, or mount a
  proxy fight, not only to sue. The court expresses doubt that Amalgamated will
  prevail against the outside directors, but "the question now... is not whether
  Amalgamated will prevail," and a potential claim against Mayer **as an officer**
  is untouched, since § 102(b)(7) "does not authorize exculpation for officers."
- **Electronic records are books and records.** Section 224 recognises records kept
  in "any information storage device," over 90% of business documents are stored
  electronically, and confining inspection to paper "could cause Section 220 to
  become obsolete or ineffective."
- Records in a personal email account are still corporate records: "rights of
  shareholders secured by § 220 cannot be defeated simply by having another entity
  hold the records." What matters "is whether the record is essential and
  sufficient to satisfy the stockholder's proper purpose, **not its source**."
- **The Incorporation Condition, as a matter of first impression, is imposed.**
  Under § 220(c) the court "may, in its discretion, prescribe any limitations or
  conditions with reference to the inspection," and conditioning production on the
  whole production being incorporated by reference in any derivative complaint
  ensures a complaint "will not be based on cherry-picked documents."
- The condition **does not change the pleading standard**. Well-pleaded factual
  allegations are still accepted as true under Rule 12(b)(6), particularised
  allegations under Rule 23.1, and the plaintiff still receives all reasonable
  inferences, including where a document supports more than one. The only effect is
  to stop a plaintiff from taking a document out of context and insisting on an
  inference the court could not draw if it saw the related documents.
- Tailored production ordered within thirty days, subject to the condition.

**Rejected**

- *The demand failed because the ownership evidence was dated three days earlier
  rather than the day of the demand.* Obtaining documentation takes a non-trivial
  amount of time, statements issue periodically, and no stockholder could comply
  without a same-day record. Recent brokerage statements are adequate.
- *A stockholder whose only realistic claim is exculpated has no proper purpose.*
  That argument has force where the stockholder has limited itself to a derivative
  suit and identified no non-exculpated conduct. Amalgamated limited itself to
  nothing, and the potential officer claim against Mayer lies outside § 102(b)(7)
  in any event.
- *Section 220 says nothing about electronically stored information, so inspection
  is limited to paper records.* The statute's silence does not confine it to paper.
  Section 224 already contemplates electronic storage, Delaware precedent has
  ordered production of emails in § 220 actions, and a contrary rule would let
  custody or medium defeat the right.
- *Rule 11 already prevents cherry-picking, so the Incorporation Condition is
  unnecessary.* Rule 11 mitigates the problem but "invoking Rule 11 is strong
  medicine." The more common case is counsel drawing an unreasonable inference from
  a document in isolation, "not in bad faith but perhaps over-zealously," and the
  condition handles that "in more measured fashion."

## Context

The case is assigned between the two versions of the statute, and the comparison is
the point of the session. Yahoo! states the pre-SB 21 law at its most
stockholder-friendly, and **SB 21 adopted half of it and reversed the other half**.

The half Delaware kept: the Incorporation Condition is now
**8 Del. C. § 220(b)(3)**, and it belongs to the corporation as of right rather
than to the court as a matter of discretion, alongside a matching right to redact
portions "not specifically related to the stockholder's purpose."

The half Delaware reversed: the personal-email holding. Post-SB 21, "books and
records" is the closed nine-category list in § 220(a)(1), emails and texts are not
on it, and § 220(e) forbids the court from ordering anything else except through
§ 220(f) functional equivalents or § 220(g), which demands a **compelling need**
proved by **clear and convincing evidence**. Run the same demand today and the
board minutes and committee materials come out under (a)(1)e. and f.; the Mayer
Documents become a § 220(g) fight. The full comparison is in
[[Books and records inspection - 220 before and after SB 21]].

Laster's analogy to Disney frames the whole opinion — "history may not repeat
itself, but it often rhymes." Disney also involved a chief executive hiring a
number two at munificent compensation, poor performance, and a no-fault
termination after about a year conferring dynastic wealth where cause might have
been available. Disney is also the procedural moral: the first complaint was
dismissed for failure to plead demand futility, the Supreme Court called it "a very
troubling case on the merits" and pointed the plaintiffs to the **"tools at hand"**,
they took the hint, used § 220, and the amended complaint survived. Then the
defendants won at trial anyway — which is why the credible basis standard is not a
prediction about the merits.

That sequence is why this session sits where it does in the course. Section 220 is
the front door to [[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test]]: the
particularised facts a Rule 23.1 complaint needs generally do not exist without an
inspection first. The Incorporation Condition then reaches forward into that same
pleading stage, and the fact that Laster is careful to say it changes nothing about
Rule 12(b)(6) or Rule 23.1 is the reason the condition was tolerable at all.

The compensation material connects backward. [[In re Investors Bancorp]] is the
director self-compensation case, and the line that "a board cannot mindlessly
swallow information, particularly in the area of executive compensation" is the
same instinct that drives the ratification limits there. The uninformed-approval
findings are [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] in a compensation committee: no modelling, no
questions, and approval of terms nobody had worked through.

One feature of the record the opinion does not lean on is the **say-on-pay vote**,
which Yahoo won with 71% of outstanding shares. Yahoo did not argue it as a bar and
Laster does not mention it in the analysis. Worth holding as a contrast with
[[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]] — a Corwin vote changes the standard of review
for a transaction, while an advisory compensation vote does neither that nor
anything to a stockholder's independent statutory inspection right.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Post-trial decision in a summary proceeding under 8 Del. C. § 220(c), tried in
  the Court of Chancery before Laster, V.C.
- Amalgamated sued only after Yahoo produced 677 pages of board-level materials and
  then refused eleven further categories.
- The posture matters twice. This is a books-and-records action, so nothing is
  adjudicated on the merits and the credible basis finding decides nothing about
  liability. And it is a **trial**, not a pleading-stage ruling, which is why the
  opinion can make findings about what the Committee did and did not receive.

### Facts

Marissa Mayer became Yahoo's chief executive in July 2012. Henrique de Castro, then
president of worldwide business partnerships at Google, soon expressed interest in
joining her. Mayer told the Compensation and Leadership Development Committee that
she was negotiating with someone for the number two role but withheld his name,
title and employer on confidentiality grounds, while describing an expected package
of "$15 million per year (with $40 million as part of that up front in a four-year
grant) and a $16 million or more make-whole payment." The Committee's consultant,
George Paulin of Frederic W. Cook & Co., said the proposal was generally more than
the data supported for a number two at peer companies but that the Committee "could
justify this compensation" regardless — advice given while still not knowing who
the candidate was.

The Committee met again on September 23 with a term sheet, still without a name,
and authorised further negotiation. On September 24 it met for thirty minutes,
finally learned the candidate was de Castro, and approved an Original Offer Letter
carrying a $600,000 base salary, a bonus targeted at 90% of base, a $1 million
signing bonus, and three types of equity awards with a total target value of $56
million. The letter's without-cause provisions accelerated vesting through a
six-month tail cut back by a Specified Percentage, with different mechanics for
each award type. Nobody gave the directors any modelling of what those provisions
paid out in any given scenario. The Committee delegated non-material changes to its
chair, Maynard Webb, reserving material changes to itself.

The full Board then met and received the same letter and nothing else. Mayer
described de Castro's background, his Google role, his visa position, his
reputation for ethics and integrity, and his insight into display advertising.
Counsel summarised the economics in figures that did not match the letter, and Webb
reported the Committee's approval and his own delegated authority. The Board
approved resolutions authorising the letter "substantially in the form attached."

De Castro wanted more: acceleration on a twelve-month tail with no Specified
Percentage cutback. At the October 13 Committee meeting Mayer described what the
Committee had previously approved and described it incorrectly, suggesting that
some of what de Castro was asking for was already in the deal. She said her concern
about the larger termination payout "was less in the termination without cause
scenario since that will be under her and the Board's control," and nobody examined
the definition of cause in light of that remark. The Committee approved removing
the Specified Percentage. Afterwards Mayer prepared the Final Offer Letter with
additional changes that materially increased the package and that the Committee
never saw.

De Castro signed on October 15. Public reaction was poor: the Wall Street Journal
called the package "staggering," and Business Insider ran "Did Marissa Mayer Just
Make a Horrible Mistake?", quoting former colleagues, one of whom said "Google
should pay Yahoo to take him." His first-year pay of $39.2 million made him the
eighth-highest-paid executive in Silicon Valley, ahead of Mayer, and higher than
all but eight public-company chief executives in the country.

He did not perform. Advertising revenue declined every quarter after he arrived, he
did not get along with management, and within a year Mayer took his advertising
team. In January 2014, fourteen months in, she decided to fire him. The Committee
approved a without-cause termination by written consent dated January 12 without
meeting, on a resolution reciting only that management had "discussed with the
Committee the termination without cause." It first discussed the reasons on
February 27. Yahoo's April 16 proxy finally disclosed that the without-cause
characterisation had triggered $59.96 million in severance.

Amalgamated had served its demand on February 24, seeking to investigate excessive
compensation, director independence, and the circumstances of the departure. Yahoo
rejected it on four grounds, then produced 677 pages of board-level materials in
September after a confidentiality agreement — a production that pointedly excluded
documents Mayer had reviewed that never reached the Board or a committee. Yahoo
refused eleven further categories in October, and Amalgamated sued in March 2015.

### Issue

- Whether documentary evidence of beneficial ownership dated three days before the
  demand satisfies § 220's form and manner requirements.
- Whether Amalgamated established a credible basis to infer possible
  mismanagement in the hiring and firing of de Castro, and whether Yahoo's
  exculpatory charter provision defeats that purpose.
- Whether email and other electronically stored documents, including material in a
  chief executive's personal account, are books and records subject to inspection.
- Whether the court may condition production on the stockholder agreeing that the
  entire production is incorporated by reference in any derivative complaint.

### Holding

- **On form and manner: satisfied.** Documentation must be sufficiently proximate
  in time to corroborate the averment of ownership, and recent brokerage statements
  do that.
- **On proper purpose: established, on three grounds.** Credible bases exist to
  suspect breach of fiduciary duty in the hiring, breach in the firing, and waste,
  and investigating director disinterestedness and independence is independently
  proper. Exculpation does not foreclose any of them.
- **On scope: electronic records are included.** Books and records are defined by
  content, not by medium or custodian, so responsive material in Mayer's personal
  account must be produced.
- **On conditions: the Incorporation Condition is imposed.** Production is
  conditioned on the entire production being deemed incorporated by reference in
  any derivative complaint on the subject matter of the demand, which changes no
  pleading standard.
- **Disposition:** tailored production ordered within thirty days, subject to the
  condition.

### Rule

- A § 220(b) plaintiff must establish by a preponderance that it is a stockholder,
  complied with the form and manner requirements, and has a proper purpose, and
  then that each category of records is essential to that purpose.
- Strict adherence to the procedural requirements is required, so that the
  corporation may receive and consider a demand in proper form before litigation.
- A desire to investigate wrongdoing or mismanagement is a proper purpose, shown by
  a credible basis from which possible mismanagement warranting further
  investigation may be inferred — the lowest possible burden of proof.
- The stockholder need not prove that waste or mismanagement is actually occurring,
  and a sufficient showing may fall well short of demonstrating that anything wrong
  occurred.
- Investigating director disinterestedness and independence is also a proper
  purpose.
- The scope order permits access to records essential to the purpose and stops at
  what is sufficient, circumscribed with rifled precision.
- Books and records include records kept in any information storage device, and a
  corporate record keeps its character regardless of medium or custodian.
- Section 220(c) gives the court broad discretion to prescribe any limitations or
  conditions on the inspection.

### Reasoning

*(Laster, V.C.)*

- The form-and-manner holding is an exercise in reading a strict rule sensibly.
  Strict adherence is the doctrine; the court supplies the content of the
  requirement — proximity sufficient to corroborate — rather than inventing an
  exception to it, and adds a reciprocal duty to disclose lost standing so the
  leniency is not one-sided.
- The credible basis analysis is deliberately framed by the Disney bookends. Laster
  compares the Yahoo record to the pre-inspection Disney complaint that was
  dismissed and the post-inspection Disney complaint that survived, and places
  Yahoo between them, which is how he can find enough for inspection while
  disclaiming any view on the merits.
- The hiring findings turn on **information the directors never received** rather
  than on the size of the package. The interlocking vesting provisions were not
  self-evident, nobody modelled them, and a Committee that approved terms it had
  not worked through is a Committee whose approval is worth investigating.
- The firing findings turn on the **choice not made**. The court never says a
  for-cause termination was available, only that nobody asked, and that a committee
  approving a $59.96 million consequence by email consent without evaluating the
  alternative is ostrich-like.
- On exculpation, the court separates the **use** of the information from the
  **claim**. Because information supports proxy fights, resolutions and board
  engagement as well as litigation, an exculpatory charter cannot foreclose the
  purpose; and because § 102(b)(7) reaches directors only, the officer claim
  against Mayer survives regardless.
- On electronic records, the reasoning is functional and anti-formalist: § 224
  already contemplates electronic storage, over 90% of business documents are
  electronic, and a rule keyed to medium or custodian would let a corporation
  defeat the statute by choosing where to keep its files.
- The Incorporation Condition is built out of the existing incorporation-by-
  reference doctrine rather than invented. That doctrine already stops a plaintiff
  from quoting a document and preventing the court from reading it; the condition
  extends the same logic to the rest of the production, and the opinion's long
  disclaimer about Rules 12(b)(6) and 23.1 is what keeps the extension from
  altering the pleading balance.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The condition does carry a real cost the opinion acknowledges only obliquely.
Laster's closing instruction is that Amalgamated should plead "as if the
Incorporation Condition had not been imposed" and that defendants must supply only
the limited documents necessary to defeat a particular inference. That instruction
is doing a great deal of work: the difference between a targeted rebuttal and a
motion to dismiss argued across an entire production is judicial discipline rather
than anything in the condition itself. Section 220(b)(3) has since made the
condition a statutory entitlement of the corporation, without carrying that
instruction along with it.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the three things the case is famous for.** Credible basis as the
  lowest possible burden; books and records defined by content rather than medium
  or custodian; and the Incorporation Condition as a matter of first impression.
  Anything else is detail.
- **Be ready for "so has Amalgamated shown wrongdoing?"** No, and the opinion says
  so repeatedly. A credible basis to suspect possible wrongdoing warranting further
  investigation "does not mean that wrongdoing actually occurred" — and even in
  Disney, where the complaint survived, the defendants won at trial.
- **Know what SB 21 did to this case.** It codified the Incorporation Condition at
  § 220(b)(3) as the corporation's right and added a redaction right; it moved
  emails outside the default by defining books and records as the closed (a)(1)
  list, so the Mayer Documents now run through § 220(g) at compelling need plus
  clear and convincing evidence. Say which half survived and which half did not.
- **Have the exculpation answer in two parts.** Information has non-litigation
  uses, so a § 102(b)(7) provision cannot foreclose the purpose; and § 102(b)(7)
  does not cover **officers**, so the potential claim against Mayer is outside it
  entirely. The second half is the sharper answer.
- **Expect to be pushed on the without-cause choice.** The court never holds cause
  was available. The credible basis is that nobody on the Committee asked, examined
  the definition of cause, or saw what the choice cost — and Mayer's own remark that
  a without-cause termination "will be under her and the Board's control" went
  unexamined.
- **Be precise about "rifled precision."** It is qualitative and contextual, not a
  quantitative cap. Wal-Mart, excerpted just before this case, makes exactly that
  point.
- **Connect forward to Rule 23.1.** Section 220 is the tools-at-hand front door to
  a demand futility complaint, and the Incorporation Condition reaches into that
  pleading stage while expressly leaving the standards alone.
- **Numbers worth holding.** $56 million target value of the equity awards; $39.2
  million first-year pay; fourteen months on the job; $59.96 million severance;
  677 pages produced; thirty-minute meeting at which the Committee learned the
  candidate's name.
