## Rule

A **managing** coadventurer who receives an opportunity that comes to him as an
incident of managing the venture must disclose it to his coadventurer. Taking it
"in secrecy and silence" extends a constructive trust over what he took, even
where the venture was about to end and even absent any intent to defraud.

## Facts

- Salmon leased the Hotel Bristol for twenty years and funded the conversion to
  shops and offices with Meinhard's money — Meinhard paying half the cost, taking
  40% of net profits for five years and 50% after, and bearing half of any losses.
- **Salmon alone held the sole power to "manage, lease, underlet and operate."**
  *This asymmetry is the case. Meinhard put in money; Salmon put in money, labour,
  and control.*
- The lease ran in Salmon's own name. **To an observer Salmon held it as owner in
  his own right**, which is why Gerry approached Salmon alone. *Had the lease run
  in the venture's name, Gerry would have laid the plan before both.*
- Gerry, owner of the reversion and of five adjoining lots, had tried and failed to
  place a large redevelopment with several capitalists and dealers. He then turned
  to the man in possession of the keystone parcel.
- With **less than four months left** on the Bristol lease, Salmon's wholly owned
  Midpoint Realty took a new lease over the whole tract — up to eighty years by
  renewal, a $3,000,000 building, rent rising from $55,000 to between $350,000 and
  $475,000.
- **Salmon told Meinhard nothing.** Meinhard learned of the lease in February, after
  it was signed, and did not know a project even existed.
- The new lease covered an **extension and enlargement of the same subject-matter**
  — the Bristol site was the keystone of the assembled tract. *A proposal for a
  building far removed would have been Salmon's to keep.*
- Salmon had made the venture a success and Meinhard had contributed money but
  neither time nor labour. *Argued and rejected — the court concedes the equities
  and holds them irrelevant to a managing fiduciary.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Joint adventurers, like copartners, owe one another "the duty of the finest
  loyalty" while the enterprise continues. The standard is "not honesty alone, but
  the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive."
- The heavier weight of that duty rested on Salmon **because he was manager**. The
  opportunity reached him "by virtue of his agency."
- The breach is the exclusion, not the taking. Salmon "might have warned Meinhard
  that the plan had been submitted, and that either would be free to compete." What
  he could not do was cut off the chance to compete. "This chance, if nothing more,
  he was under a duty to concede."
- No finding of conscious fraud is required. The court expressly assumes Salmon
  acted in good faith and holds him liable anyway.
- Remedy: a constructive trust over half the new lease — at Salmon's option
  attaching instead to the Midpoint shares, with one extra share to Salmon so he
  keeps control, and with Meinhard assuming the corresponding obligations.

**Rejected**

- *The chance would have been worth little to Meinhard even if offered.* "Such a
  calculus of probabilities is beyond the science of the chancery." Meinhard might
  have offered better terms, allied with other capital, or persuaded Gerry to renew
  the Bristol lease alone.
- *The venture was four months from its end, so the duty had effectively expired.*
  If Salmon could take the renewal with four months to run, "he might do so with
  equal right while there remained as many years." The duty does not thin out as
  the term runs down.
- *Meinhard had already been richly paid and was grasping for more.* The court
  accepts the characterisation — "There might seem to be something grasping in his
  insistence upon more" — and holds that such recriminations "are not without their
  force if conduct is to be judged by the common standards of competitors," which
  is precisely the standard that does not apply.
- *The interest should be limited to the Bristol portion of the assembled tract.*
  A single building covers the whole area; physical and financial division are
  impracticable, and the lease "is single and entire."

## Context

The anchor for partnership and joint-venture loyalty, and the source of the
formulation the rest of the course argues with. Read it against
[[Detroit Lions v. Argovitz]], assigned the day before: Argovitz concerns an agent
**taking** something from the principal's transaction, while Meinhard concerns a
manager **withholding** an opportunity that arose from the management. Both
resolve on disclosure rather than on outcome.

Cardozo's own limiting principle is the part most often forgotten and the part most
useful on an exam. A "different question would be here if there were lacking any
**nexus of relation** between the business conducted by the manager and the
opportunity brought to him as an incident of management." A proposal to lease a
building "at a location far removed" would have been Salmon's to keep. The doctrine
therefore has a scope element, and the scope is set by the relationship between the
opportunity and the enterprise.

The dissent's position — that the parties' written contract defined a limited
object ending at a limited time — is the contractarian argument that governs the
modern analogues. When the course reaches [[Elf Atochem North America v. Jaffari]]
and the note on LLC default fiduciary duties, Andrews' view is substantially what
the LLC statute adopts by letting the operating agreement displace these duties.
Meinhard is the default; the later material is about contracting out of it.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Suit to impress a trust on the new lease as an asset of the joint venture.
- A referee gave judgment for Meinhard but limited his interest to 25%, on the
  theory that his equity reached only half the value attributable to the Bristol
  site.
- The Appellate Division enlarged the interest to half the whole lease.
- Defendants appealed. The Court of Appeals affirmed as modified, adding the
  share-substitution option and the extra share preserving Salmon's control.

### Facts

In 1902 Louisa Gerry leased the Hotel Bristol at Forty-Second and Fifth to Walter
Salmon for twenty years. Salmon undertook to convert the hotel to shops and offices
at a cost of $200,000, with the alterations accreting to the land. While
negotiating the lease he was simultaneously negotiating with Meinhard for the money.

Their written agreement made Meinhard responsible for half the funds to
reconstruct, alter, manage and operate, gave him 40% of net profits for five years
and 50% thereafter, split losses equally, and reserved sole management to Salmon.
The early years lost money — "for each the venture had its phases of fair weather
and of foul" — and later the profits became large.

By 1921 Elbridge Gerry owned the reversion plus one adjoining Fifth Avenue lot and
four on Forty-Second Street, and wanted the whole tract redeveloped under a single
long lease. He shopped the project to capitalists and dealers without success. In
January 1922 he went to Salmon.

The resulting lease to Salmon's Midpoint Realty ran twenty years with renewals to
eighty, allowed the existing buildings to stand seven years before demolition, and
required a $3,000,000 replacement. Salmon personally guaranteed performance until
the new building was complete and paid for. It was signed January 25, 1922.
Meinhard heard about it in February.

### Issue

- Whether a managing coadventurer who takes for himself, without notice to his
  coadventurer, a lease that extends and enlarges the subject-matter of the venture
  breaches the duty of loyalty, where the venture is within months of expiring and
  no fraud is found.
- What form and extent of equitable interest the excluded coadventurer receives.

### Holding

- **On the breach: yes.** The opportunity came to Salmon as an incident of his
  management, and he was bound at minimum to disclose it so that Meinhard could
  compete. Excluding him from the chance to compete is itself the wrong.
- **On the remedy: half the whole lease.** Not half of a notional Bristol-only
  fragment, because the tract and the building are single and entire, and the
  burdens cannot be divided either.
- **On the mechanics:** at Salmon's option the trust attaches to the Midpoint shares
  rather than the lease, with one additional share to Salmon so the management
  control contemplated by the venture survives.

### Rule

- Joint adventurers, like copartners, owe one another the duty of the finest
  loyalty for the duration of the enterprise.
- The standard is "not honesty alone, but the punctilio of an honor the most
  sensitive," and courts will not erode it by particular exceptions.
- A **managing** coadventurer bears a heavier weight of that duty than a passive one.
- An opportunity that comes to the manager as an incident of the management must be
  disclosed, so the opportunity to compete is equalised.
- Conscious intent to defraud is not required.
- The rule reaches only opportunities bearing a **nexus of relation** to the
  enterprise's business; an unrelated opportunity is the manager's to keep.
- Whether the excluded party would have won the opportunity is not inquired into.

### Reasoning

*(Cardozo, C.J.)*

- The duty is framed as a standard of conduct rather than a prohibition on
  outcomes — "Many forms of conduct permissible in a workaday world for those
  acting at arm's length, are forbidden to those bound by fiduciary ties."
- Causation is located in the appearance Salmon created. Because the lease ran in
  his own name, Gerry dealt with him alone; had the lease named a partnership,
  Gerry "would have laid before the partners... his plan of reconstruction."
- Control generates the disclosure obligation: "The very fact that Salmon was in
  control with exclusive powers of direction charged him the more obviously with
  the duty of disclosure, since only through disclosure could opportunity be
  equalized."
- The court refuses counterfactual valuation as a matter of institutional
  competence, not indifference — "beyond the science of the chancery."
- The remedy is calibrated to preserve the venture's original allocation of power,
  which is why Salmon gets the extra share.

### Dissent / concurrence

*(Andrews, J., with Kellogg and O'Brien, JJ.)*

- Concedes the result would likely be right in a **general partnership**, where
  trust, confidence and goodwill are at stake and a managing partner can rarely
  take a renewal even after dissolution.
- Holds a joint venture is governed by "less drastic principles," and that the
  written contract defines the parties' rights and duties.
- Reads that contract as creating a **limited object ending at a limited time** —
  the exploitation of one particular lease for twenty years. Meinhard's equity was
  in that lease alone and terminated when the venture did.
- Argues no expectancy of renewal formed part of the venture's scope, so nothing
  was appropriated.
- Offers the Albany-to-Schenectady hypothetical: two venturers resurfacing a
  highway rent a storage parcel; one privately arranges to lease that parcel and an
  adjoining one afterward for an iron foundry. Unfair? "The mere fact that the joint
  ventures rent property together does not call for the strict rule that applies to
  general partners."
- Would reverse and order a new trial.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect to be asked what Salmon should have done.** Tell Meinhard the project
  exists. That is the floor, and the majority says so explicitly — Cardozo
  expressly declines to decide whether Salmon would then have had to hold a won
  lease for the venture. Disclosure was required; sharing may not have been.
- **The nexus limit is the exam handle.** Same tract, extension and enlargement of
  the same subject-matter, so inside. A building far removed would be outside.
  Any hypothetical here will move the opportunity nearer or further from the
  venture's business and ask you to draw that line.
- **The trap** is arguing Salmon earned it. The court agrees he earned it — time,
  labour, made it a success — and says the argument would have force "if conduct is
  to be judged by the common standards of competitors." Quoting that concession
  before rejecting it is stronger than ignoring it.
- **Have the dissent's contract point ready.** Andrews is not arguing Salmon behaved
  well; he is arguing the parties wrote down a limited deal and the court rewrote
  it. That is the live argument, and it wins later in the LLC materials.
- **The phrase to have verbatim:** "Not honesty alone, but the punctilio of an honor
  the most sensitive, is then the standard of behavior."
- **Numbers:** twenty-year lease, $200,000 conversion, 40% then 50% of profits, four
  months remaining, eighty-year maximum term, $3,000,000 building, rent from
  $55,000 to $350,000–$475,000.
