## Rule

Deal protection devices adopted to lock up a merger are subject to **Unocal**
enhanced scrutiny even where the merger works no change of control, and a
combination of devices that makes a superior competing proposal mathematically
impossible is preclusive and coercive, and therefore invalid regardless of how
reasonable the board's process was.

## Facts

- NCS was insolvent and in default on roughly $350 million of debt, including
  $206 million of senior bank debt and $102 million of convertible subordinated
  Notes. Full creditor recovery was remote and stockholder recovery seemed
  impossible. That baseline is what made the Genesis deal look like the only
  value-creating option available.
- Genesis had previously lost a last-minute bidding war to Omnicare, and its
  bitterness over that loss drove its insistence on exclusivity and a
  "bulletproof" lock-up. The board was told as much: Genesis "wanted to have a
  pretty much bulletproof deal or they were not going to go forward."
- The merger agreement contained a § 251(c) force-the-vote provision requiring
  NCS to submit the agreement to stockholders **even if the board withdrew its
  recommendation**.
- At Genesis's insistence the merger agreement omitted any effective fiduciary
  out clause.
- Outcalt (chairman) and Shaw (chief executive), who between them held a majority
  of NCS's voting power, signed irrevocable voting agreements committing all
  their shares to the merger, granting Genesis an irrevocable proxy, and
  specifically enforceable by Genesis. NCS itself was required to be a party.
- Those three devices together guaranteed, **ab initio**, that the Genesis merger
  would be approved. This is the pivotal fact: each device alone is ordinary, and
  the combination is what produced the fait accompli.
- Outcalt and Shaw wore two hats. The same two people were half of the four-person
  board and the holders of majority voting power, so the board action and the
  stockholder action were not independent of one another.
- Genesis gave the board less than twenty-four hours to sign, on unalterable
  terms, or the offer would be withdrawn.
- Omnicare's competing proposal offered NCS stockholders cash worth **more than
  twice** the market value of the Genesis stock consideration and treated the
  other stakeholders on equal terms. Public stockholders held 80% of NCS and
  overwhelmingly supported it.
- The NCS board **withdrew its recommendation** of the Genesis merger and
  recommended that stockholders reject it. The devices operated anyway, which is
  what exposes them as preclusive.
- NCS formed an Independent Committee of directors who were neither employees nor
  major stockholders, and the committee and the noteholders' Ad Hoc Committee
  extracted repeated improvements from Genesis. *Argued and rejected — a good
  process does not save a preclusive result.*
- The Genesis deal paid the senior debt in full, paid the Notes at par, assumed
  trade obligations, and delivered $24 million in stock to equity holders who
  were otherwise facing zero. *Argued and rejected — the threat of losing that
  deal was real, and the response was still draconian.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- A board's decision to adopt defensive devices protecting a merger agreement is
  reviewed under **Unocal**, not the business judgment rule, even where the
  merger itself works no change of control and therefore triggers no Revlon duty.
  Paramount v. Time settles that structural safety devices do not themselves
  trigger Revlon but are properly subject to Unocal.
- Stockholder voting agreements are not immune from that review when they are
  inextricably intertwined with the board's defensive scheme. Genesis made the
  voting agreements a non-negotiable condition of the merger agreement, and NCS
  was required to be a party, so the voting agreements were the linchpin of a
  single tripartite defence and are scrutinised collectively.
- Under Unocal's second stage the court asks first whether the devices are
  preclusive or coercive, and only if they are neither does the proportionality
  inquiry proceed. The analysis is **disjunctive**: either quality alone makes a
  measure draconian.
- The devices were **coercive**. The public stockholders who held 80% of NCS and
  favoured Omnicare were forced to accept the Genesis merger, so any stockholder
  vote "would have been robbed of its effectiveness by the impermissible coercion
  that predetermined the outcome."
- The devices were **preclusive**. The § 251(c) provision, the voting agreements
  and the missing fiduciary out operated in concert to make the Omnicare
  transaction or any other proposal "mathematically impossible" and
  "realistically unattainable," no matter how superior.
- Independently of Unocal, the devices were invalid because they left the board
  unable to discharge its continuing fiduciary duty to the minority when a
  superior proposal arrived. A merger contract provision that purports to require
  a board to act so as to limit the exercise of fiduciary duties is invalid and
  unenforceable.
- Where a cohesive group with majority voting power was irrevocably committed,
  the board could not abdicate to the stockholders, because the outcome of the
  vote was already a foregone conclusion. The board owed the minority an
  affirmative responsibility to protect their interests.
- The merger agreement and voting agreements, as combined, are invalid and
  unenforceable. Injunction granted against the Genesis transaction.

**Rejected**

- *Revlon governed, because the board effectively sold the company.* The Court of
  Chancery held the stock-for-stock merger worked no change of control and that
  NCS never started an active bidding process, abandoning any sale effort when it
  signed the exclusivity agreement. The Supreme Court does not disturb that
  conclusion, resolving the case entirely under Unocal instead.
- *Voting agreements are private stockholder conduct and cannot be deal protection
  devices, because stockholders may vote their own interest.* True in isolation,
  but these agreements were demanded by the acquiror as a condition precedent to
  the merger, were executed simultaneously with it, made NCS a party, and were
  the element without which the other two devices gave Genesis nothing. Their
  legal character follows their function within the board's defensive scheme.
- *The board acted in good faith on a reasonable investigation and faced a real
  threat of losing the only deal available.* The first Unocal stage was
  satisfied; the case is decided at the second. A preclusive or coercive response
  is outside the range of reasonableness however well-founded the threat.
- *Genesis bargained for certainty and Delaware should honour the contract.* The
  protection of a counterparty's contractual expectations "must yield to the
  supervening responsibility of the directors to discharge their fiduciary duties
  on a continuing basis," and a sophisticated counterparty that demands
  unreasonable terms from a board acting in violation of its duties acquires no
  vested rights in them.

## Context

Omnicare is the far end of the deal protection spectrum, and everything
interesting about the case sits in the **combination**. A § 251(c) force-the-vote
provision is lawful and common; irrevocable voting agreements from large holders
are lawful and common; even the absence of a fiduciary out is a negotiated term.
Stack all three on a company where two directors personally control a majority of
the vote and the stockholder franchise ceases to exist. The rule to carry forward
is that Unocal scrutinises the **overall response** as a unitary whole where the
measures are inextricably related.

Statutory update worth noting. The force-the-vote authority Omnicare calls
§ 251(c) now lives in **8 Del. C. § 146**, added in 2018, which lets a board
submit a matter to stockholders notwithstanding a later withdrawal of its
recommendation. Nothing in the 2018 amendment disturbs the holding, but a current
answer should cite § 146 rather than § 251(c).

The relationship to [[Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes]] is the structural point.
Revlon and Omnicare both enjoin lock-ups and both apply Unocal, but they get to
Unocal by different routes. Revlon reaches it because the company was being sold,
so price was the only cognisable objective; Omnicare reaches it because defensive
devices always get enhanced scrutiny, sale or no sale. That is why Omnicare
matters more in practice: it governs the ordinary strategic merger where Revlon is
nowhere in sight.

The relationship to [[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide]] and
[[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]] runs through the franchise. All three cases
turn on whether the minority stockholders had a real choice. MFW rewards a
controller who genuinely disables itself; Corwin defers to a vote that was fully
informed and uncoerced; Omnicare condemns a structure in which the vote was
predetermined before it was taken. A vote that cannot change anything cleanses
nothing, which is the through-line.

The dissents are unusually important. Two of the five justices, including the
Chief Justice, thought the majority had announced a bright-line rule requiring a
fiduciary out in every deal, and Veasey wrote that he hoped the holding would be
"interpreted narrowly and... seen as sui generis." That hope has largely been
realised: the Delaware bar treats Omnicare as confined to complete lock-ups, and
the case is cited far more often for the proposition that deal protections get
Unocal review than for any per se rule.

[[In re Trulia]], assigned the same day, is the procedural sequel. Omnicare is
what pre-closing deal litigation looks like when the claim is real; Trulia is what
the same docket looks like when the claim is manufactured.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Omnicare, a disappointed competing bidder, sought to enjoin the NCS–Genesis
  merger; NCS stockholders brought parallel fiduciary claims.
- The Court of Chancery declined to enjoin, finding the deal protections
  reasonable and proportionate under Unocal. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed,
  3–2.
- The 3–2 split is itself a fact about the case. The Delaware Supreme Court rarely
  divides, and both dissenters wrote separately.

### Facts

NCS Healthcare provided institutional pharmacy services. Reimbursement changes
beginning in late 1999 wrecked its collections and its stock: shares above $20 in
January 1999 were worth about $5 by year end, and after the 2001 defaults the
stock traded near zero. By early 2001 NCS was in default on approximately $350
million, had received a formal notice of acceleration from the Notes trustee, and
was negotiating pre-packaged bankruptcies with investor groups, none of which
offered adequate consideration for its stakeholders. The Noteholders organised an
Ad Hoc Committee to represent themselves.

In January 2002 Genesis, itself recently emerged from bankruptcy for much the same
reimbursement reasons, was approached by members of the Ad Hoc Committee and began
diligence. Genesis carried a grievance: it had lost an earlier institutional
pharmacy acquisition to Omnicare at, as one director put it, "11:59:59," and was
determined not to repeat the experience.

NCS's operating performance improved through early 2002, and the directors began
to think equity might recover something after all. In March the board formed an
Independent Committee of directors who were neither employees nor major
stockholders, on the view that with NCS in the zone of insolvency duties ran to
the enterprise as a whole. In June Genesis proposed a transaction outside
bankruptcy, and its terms improved steadily: senior debt repaid in full, trade
obligations assumed, Notes taken out at par in cash and stock, and by late June
$24 million in Genesis stock for the NCS common.

On June 26 Genesis demanded exclusivity before any further negotiation. The
Independent Committee, told on July 3 that Genesis wanted exclusivity as the first
step toward a completely locked-up transaction, agreed, and extended the
exclusivity period through July 31.

Omnicare, inferring from a run-up in NCS's stock that a deal was near, faxed a
proposal on July 26 that did not involve a bankruptcy sale. The Independent
Committee judged the risk of losing Genesis too great to respond directly, and
instead used the Omnicare letter as leverage. Genesis improved its terms
substantially on July 27 and delivered its twenty-four hour ultimatum. The board
approved the merger agreement, Outcalt and Shaw signed the voting agreements, and
hours after execution on July 29 Omnicare restated its proposal publicly with a
draft merger agreement attached. The board later withdrew its recommendation and
told stockholders to reject the Genesis deal, but the devices had already made the
outcome certain.

The merger agreement also carried a $6 million termination fee plus up to $5
million of documented expenses, and provided that a breach of the voting
agreements would let Genesis terminate and collect the fee — a breach that was
impossible, since the voting agreements were specifically enforceable.

### Issue

- Whether the NCS board's adoption of deal protection devices to secure a merger
  that worked no change of control is reviewed under the business judgment rule
  or under Unocal enhanced scrutiny.
- Whether stockholder voting agreements signed by two directors in their capacity
  as stockholders form part of the board's defensive response for that purpose.
- Whether the combination of a § 251(c) force-the-vote provision, irrevocable
  voting agreements carrying majority voting power, and the absence of an
  effective fiduciary out is within the range of reasonable responses to the
  threat of losing the Genesis transaction.

### Holding

- **On the standard: Unocal applies.** Defensive devices protecting a merger
  implicate the stockholders' statutory right to vote the transaction down, so
  the omnipresent spectre of conflict is present and enhanced scrutiny attaches
  even absent a change of control.
- **On the voting agreements: they are part of the defence.** Genesis made them a
  non-negotiable condition precedent, NCS was a party, and without them the other
  two devices were worth nothing to Genesis, so all three are scrutinised as a
  unitary response.
- **On proportionality: the devices fail.** They were both coercive, forcing the
  80% public float to accept a transaction it opposed, and preclusive, making any
  superior proposal mathematically impossible. Being draconian, they never reach
  the range-of-reasonableness inquiry, and they are unenforceable.
- **On the independent ground: an effective fiduciary out was required here.**
  With a majority voting bloc irrevocably committed, the board could not
  discharge its continuing duty to the minority, and a contract term that
  disables a board from exercising its fiduciary duties is invalid.
- **Disposition:** reversed; the merger agreement and voting agreements as
  combined are invalid and unenforceable.

### Rule

- A merger becomes final only on a stockholder vote, so Delaware law makes
  mergers "a shared enterprise and ownership decision" between board and
  stockholders.
- A stockholder's ability to reject a merger bears an inversely proportionate
  relationship to the structural and economic devices the board approves to
  protect it.
- Board-approved defensive devices protecting a merger must be within the board's
  statutory authority and consistent with its fiduciary duties, and are reviewed
  under Unocal even when the merger works no change of control.
- Where defensive actions are inextricably related, Unocal requires that they be
  scrutinised collectively as a unitary response.
- A response is **coercive** if it is aimed at forcing a management-sponsored
  alternative on stockholders; **preclusive** if it deprives stockholders of the
  right to receive all tender offers or precludes a bidder from seeking control
  by fundamentally restricting proxy contests or otherwise.
- The test is disjunctive: either preclusion or coercion is draconian and
  impermissible, and the proportionality inquiry is never reached.
- A contract provision requiring a board to act so as to limit the exercise of
  its fiduciary duties is invalid and unenforceable, and a counterparty that
  demanded it takes no vested right in it.

### Reasoning

*(Holland, J., for the majority)*

- The starting premise is statutory rather than equitable. Section 251 splits the
  merger decision between board and stockholders, so a device that neutralises
  the stockholder half of that decision is not merely unfair but inconsistent with
  the allocation the statute makes.
- Because Paramount v. Time already held that structural safety devices get
  Unocal review without triggering Revlon, the absence of a change of control
  answers the wrong question. The court never needs to decide whether Revlon
  applied.
- The voting agreements are characterised functionally. Genesis demanded them, NCS
  signed them, and they were "the linchpin of Genesis' proposed tripartite
  defense" — the other two devices "would not have provided Genesis with a
  complete defense in the absence of the voting agreements."
- Coercion is found in the position of the 80% public float, which was not forced
  to vote for the merger but was required to accept it "because it was a fait
  accompli." A vote whose outcome is predetermined is not a franchise.
- Preclusion is found in the arithmetic. With a majority irrevocably committed and
  the agreement required to go to a vote regardless of the board's
  recommendation, no competing proposal could succeed at any price.
- The independent fiduciary-out holding is deliberately narrower than it reads:
  it rests on the presence of "a cohesive group of stockholders with majority
  voting power... irrevocably committed," which is precisely the condition the
  dissenters call unique.

### Dissent / concurrence

*(Veasey, C.J., joined by Steele, J.)*

- The strength of Delaware law is an enabling statute with fiduciary principles
  applied case by case, and the majority has replaced that with a rule.
- These facts are unlikely to recur: insolvency, creditor pressure, a lengthy
  search, and no other viable bid when the agreement was signed.
- Review must be in real time, on the record before the board when the board
  acted, not on the ex post knowledge that Omnicare later paid more. "Our
  jurisprudence cannot... be seen as turning on such ex post felicitous results."
- Omnicare's bid was conditioned on completing diligence, which the board and the
  Ad Hoc Committee both saw as a crippling condition. Trading a certain Genesis
  deal for an uncertain Omnicare one was a business judgment.
- A lock-up lets the parties "exchange certainties," and certainty has value: the
  acquiror pays more for it, and the target avoids the damaged-goods discount of a
  failed deal. A bright-line prohibition will chill wealth-enhancing transactions.
- The lock-up was "indisputably a sine qua non to any deal with Genesis," so the
  majority's rule does not produce a better deal — it produces no deal.

*(Steele, J., separately)*

- Asks whether the court has moved from "thoughtful, retrospective, restrained
  flexibility" to "a bright line regulatory alternative," and whether a mandatory
  ex post efficient-breach analysis now applies to every challenged merger
  agreement.

There is a genuine tension the majority does not confront. NCS was insolvent, and
the board itself had concluded that in the zone of insolvency its duties ran to
the enterprise as a whole — which is why it formed the Independent Committee in
the first place. The majority's remedy is framed entirely in terms of an
"affirmative responsibility to protect those minority shareholders' interests,"
without engaging the board's own creditor-facing premise. That gap is where the
dissenters' real complaint lives.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the combination, not with any single device.** Nothing here is
  individually unlawful. Force-the-vote provisions are statutory, voting
  agreements are ordinary, and fiduciary outs are negotiated. The answer is that
  the three together made the vote a formality, and Unocal reviews the overall
  response as a unitary whole.
- **Know why Revlon is not the answer.** A stock-for-stock merger with a widely
  held acquiror is no change of control, and the Chancery found NCS never ran a
  bidding process. The Supreme Court never reaches Revlon, which is exactly the
  point of the case: Unocal reaches deal protections regardless.
- **Have the disjunctive test ready.** Preclusive **or** coercive is draconian,
  and a draconian measure never gets a proportionality analysis. Students
  routinely run the range-of-reasonableness inquiry anyway and lose the structure
  of the opinion.
- **Be precise on what coercion means here.** The stockholders were not compelled
  to vote yes; the merger was a fait accompli whatever they did. Coercion in this
  sense is the emptying of the franchise, not pressure on a voter.
- **Expect a question on whether a fiduciary out is always required.** Say no, and
  say why: the holding is conditioned on a cohesive majority bloc irrevocably
  committed, and Veasey's dissent asked for exactly that narrowing. Practitioners
  treat Omnicare as confined to total lock-ups.
- **Be ready to defend the dissent.** The strongest version is the sine qua non
  point — if Genesis would not deal without the lock-up, the majority's rule
  returns an insolvent company's stockholders to zero rather than $24 million,
  and the Omnicare bid was conditional on diligence that might never have closed.
  The best majority answer is that the board, having withdrawn its
  recommendation, was left as a spectator to its own merger.
- **Update the citation.** Say § 251(c) as the opinion does, then add that the
  force-the-vote authority now sits in § 146.
- **Numbers worth holding.** Four directors, two of whom held majority voting
  power; 80% public float; less than 24 hours to sign; $6 million termination fee
  plus $5 million of expenses; an Omnicare bid worth more than twice the Genesis
  consideration; a 3–2 decision.
