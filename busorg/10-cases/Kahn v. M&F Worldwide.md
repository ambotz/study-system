## Rule

In a controller buyout, business judgment review applies **if and only if** the
transaction is conditioned **ab initio** on both the approval of an independent,
adequately empowered special committee that meets its duty of care and the
uncoerced, informed vote of a majority of the minority — and a controller that
supplies only one of the two protections still gets nothing more than
burden-shifting inside entire fairness.

## Facts

- The controller conditioned the transaction on **both** protections **from the
  outset**, before negotiations began. The timing is the pivotal fact: a
  majority-of-the-minority condition offered late in the process is a deal-closer
  rather than a negotiating constraint, and produces none of the effects the rule
  rewards.
- The majority-of-the-minority condition was **non-waivable**, so the controller
  gave up its voting power rather than merely promising not to use it. The court
  identifies that surrender as "the vital distinction" between this case and
  Kahn v. Lynch, Southern Peru and Kahn v. Tremont.
- The special committee was independent, selected its own legal and financial
  advisors, and had the power to say no definitively rather than only to
  negotiate.
- The committee fulfilled its duty of care in negotiating price, and the minority
  vote was informed and uncoerced. Those are the two price-related pretrial
  determinations the structure is designed to produce.
- Appellants argued that independent directors may be inept or timid and that
  minority votes are skewed by arbitrageurs who approve any premium. *Argued and
  rejected — the first assertion is unsupported by the record and by Delaware's
  view of independent directors; the second is an editorial about investor
  motives that does not show the vote was involuntary.*
- Appellants conceded below that this transactional structure "is the optimal one
  for minority shareholders." *The concession does real work, because the rule is
  justified as an incentive to produce that structure.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Business judgment is the standard of review for a merger between a controlling
  stockholder and its subsidiary where the merger is conditioned **ab initio**
  upon both the approval of an independent, adequately empowered special
  committee that fulfils its duty of care and the uncoerced, informed vote of a
  majority of the minority.
- The elements, stated as a closed list: (i) the controller conditions the
  transaction on approval of both a special committee and a majority of the
  minority; (ii) the special committee is independent; (iii) the special committee
  is empowered to freely select its own advisors and to say no definitively;
  (iv) the special committee meets its duty of care in negotiating a fair price;
  (v) the vote of the minority is informed; and (vi) there is no coercion of the
  minority.
- A plaintiff who pleads a reasonably conceivable set of facts showing that **any**
  of those conditions did not exist states a claim and proceeds to discovery.
- If triable issues remain after discovery about whether either protection was
  established, or whether an established protection was effective, the case goes
  to trial under entire fairness.
- A controller that employs or establishes only **one** of the two protections
  continues to receive nothing more than burden-shifting within entire fairness.
- Entire fairness is applied in the controller merger context as a substitute for
  the dual statutory protections of disinterested board and stockholder approval,
  because the controller's influence potentially undermines both. Where the
  controller irrevocably and publicly disables itself from dictating either
  outcome, the countervailing influence is of equal or greater force and the
  merger acquires the protective characteristics of an arm's-length deal.
- The rule is justified as an incentive. Offering business judgment review gives
  controllers a strong reason to grant minority stockholders the structure most
  likely to protect them, and the "both" structure replicates the two independent
  approvals the DGCL requires in a third-party merger while the "or" structure
  does not.
- Both the dual protection structure and entire fairness converge on **price**,
  and the structure produces two price-related pretrial determinations: a fair
  price achieved by an empowered, careful, independent committee, and a fully
  informed, uncoerced majority-of-the-minority vote in favour of that price.

**Rejected**

- *Kahn v. Lynch and its progeny establish that entire fairness always governs a
  controller merger, however many protections are used.* Lynch held only that
  **either** protection shifts the burden, and did not involve a merger
  conditioned on both. In Lynch, Southern Peru and Tremont the controller never
  surrendered its voting power through a non-waivable
  majority-of-the-minority condition, which is the distinction that decides this
  case.
- *Special committee protection is illusory because independent directors may be
  inept or timid.* Directors with little regard for their duties or for how
  institutional investors perceive them are likely to be exceptional, and
  Delaware's jurisprudence does not embrace so sceptical a view of the
  independent director.
- *The minority vote is unreliable because arbitrageurs favour any premium.*
  Plaintiffs did not argue that minority holders vote out of fear of retribution.
  The argument that investors like premiums and that long-term holders sell into
  them is an observation about motive, not a showing that the vote is
  involuntary, and a majority-of-the-minority condition still gives minority
  investors a free opportunity to decide what is fair for themselves.

## Context

The problem MFW solves is an incentive problem, and Strine's analogy below is the
cleanest statement of it. A controller that knew one protection bought a burden
shift, and did not know whether a second bought anything at all, behaved like a
teenager told she may go to the movies if she finishes her math **or** her
English: she does one assignment and leaves the other for Sunday night. The "or"
structure therefore under-produced exactly the protection minority holders needed
most, and the "both" rule exists to make the second assignment worth doing.

Note the procedural history, because the citations are confusing. The Court of
Chancery opinion is In re MFW Shareholders Litigation, 67 A.3d 496 (Del. Ch.
2013), by then-Chancellor Strine, which supplies the teenager passage and most of
the reasoning the Supreme Court adopts by quotation. The Supreme Court affirmed on
March 14, 2014; the casebook heading dates the case to 2013.

The underlying transaction is not in the assigned excerpt, which is confined to
the standard-of-review discussion. In outline: Perelman's MacAndrews & Forbes held
roughly 43% of M&F Worldwide and offered in June 2011 to buy the rest at $24 in
cash, stating in the offer letter that the deal would proceed only with both a
special committee's approval and a non-waivable majority-of-the-minority vote, and
that no tender offer would follow if the committee said no. A four-member
independent committee retained Evercore and Willkie Farr, negotiated the price to
$25, and 65.4% of the minority voted in favour. Chancery granted summary judgment
for the defendants and the Supreme Court affirmed.

**MFW is now largely superseded by statute, and the comparison is the exam
question.** SB 21 added 8 Del. C. § 144(b) and (c), covered in
[[Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)]]. Under § 144(b) an
ordinary controller transaction needs only **one** of three routes — a
disinterested committee empowered to negotiate and reject, a disinterested
stockholder vote, or fairness. The two-protection requirement survives only for
**going private** transactions under § 144(c), and even there the statute
substitutes a looser timing rule: the vote condition must be in the deal's terms
when the deal is submitted to stockholders, rather than ab initio. On any post-SB
21 fact pattern the first move is to classify the deal as going private or not,
because that classification decides how many protections are needed.

[[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 2)]] is the clearest illustration of the old rule's
cost. Musk's board conditioned the SolarCity acquisition on a majority-of-the-
disinterested vote but formed no special committee, which under MFW left entire
fairness in place and forced a full trial that Musk ultimately won on price.

The relationship to [[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]], assigned the same day,
is complementary rather than overlapping. Corwin cleanses only transactions **not
subject to entire fairness**, which is to say deals with no controller on the
other side. MFW is the route back to business judgment when a controller **is** on
the other side. Get the order right: ask first whether a controller stands on both
sides, then apply MFW if so and Corwin if not.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from summary judgment for the defendants in the Court of Chancery.
- The Supreme Court frames the appeal as a question of first impression: what
  standard governs a controller merger conditioned on both protections.
- The posture shapes the holding's architecture. Because the case arrives on
  summary judgment, the opinion is written as a set of **pretrial gates** — what a
  plaintiff must plead to survive dismissal, and what triable issue sends the case
  to an entire fairness trial — rather than as a liability standard.

### Facts

The doctrinal setting matters more than the transaction. For two decades after
Kahn v. Lynch it was settled that a controller who used **one** procedural
protection — either an independent special committee or a
majority-of-the-minority vote — shifted the burden of proving entire fairness from
the defendant to the plaintiff. The Supreme Court had never said what happened
when a controller used both, and language in Lynch, Southern Peru and Kahn v.
Tremont could be read to suggest entire fairness applied regardless.

That uncertainty had a predictable effect on behaviour. A controller assessing
whether to accept a non-waivable majority-of-the-minority condition was being
asked to take a certain and substantial business risk — the deal could simply
fail — in exchange for a legal benefit that might not exist. As then-Chancellor
Strine put it, the incentive to use both devices and thereby replicate the key
elements of an arm's-length merger process was "minimal to downright
discouraging."

The Court of Chancery's answer rested on a premise about which common law rule
best protects minority investors: the rule that most encourages controllers to
grant both protections. A structure with both is not a marginal improvement on a
structure with one. The "or" structure satisfies only one of the DGCL's two
statutory requirements — director approval and stockholder approval — through
impartial decision-makers, while the "both" structure supplies two independent
approvals serving independent integrity-enforcing functions.

The transaction itself, drawn from the full opinion rather than the assigned
excerpt, was Perelman's buyout of the roughly 57% of M&F Worldwide his
MacAndrews & Forbes entities did not already own, offered at $24 and closed at $25
after negotiation by a four-member independent committee with its own advisors,
approved by 65.4% of the minority.

### Issue

- What standard of review governs a merger between a controlling stockholder and
  its subsidiary where the merger is conditioned ab initio on both an independent,
  adequately empowered special committee that meets its duty of care and an
  uncoerced, informed majority-of-the-minority vote.
- Whether either protection is adequate to justify departing from entire fairness,
  given the risks of director timidity and arbitrageur-driven voting.
- What a plaintiff must plead, and what must remain in dispute after discovery, to
  avoid business judgment review under the new standard.

### Holding

- **On the standard: business judgment review applies, but only on the full
  six-element showing.** The controller must condition the deal on both
  protections from the outset, the committee must be independent, empowered to
  choose its own advisors and to refuse, and careful as to price, and the minority
  vote must be informed and uncoerced.
- **On the adequacy objections: rejected.** Neither the possibility of ineffective
  independent directors nor the premium-seeking behaviour of investors shows that
  the protections fail, and the appellants conceded that the dual structure is
  optimal for the minority.
- **On the pleading and trial gates: a plaintiff who conceivably pleads the
  absence of any element proceeds to discovery**, and a triable issue about
  whether either protection existed or was effective sends the case to an entire
  fairness trial.
- **On partial compliance: one protection still yields only burden-shifting**
  within entire fairness, exactly as under Kahn v. Lynch.
- **Disposition:** summary judgment for the defendants affirmed.

### Rule

- Entire fairness is the highest standard of review in corporate law, applied in
  the controller merger context as a substitute for the dual statutory protections
  of disinterested board and stockholder approval.
- That substitution is warranted only where the controller's influence actually
  undermines both protections, which is not true in every controlled merger.
- Where a controller irrevocably and publicly disables itself from using its
  control to dictate both the negotiation and the vote, the merger acquires the
  protective characteristics of a third-party, arm's-length merger.
- Business judgment review then applies if and only if all six enumerated
  conditions are satisfied.
- The condition must be imposed **ab initio**, so that from inception the
  controller knows it cannot bypass the committee's ability to say no and cannot
  dangle the minority vote late in the process as a deal-closer instead of moving
  on price.
- A single protection, or protections adopted after negotiations begin, produce
  burden-shifting within entire fairness and nothing more.

### Reasoning

*(Holland, J.)*

- The vital distinction from Lynch, Southern Peru and Tremont is not the number of
  procedural devices as such but that in those cases the controller never gave up
  its voting power through a non-waivable majority-of-the-minority condition.
- Entire fairness is instrumental, not intrinsic. It stands in for the two
  statutory approvals when a controller can taint both; when the controller has
  disabled itself as to both, the reason for the substitution disappears.
- The court reasons prospectively about incentives rather than retrospectively
  about fairness. Its central claim is that the rule announced will change how
  controllers structure deals, and that the resulting structures will protect
  minorities better than judicial fairness review does.
- The convergence argument closes the gap. Because entire fairness in a
  non-fraudulent transaction treats price as potentially "the preponderant
  consideration," and because the dual structure forces two independent
  determinations about price, the structure reaches the same destination by a
  cheaper route.
- The ab initio requirement is doing bargaining-power work, not formal work. Its
  function is to deny the controller the ability to hold the vote condition in
  reserve and spend it instead of raising the price.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion's weakest link is the one the appellants identified and the court
answered mainly by assertion. Whether independent directors are as reliable as the
court supposes is an empirical claim, and the answer given — that inattentive
directors "are likely to be exceptional" and that Delaware's jurisprudence does
not embrace a sceptical view — is a statement of judicial policy rather than
evidence. The response to the arbitrageur objection is stronger, because it
correctly separates the motive behind a vote from the voluntariness of it.

### Cold-call notes

- **Recite all six elements, in order.** The holding is expressly "if and only
  if," and the opinion lists them as a closed set. Dropping element (iii) — the
  power to select advisors and to say no definitively — is the most common
  omission, and it is the element Tesla 2 turns on.
- **Ab initio is the element most likely to be tested.** Be ready to say why:
  a condition imposed at the start constrains the controller through the whole
  negotiation, while one offered late is spent as a closing concession rather than
  a price concession.
- **Distinguish burden-shifting from standard-shifting.** One protection shifts
  the burden of proving entire fairness to the plaintiff. Both protections change
  the standard itself to business judgment. Students blur these constantly.
- **Have the pleading gate ready.** A plaintiff who conceivably pleads that any
  one element was absent survives dismissal and gets discovery. That is why MFW
  compliance is litigated on the facts far more often than the rule's clean
  structure suggests.
- **Lead the post-SB 21 comparison with classification.** Ask first whether the
  deal is going private. If it is, § 144(c) requires both protections and MFW is
  effectively codified. If it is not, § 144(b) requires only one of three routes
  and reciting MFW is a wrong answer.
- **Do not confuse MFW with Corwin.** Corwin cleanses only where entire fairness
  does not apply, so a controller standing on both sides takes it out of Corwin
  entirely. MFW is the controller route; Corwin is the non-controller route.
- **Know the teenager analogy well enough to use it.** It is the most quotable
  thing in the assignment and it states the entire rationale: a reward for doing
  one assignment produces one assignment.
