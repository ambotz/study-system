## Rule

Stockholders may not use a **bylaw** to take a core managerial function from the
board. Bylaws may define the **process and procedures** by which board decisions
are made; they may not mandate how the board decides substantive business
questions, and a facially procedural bylaw still fails if its intent and effect
intrude on board authority.

## Facts

- John Gorman held a **majority of the voting stock** of Westech Capital Corp.
  *Control is assumed throughout and changes nothing — the bylaw is invalid
  regardless of who holds the shares.*
- Gorman amended the bylaws **by written consent** to let a stockholder remove and
  replace corporate officers, then immediately fired the CEO, Salamone, and
  appointed himself.
- The Amended Bylaw permitted removal **without cause**, at a meeting or by written
  consent. *This is the feature that converts it from procedure into substance.*
- The bylaw required the board to "immediately implement ... [the] removal of an
  officer by the stockholders." *A directive compelling board action is what takes
  it outside the process/procedure safe harbour.*
- Gorman characterised the bylaw as merely prescribing who may select and replace
  officers. *Argued and rejected — the bylaw did more than say how; it relocated
  the decision.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Delaware law does not allow stockholders to remove corporate officers directly
  through authority purportedly conferred by a bylaw. Such a bylaw "would unduly
  interfere with directors' management prerogatives by preventing them from
  discharging one of their most important functions."
- § 141(a) "establishes the bedrock statutory principle of director primacy." The
  board's responsibility "entails the duty to establish or approve the long-term
  strategic, financial and organizational goals of the corporation; to approve
  formal or informal plans for the achievement of these goals; to monitor corporate
  performance; and to act."
- Stockholders "may not directly manage the business and affairs of the
  corporation, at least without specific authorization in either the statute or the
  certificate of incorporation."
- **The operative line**: bylaws may not "mandate how the board should decide
  specific substantive business decisions, but ... [they may] define the process and
  procedures by which those decisions are made." Whether a bylaw is process-related
  "must necessarily be determined in light of its context and purpose," and "even
  facially procedural bylaws can unduly intrude upon board authority."
- Choosing who conducts daily business is "a primary way by which a corporate board
  manages a company." A board without power to control who serves as CEO could not
  conceivably set long-term strategy.
- A bylaw "cannot mandate board action in circumstances that a proper application of
  fiduciary principles could preclude."
- The Amended Bylaw is invalid and the written consents removing Salamone were of
  no effect.

**Rejected**

- *The Amended Bylaw merely prescribes the procedure by which officers are elected
  and removed — it defines who may select and replace them.* The bylaw permitted
  removal without cause, which "would allow them to make substantive business
  decisions for the Company," and was "apparently intended to take an important
  managerial function from the board." It gave stockholders more than an advisory
  function.
- *Stockholders can agree among themselves through a bylaw to manage the
  corporation as they see fit — this is private ordering.* "Perhaps the question
  should be viewed as one of private ordering. However, ... a Delaware corporation
  is a board-centric entity. Other governance structures can be imposed on other
  entities, if that is what the stakeholders desire." **If you want stockholders to
  run the business, do not use a corporation.**

## Context

Paired with [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]]
as the two halves of the same § 141(a) limit. Gorman concerns a **bylaw**; Moelis
concerns a **stockholder agreement**. Both fail for the same reason and both are
rescued by the same instrument — the certificate of incorporation, which § 141(a)
itself names as the place where board authority may be varied.

The three-tier hierarchy the session is built around: **charter** can displace
board authority because § 141(a) says so; **bylaws** can regulate process but not
substance; **contracts among stockholders** cannot do internal governance at all.
Everything in this part of the course is an application of that ordering.

Footnote 13's "infinite loop" point is the structural argument and is worth keeping:
stockholders remove an officer and appoint a successor, the board replaces that
successor, the stockholders repeat. A governance rule with no stable equilibrium is
evidence the rule sits in the wrong instrument.

Footnote 14 leaves open whether a bylaw could let stockholders fill **vacant**
offices directly — a narrower question than removal without cause, and a plausible
exam variation.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Delaware Court of Chancery, on the validity of a bylaw amendment and the written
  consents taken under it.
- The casebook presents the opinion in heavily edited form with bracketed editorial
  summary, so the procedural detail is thin.

### Facts

Westech Capital Corp. had a controlling stockholder in John Gorman, who held a
majority of its voting stock. Rather than act through the board, Gorman amended the
company's bylaws by written consent to give stockholders the power to remove and
replace corporate officers, and then used that power at once — terminating
Salamone as CEO and installing himself in the role.

The dispute is therefore not about whether Gorman had the votes. He did. It is
about whether the corporate form permits a stockholder, however large, to reach
past the board and operate the company directly.

### Issue

- Whether stockholders may adopt a bylaw empowering themselves to remove and replace
  corporate officers directly, without cause.
- Whether such a bylaw is saved as "process-related" because it prescribes who
  selects and removes officers rather than dictating a particular business outcome.

### Holding

- **The bylaw is invalid.** Removing and replacing officers is a core management
  function protected by § 141(a), and a bylaw that relocates it to stockholders
  unduly interferes with the board's management prerogatives.
- **The process/procedure defence fails.** Permitting removal without cause lets
  stockholders make substantive business decisions, and the compulsion on the board
  to implement removals could force action that fiduciary duty would preclude.
- **Disposition:** the written consents removing Salamone as CEO were of no effect.

### Rule

- § 141(a) makes director primacy the bedrock statutory principle: the business and
  affairs of every corporation shall be managed by or under the direction of a
  board, except as otherwise provided in the chapter or **in the certificate of
  incorporation**.
- Stockholders may not directly manage the business and affairs of the corporation
  absent specific authorisation in the statute or the charter.
- Bylaws may define the process and procedures by which board decisions are made.
- Bylaws may not mandate how the board decides specific substantive business
  questions.
- Whether a bylaw is process-related is judged by its context and purpose, and even
  facially procedural bylaws can unduly intrude on board authority.
- A bylaw cannot mandate board action in circumstances where a proper application of
  fiduciary principles could preclude that action.

### Reasoning

*(Noble, V.C.)*

- The court locates the function within board primacy by asking what a board does:
  set long-term strategy, approve plans, monitor performance, act when its informed
  judgment says to. Choosing the CEO is instrumental to all four — "often it is said
  that a board's most important task is to hire, monitor, and fire the CEO."
- The process/substance test is applied to **intent and effect**, not to the bylaw's
  drafting. Gorman's framing was accurate on its face; the court looks at what the
  bylaw was for and what it would do.
- The fiduciary-conflict point supplies an independent ground: a mandatory directive
  to implement stockholder removals could compel a board to act against its own
  considered judgment of the company's interest.
- The private-ordering answer is jurisdictional rather than dismissive. Delaware
  does not deny that stakeholders may want stockholder management; it says that
  desire belongs in a different entity form.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None.
- The pressure point, in short: Delaware invalidates a governance arrangement that
  a unanimous, fully informed set of stockholders actually wanted, on the basis of
  a default rule in § 141(a) that the charter itself is allowed to displace. The
  answer is formal — put it in the charter — which makes the rule look like one
  about **instrument choice** rather than about substance. Moelis confronts the same
  objection directly and answers it with the doctrine of independent legal
  significance.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect to be asked how Gorman could have achieved this lawfully.** Put it in the
  certificate of incorporation. § 141(a) contains its own exception — "except as may
  be otherwise provided in this chapter or in its certificate of incorporation" —
  and that clause is the answer to almost every question in this session.
- **The test to state**: bylaws govern process, not substance, judged by context and
  purpose. Then apply it: removal *without cause* is substance, because it lets
  stockholders decide who runs the company and why.
- **Do not argue he had the votes.** Control is conceded and irrelevant. A majority
  stockholder is as bound by § 141(a) as a minority one; Moelis makes the same point
  explicitly.
- **Have footnote 13 ready.** The infinite removal loop is a memorable structural
  argument and shows you read past the text.
- **Have footnote 14 ready as the variation.** The court expressly leaves open
  whether a bylaw could let stockholders fill *vacant* offices — no incumbent
  displaced, no compulsion on the board. That is the obvious next hypothetical.
