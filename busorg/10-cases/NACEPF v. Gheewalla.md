## Rule

Creditors of a Delaware corporation that is **either insolvent or in the zone of insolvency**
have **no right, as a matter of law, to assert direct claims** for breach of fiduciary duty
against its directors. On **insolvency**, creditors gain **standing to sue derivatively** on
the corporation's behalf, because they then "take the place of the shareholders as the residual
beneficiaries of any increase in value."

## Facts

- NACEPF sued **as a creditor, not a stockholder**, and asserted **direct, not derivative**
  claims. It "waived any basis it may have had for pursuit of its claim derivatively." That
  choice is what decides the case.
- The defendants were three directors serving "at the behest of Goldman Sachs," alleged to have
  controlled Clearwire despite comprising **less than a majority** of the board, because Goldman
  was the company's "only source of funding," and to have used that power "to favor Goldman
  Sachs' agenda in derogation of their fiduciary duties."
- The relationship arose from the **Master Use and Royalty Agreement** — a contract. NACEPF held
  FCC-regulated radio spectrum licences and contracted for Clearwire to obtain rights to them as
  existing leases expired.
- For Rule 12(b)(6) purposes the Court of Chancery accepted that Clearwire was **in the zone of
  insolvency** for a substantial part of the relevant period and **actually insolvent** for at
  least part of it. *So the case is not decided on any failure to plead insolvency.*
- The Court of Chancery **assumed arguendo** that a direct claim is cognisable in actual
  insolvency and dismissed for failure to meet the pleading requirements. *The Supreme Court
  reaches the legal question the lower court avoided.*
- Insolvency may be shown by either "**a deficiency of assets below liabilities** with no
  reasonable prospect that the business can be successfully continued in the face thereof," or
  "**an inability to meet maturing obligations** as they fall due in the ordinary course of
  business."
- The court expressly **declines to define the zone of insolvency**, both below and on appeal.
  *The holding makes the definition unnecessary, which is itself part of the point.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- "It is well established that the directors owe their fiduciary obligations **to the corporation
  and its shareholders**," and "the general rule is that **directors do not owe creditors duties
  beyond the relevant contractual terms**."
- Creditors "are afforded protection through **contractual agreements, fraud and fraudulent
  conveyance law, implied covenants of good faith and fair dealing, bankruptcy law, general
  commercial law and other sources of creditor rights**," and "Delaware courts have traditionally
  been reluctant to expand existing fiduciary duties."
- **Zone of insolvency: no direct claim.** "[T]he need for providing directors with **definitive
  guidance** compels us to hold that no direct claim for breach of fiduciary duties may be
  asserted by the creditors of a solvent corporation that is operating in the zone of insolvency."
- "When a solvent corporation is navigating in the zone of insolvency, **the focus for Delaware
  directors does not change**: directors must continue to discharge their fiduciary duties to the
  corporation and its shareholders by exercising their business judgment in the best interests of
  the corporation for the benefit of its shareholder owners."
- Adopting Chancery's reasoning, recognising such claims would be "using the law of fiduciary duty
  to **fill gaps that do not exist**," the benefit "appears minimal, at best, and significantly
  outweighed by the costs to economic efficiency," and "an otherwise solvent corporation operating
  in the zone of insolvency is one **in most need of effective and proactive leadership**—as well
  as the ability to negotiate in good faith with its creditors."
- **Actual insolvency: derivative standing, yes; direct claims, no.** "When a corporation is
  insolvent... its creditors take the place of the shareholders as the residual beneficiaries of
  any increase in value," and "[t]he corporation's insolvency 'makes the creditors the principal
  constituency injured by any fiduciary breaches that diminish the firm's value.' Therefore,
  **equitable considerations give creditors standing to pursue derivative claims**."
- Insolvency does not transform the claims. "The fact that the corporation has become insolvent
  **does not turn [derivative] claims into direct creditor claims, it simply provides creditors
  with standing** to assert those claims. At all times, claims of this kind **belong to the
  corporation itself**," because the acts "operate to injure the firm in the first instance by
  reducing its value, injuring creditors only indirectly."
- "[I]ndividual creditors of an insolvent corporation have **no right to assert direct claims** for
  breach of fiduciary duty against corporate directors."
- The reason is conflict avoidance. Recognising direct duties "would create **uncertainty** for
  directors who have a fiduciary duty to exercise their business judgment in the best interest of
  the insolvent corporation," and "would create a **conflict** between those directors' duty to
  maximize the value of the insolvent corporation for the benefit of all those having an interest
  in it, and the newly recognized direct fiduciary duty to individual creditors."
- "Directors of insolvent corporations must **retain the freedom to engage in vigorous, good faith
  negotiations with individual creditors** for the benefit of the corporation."
- Creditors "may nonetheless protect their interest by bringing **derivative claims** on behalf of
  the insolvent corporation or any other **direct nonfiduciary claim**... that may be available."
- Affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *A corporation in the zone of insolvency should owe direct fiduciary duties to creditors, because
  the residual risk has begun to shift to them.* Creditors already have contract, fraudulent
  conveyance, the implied covenant, bankruptcy and commercial law; the additional layer is
  unnecessary, the benefit minimal, and the cost to a company most in need of proactive leadership
  significant.
- *At least on actual insolvency, creditors should have direct claims, since they are then the
  residual claimants.* Residual status supplies **derivative standing**, not a direct right. The
  claims belong to the corporation because the injury is to the firm in the first instance.
- *Production Resources and Big Lots leave room for a narrow direct claim where directors display
  "such a marked degree of animus towards a particular creditor with a proven entitlement to
  payment."* "**We think not.**" While a direct claim may arise "out of contract or tort," today's
  holding "precludes a direct claim arising out of a purported breach of a fiduciary duty owed to
  that creditor," and the Big Lots dicta is "inconsistent with and precluded by our holding."

## Context

Gheewalla is a **standing** case that reads like a duty case, and keeping that straight is the
whole exam point. It does not hold that directors owe creditors nothing. It holds that whatever
directors owe runs **to the corporation**, so a creditor's remedy is to sue **derivatively** once
the corporation is insolvent, and never directly.

The decision was a deliberate retreat from Credit Lyonnais and the "zone of insolvency" literature
that had grown up around it. The court's stated reason is institutional rather than economic: "the
need for providing directors with **definitive guidance**," and the aim of supplying "clear signal
beacons and brightly lined channel markers." Having held that the zone changes nothing, the court
never has to define it — which is why the opinion twice declines to.

The doctrinal architecture is the **residual claimant** framework that [[In re Trados]] states
directly and that [[Quadrant Structured Products v. Vertin]] applies the following day. Directors
maximise firm value for the residual claimants. In a solvent company that is the stockholders; on
insolvency creditors "join the class of residual claimants" and get standing to enforce the same
duties. The duties themselves never change, which is exactly what makes the rule administrable.

Quadrant then supplies the operational content Gheewalla leaves open, and it is the more useful
case in practice. Insolvency is pleaded by the **balance sheet** test or the **cash flow** test.
Specific transfers from an insolvent company to its controller draw **entire fairness**. But
ordinary **business strategy** decisions — including the decision to take more risk, or to keep
operating rather than liquidate — stay in the business judgment rule, and Quadrant explains that
holding otherwise "would be inconsistent with the explanation the Delaware Supreme Court gave in
Gheewalla for declining to recognize" direct duties.

Three practical limits follow from the holding and are worth carrying.

**Section 102(b)(7) still applies.** A creditor suing derivatively "seek[s] to impose personal
liability on directors," so an exculpatory charter reaches the claim — which means creditor
derivative suits must be pleaded as **loyalty or bad faith**, exactly like stockholder suits.

**The trust fund doctrine is not the law.** Directors of an insolvent firm need not wind down for
creditors; they "continue to have the task of attempting to maximize the economic value of the
firm," and "do not become a guarantor" of a strategy's success.

**Non-fiduciary direct claims survive.** Contract, fraudulent conveyance, the implied covenant,
and tort are all untouched. Gheewalla closes one door and expressly leaves the others open — and
in a case like this, where the relationship began with the Master Use and Royalty Agreement, the
contract was always the natural place to look.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from the Court of Chancery's dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim.
- Only **Count II** is relevant: that because Clearwire was insolvent or in the zone of insolvency,
  the defendants owed fiduciary duties to NACEPF "as a substantial creditor" and breached them.
- Affirmed.
- The posture is decisive and self-inflicted. NACEPF **waived** any derivative theory both below and
  on appeal, so the only question was whether a **direct** creditor claim exists. A derivative
  complaint on the same facts would have survived Gheewalla's own holding.

### Facts

NACEPF holds radio wave spectrum licences regulated by the Federal Communications Commission. In
March 2001 it joined other spectrum licence-holders in entering the Master Use and Royalty
Agreement with Clearwire Holdings, a Delaware corporation, under which Clearwire could obtain
rights to those licences as existing leases expired and the current lessees failed to exercise
rights of first refusal.

Gheewalla, Cardinale and Daly served as directors of Clearwire at the behest of Goldman Sachs.
NACEPF's complaint alleged that although they comprised less than a majority of the board, they
were able to control Clearwire because Goldman Sachs was its only source of funding, and that they
used that power to favour Goldman's agenda in derogation of their duties as directors.

NACEPF was not a stockholder. It sued as a putative creditor, asserting direct claims against
directors who had served while Clearwire was either insolvent or in the zone of insolvency.

The Court of Chancery set out the two tests for insolvency — a deficiency of assets below
liabilities with no reasonable prospect of successful continuation, or an inability to meet
maturing obligations as they fall due — and found, for Rule 12(b)(6) purposes, that the complaint
adequately alleged operation in the zone of insolvency for a substantial part of the relevant
period and actual insolvency for at least part of it. It declined to define the zone precisely.

It then held that creditors in the zone may not assert direct claims, and, assuming arguendo that
a direct claim might exist in actual insolvency, held that the complaint failed to meet the
pleading requirements described in Production Resources and Big Lots.

### Issue

- Whether, as a matter of Delaware law, a creditor of a corporation operating within the **zone of
  insolvency** may bring a direct action against its directors for breach of fiduciary duty.
- Whether a creditor of an **actually insolvent** corporation may do so.
- What remedy creditors do have against the directors of an insolvent corporation.

### Holding

- **Zone of insolvency: no direct claim.** Directors' focus does not change; they continue to owe
  duties to the corporation and its shareholders and to exercise business judgment for the
  shareholder owners.
- **Actual insolvency: no direct claim either.** Recognising one would create uncertainty and a
  conflict between the duty to maximise value for all those interested in the insolvent corporation
  and a new duty to individual creditors, and would deprive directors of the freedom to negotiate
  vigorously and in good faith with individual creditors.
- **Actual insolvency: derivative standing, yes.** Creditors become the principal constituency
  injured by fiduciary breaches that diminish firm value, and equitable considerations give them
  standing to sue derivatively.
- **The narrow "animus" direct claim floated in Production Resources and Big Lots does not exist.**
  "We think not."
- **Disposition:** affirmed; Count II fails to state a claim.

### Rule

- Directors owe their fiduciary obligations to the corporation and its shareholders.
- Directors do not owe creditors duties beyond the relevant contractual terms.
- Creditors of a corporation in the zone of insolvency have no right to assert direct fiduciary
  claims against its directors.
- When a solvent corporation navigates the zone of insolvency, the directors' focus does not change.
- When a corporation is insolvent, its creditors take the place of the shareholders as the residual
  beneficiaries of any increase in value.
- Creditors of an insolvent corporation have standing to maintain derivative claims on the
  corporation's behalf for breaches of fiduciary duty.
- Insolvency does not convert derivative claims into direct ones; it supplies standing, and the
  claims continue to belong to the corporation.
- Individual creditors of an insolvent corporation have no right to assert direct fiduciary claims,
  but may bring derivative claims or any available direct non-fiduciary claim.

### Reasoning

*(Holland, J.)*

- The court's premise is that creditors are **already protected**, and by instruments they chose.
  Contract, security, the implied covenant, fraudulent conveyance, bankruptcy and commercial law
  are listed twice, and the Production Resources passage adopted in full makes the point sharply:
  recognising fiduciary duties here means "using the law of fiduciary duty to fill gaps that do not
  exist."
- The reasoning is explicitly **administrability-driven**. Directors need "clear signal beacons and
  brightly lined channel markers," and a doctrine that shifts duties somewhere inside an undefined
  zone supplies the opposite. Having held the zone irrelevant, the court is spared having to map it.
- The **incentive** argument cuts the other way from the plaintiff's intuition. A company near
  insolvency is the one that most needs decisive leadership and the ability to bargain with its
  creditors, and personal liability exposure for directors would chill precisely that.
- On actual insolvency the court reaches the merits question Chancery had ducked, and separates
  **standing** from **duty** cleanly. Residual status explains why creditors may enforce the
  corporation's claims; it does not create claims of their own, because the injury runs to the firm
  first and to creditors only through the firm.
- The conflict argument is the strongest part of the holding. A direct duty to individual creditors
  would collide with the duty to maximise value for everyone interested in the corporation, and
  would put a director in an impossible position whenever those interests diverged — which, in an
  insolvent company with a capital stack, is constantly.
- The disposal of the Production Resources animus dicta is deliberately blunt — "We think not" — and
  the court preserves the safety valve in the same breath by noting that contract and tort claims
  remain available. The message is that the **label** matters: the same conduct may be actionable,
  but not as a fiduciary breach owed to a creditor.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The rule is clean but it leaves a real gap in the one case that motivated the doctrine. A creditor
whose corporation is insolvent must sue derivatively, which means any recovery goes to the
corporation and is then distributed according to priority — and where the wrongdoing directors are
aligned with a controller who also sits above or alongside that creditor in the stack, the
practical benefit can be thin. Quadrant shows the mechanism working, but it also shows what it
takes: specific transfers to the controller, pleaded with enough particularity to draw entire
fairness, and directors who cannot invoke § 102(b)(7) because their independence is successfully
challenged.

### Cold-call notes

- **Answer the standing question, not the duty question.** Directors' duties run to the
  **corporation** throughout. Insolvency changes **who may enforce** them, not what they are.
- **Give the two-by-two.** Zone of insolvency: no direct claim, no derivative standing (the company
  is still solvent, so stockholders enforce). Actual insolvency: no direct claim, but **derivative
  standing** for creditors.
- **Know the two tests for insolvency.** Balance sheet — assets below liabilities with no reasonable
  prospect of continuing; or cash flow — inability to meet maturing obligations as they fall due.
  Quadrant applies the balance sheet test the next day.
- **Have the list of existing creditor protections ready.** Contract, fraudulent conveyance, the
  implied covenant, bankruptcy, general commercial law. It is the court's central answer, and it is
  why the direct claim is called a gap-filler for a gap that does not exist.
- **Quote the guidance rationale.** "[C]lear signal beacons and brightly lined channel markers." The
  holding is driven by administrability, which is also why the zone is never defined.
- **State the conflict point.** A direct duty to individual creditors would conflict with the duty to
  maximise value for all those interested in the insolvent corporation, and would chill vigorous
  good faith negotiation with creditors.
- **Do not overstate the holding.** Non-fiduciary direct claims — contract, tort, fraudulent transfer
  — are expressly preserved. NACEPF's own relationship was contractual, under the Master Use and
  Royalty Agreement.
- **Note the self-inflicted wound.** NACEPF waived the derivative theory. The opinion is a lesson in
  pleading as much as in doctrine.
- **Connect forward to § 102(b)(7).** A creditor suing derivatively is seeking to impose personal
  liability on directors, so an exculpatory charter applies and the claim must be loyalty or bad
  faith.
