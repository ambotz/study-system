## Rule

A special litigation committee moving to terminate a derivative action **bears the burden**
of proving its independence, good faith, and the reasonableness of its investigation, and
must "convince [the court] that there is no material issue of fact calling into doubt its
independence." Independence is assessed **in the aggregate**, not fact by fact, and asks
whether "material considerations other than the best interests of [the corporation] could
have influenced" the committee.

## Facts

- The derivative claims alleged **insider trading** by four Oracle directors who sold while
  holding material non-public information that Oracle would miss its Q3 FY2001 guidance.
- **Ellison** was Chairman, CEO, and largest stockholder with nearly **25%** of the voting
  shares, "one of the wealthiest men in America," with "regular access to a great deal of
  information about how Oracle is performing on a week-to-week basis." **Henley** was CFO
  and a director, whose "finger [was] on the pulse of Oracle's performance constantly."
- **The SLC was two Stanford professors**, recruited to the Oracle board in summer 2001 and
  then "soon asked to investigate a fellow professor and two benefactors of the University."
- The Stanford web: defendant **Boskin** was a Stanford professor who had taught one of the
  SLC members; defendant **Lucas** was a Stanford donor; and **Ellison was considering an
  enormous donation to Stanford at the very time the two professors were being courted for
  the Oracle board**.
- *Each connection, taken alone, might have been survivable.* "Taken in isolation, the facts
  about Ellison might well not be enough to compromise the SLC's independence. But that is not
  the relevant inquiry."

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The SLC failed its **Zapata** burden; the motion to terminate is denied.
- The inquiry is aggregate: "The pertinent question is whether, **given all the facts**, the
  SLC has met its independence burden."
- "[B]y any measure this was a social atmosphere painted in too much vivid **Stanford Cardinal
  red** for the SLC members to have reasonably ignored it."
- The connections "generate a reasonable doubt about the SLC's impartiality because they
  suggest that **material considerations other than the best interests of Oracle** could have
  influenced the SLC's inquiry and judgments."
- **This is not a finding of bad faith.** The conclusion "is not a conclusion that the two
  accomplished professors who comprise it are not persons of good faith and moral probity, it
  is solely to conclude that they were **not situated to act with the required degree of
  impartiality**."
- The rationale: "Zapata requires independence to ensure that stockholders do not have to rely
  upon special litigation committee members who must put aside personal considerations that
  are ordinarily influential in daily behavior in making the already difficult decision to
  **accuse fellow directors of serious wrongdoing**."
- "Rather than form an SLC whose membership was free from bias-creating relationships, Oracle
  formed a committee **fraught with them**."

**Rejected**

- *The SLC members had no financial interest in the defendants, no business dealings with
  them, and were accomplished academics of unquestioned integrity.* All accepted, and none
  answers the question. Independence is about situation, not character.
- *Each Stanford connection is individually too attenuated to matter.* The court agrees as to
  each and holds the inquiry is cumulative.

## Context

The other half of the derivative-litigation apparatus. [[United Food (Tri-State) Pension Fund v. Zuckerberg|Zuckerberg]] governs whether a stockholder may sue **without** demand; Zapata
and Oracle govern what happens when the corporation tries to take the suit **back** through a
special litigation committee.

The casebook's framing note poses the structural problem plainly: the directors being sued are
the ones who appoint the SLC, "[b]ut who else could appoint them?" **Zapata's two-step** is
Delaware's answer — (1) the SLC must prove independence, good faith and reasonableness, and if
it fails the motion is denied; (2) if it succeeds, the court "appl[ies] its own independent
business judgment," so that "instances could arise where a committee can establish its
independence and sound bases for its good faith decision and still have the corporation's
motion denied."

That second step is genuinely anomalous, and the casebook flags it: a court exercising its own
business judgment sits awkwardly beside everything in
[[Business judgment rule - scope of protection]]. **Not every state accepts it.** New York, in
*Auerbach v. Bennett*, reviews an independent committee's **procedures and methodologies**
while protecting its substantive decision under the business judgment rule.

On independence generally, Oracle belongs with [[Marchand v. Barnhill]] and Zuckerberg as the
three assigned data points. Strine's formulation — "Delaware law should not be based on a
reductionist view of human nature" — is quoted in Marchand, and the Supreme Court commented on
Oracle in *Beam*, the source of the rule that personal friendship alone is insufficient.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Motion by Oracle's special litigation committee to **terminate** the derivative action, under
  *Zapata v. Maldonado*.
- **Posture inverts the usual burden.** The moving party is the corporation, and it carries the
  burden of persuasion — the opposite of a Rule 12 motion. The court need find only a material
  issue of fact about independence to deny.
- Chancery opinion; **never reviewed by the Delaware Supreme Court**, though commented on in
  *Beam*.

### Facts

The underlying allegation was ordinary enough: four Oracle directors, including Ellison and
Henley, sold stock in January 2001 while allegedly knowing the company would miss the earnings
guidance it had given for the third quarter of fiscal 2001.

What makes the case is the committee Oracle assembled to investigate it. Two Stanford
professors were recruited onto the Oracle board in the summer of 2001 and then asked to decide
whether to accuse fellow directors of insider trading. Those fellow directors included Boskin,
himself a Stanford professor who had taught one of them, and Lucas, a Stanford donor. And
Ellison — the company's founder, CEO and largest stockholder — was at that time weighing a very
large gift to Stanford.

None of this involved payments to the SLC members, business dealings with the defendants, or
anything resembling a financial stake. It was a dense set of institutional and social
affiliations running through a single university.

### Issue

- Whether a special litigation committee has carried its Zapata burden of establishing
  independence, where its members share extensive institutional and social affiliations with
  the defendants but no financial interest.

### Holding

- **Burden not met; motion denied.** The accumulated Stanford connections raise a reasonable
  doubt that material considerations other than Oracle's best interests could have influenced
  the committee's judgment.
- **The standard is situational, not moral.** The members' integrity is assumed; the holding is
  that they were not positioned to act with the required impartiality.
- The correct inquiry is aggregate rather than connection-by-connection.

### Rule

- An SLC may move to terminate a derivative action.
- **Zapata step one**: the corporation bears the burden of proving the committee's
  independence, good faith, and the reasonableness of its investigation. Failure means denial.
- **Zapata step two**: if the burden is met, the court applies **its own independent business
  judgment** to the motion, and may deny it even where the committee was independent and acted
  in good faith.
- The SLC must show there is no material issue of fact calling its independence into doubt.
- Independence is judged on all the facts together, asking whether material considerations
  other than the corporation's best interests could have influenced the committee.
- A finding of non-independence is not a finding of bad faith.

### Reasoning

*(Strine, V.C.)*

- The opinion's method is to refuse atomisation. Each tie is conceded to be weak; the holding
  is that the question was never about any single tie.
- The reasoning rests on a candid account of how people actually behave — that "persons of
  integrity and reputation can be compromised in their ability to act without bias when they
  must make a decision adverse to others with whom they share material affiliations."
- The purpose of the independence requirement is stated functionally: stockholders should not
  have to depend on committee members who must suppress ordinary human considerations to
  accuse colleagues of serious wrongdoing.
- The separation of situation from character does real work. It lets the court disqualify
  reputable people without impugning them, which is what makes the standard administrable.
- The closing sentence is the practical lesson and reads as advice to counsel: a company
  choosing an SLC controls its composition entirely, and Oracle chose badly.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None.
- The genuine objection is the one the casebook raises about Zapata itself rather than about
  this application: step two has a court substituting its own business judgment for a
  committee's, which is precisely what the business judgment rule elsewhere forbids. New York's
  *Auerbach* rejects it, confining review to procedure and methodology. Delaware's answer is
  structural — the SLC is appointed by the very directors being sued, so ordinary deference
  would let defendants pick their own judges.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect to be asked who Oracle should have appointed**, because the casebook asks it
  directly before the case. Directors with no Stanford affiliation, no prior relationship with
  any defendant, and preferably recruited before the litigation arose — which is the practical
  problem, since a company rarely has such directors lying spare.
- **Do not argue the SLC members were biased or dishonest.** The court goes out of its way to
  say they were not. The word is **situated**.
- **Have both Zapata steps.** Most answers give step one and stop. Step two — the court's own
  independent business judgment — is the doctrinally interesting half and the part that
  distinguishes Delaware from New York.
- **Know the aggregate point.** "Taken in isolation, the facts about Ellison might well not be
  enough... But that is not the relevant inquiry."
- **Distinguish this from Zuckerberg on independence.** Different postures and different burdens:
  here the **corporation** must prove independence; there the **plaintiff** must plead facts
  raising reasonable doubt. Same word, opposite allocation.
- **The quotable line:** "a social atmosphere painted in too much vivid Stanford Cardinal red."
