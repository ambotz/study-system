## Rule

Once the break-up of the company is inevitable and the board has recognised that
the company is for sale, the board's duty shifts from preserving the corporate
entity to **maximising the price stockholders receive**, and a defensive measure
that ends an active auction on an insubstantial basis cannot survive Unocal
scrutiny.

## Facts

- Pantry Pride's bids climbed from $47.50 to $50 to $53 to $56.25 to $58, each
  in cash for any and all shares. The escalation is what made the break-up of
  Revlon inevitable and moved the case out of ordinary defensive territory.
- On September 24 the Revlon board authorised management to negotiate a merger
  or buyout with other parties. That authorisation was the board's own
  recognition that Revlon was for sale, and it is the fact that triggers the
  change in duty.
- Revlon's exchange offer had issued Notes whose covenants limited additional
  debt, asset sales and dividends unless the independent directors waived them.
  Announcement of the Forstmann merger and the impending waiver dropped the
  Notes from roughly par to 87.50, producing a "deluge" of calls from irate
  noteholders and reported threats of litigation.
- The October 12 lock-up option let Forstmann buy the Vision Care and National
  Health Laboratories divisions for $525 million, **$100–$175 million below** the
  value Lazard Freres ascribed to those divisions, exercisable if any other
  acquiror obtained 40% of Revlon's shares. The below-value price is what made
  the option auction-ending rather than auction-inducing.
- Forstmann had twice been given access to Revlon financial data that Pantry
  Pride was denied, along with management cooperation and exclusive access to the
  board. The two bidders were not negotiating on equal terms.
- Forstmann's $57.25 exceeded Pantry Pride's $56.25 by one dollar on its face,
  and by less than that once discounted for the delay to closing. The auction was
  ended in exchange for very little actual improvement in price.
- In return for the lock-up, Forstmann agreed to support the Notes at par by an
  exchange of new notes. The directors thereby extinguished their own exposure to
  a class of creditors, which the court identifies as the principal benefit the
  transaction produced.
- The Rights Plan was adopted against a $45 bid that Lazard Freres advised was
  grossly inadequate, and the Plan drove the bidding from $42 to an eventual $58.
  *Argued and upheld — valid when adopted, and mooted by the October 3 and
  October 12 redemption resolutions.*
- The self-tender exchange offer for 10 million shares was adopted against a
  $47.50 bid the board reasonably concluded was grossly inadequate. *Argued and
  upheld under Unocal.*
- The noteholders' rights were fixed by contract, and the Notes covenants
  expressly contemplated a waiver to permit a sale of the company at a fair
  price. *Argued and rejected — the holders accepted that market risk when they
  took the Notes, so nothing remained for Revlon to protect.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Adoption of the Rights Plan was a valid defensive measure. The board acted in
  good faith and on reasonable investigation against a price it reasonably found
  grossly inadequate, and far from being a showstopper the Plan spurred the
  bidding to new heights. The Plan's continued usefulness was mooted once the
  board resolved to redeem the Rights in connection with any cash proposal at
  $57.25 or more.
- The company's own exchange offer for 10 million shares was likewise a proper
  Unocal response to a grossly inadequate bid, supported by the board's specific
  authority to deal in its own stock under 8 Del. C. § 160(a).
- When Pantry Pride raised its bid to $50 and then $53, the break-up of Revlon
  became inevitable. The board's authorisation to negotiate with third parties
  was a recognition that the company was for sale, and the duty accordingly
  changed from preservation of Revlon as a corporate entity to maximisation of
  the company's value at a sale. "The directors' role changed from defenders of
  the corporate bastion to **auctioneers** charged with getting the best price for
  the stockholders at a sale of the company."
- Making support of the Notes an integral part of the Forstmann deal was
  inconsistent with that duty. A board may have regard for non-stockholder
  constituencies only where **rationally related benefits accrue to the
  stockholders**, and such concern is inappropriate once an auction among active
  bidders is in progress.
- A lock-up is not per se illegal. An option that entices a new bidder into the
  contest creates an auction and benefits stockholders; an option that ends an
  active auction and forecloses further bidding harms them. Forstmann had already
  been drawn in on a preferred basis, so the lock-up could only destroy bidding.
- Where a board ends an intense bidding contest on an insubstantial basis and a
  significant byproduct of that action is protecting the directors against
  perceived personal liability, the action cannot withstand Unocal's enhanced
  scrutiny.
- The no-shop provision, also not per se illegal, is impermissible once the
  board's primary duty is that of an auctioneer, because an agreement to
  negotiate only with Forstmann ended rather than intensified the bidding.
- Enjoining the $25 million cancellation fee as part of the same plan was not an
  abuse of discretion.
- Irreparable harm was shown, because without an injunction Pantry Pride's
  opportunity to bid was lost and the complexity of the Forstmann transaction put
  a meaningful legal remedy out of reach. Affirmed.

**Rejected**

- *Forstmann's financing was firmer, which justified preferring Forstmann.* Any
  distinction between the two bidders' financing was nominal at best, and the
  method of financing carries little significance in a cash offer for any and all
  shares. About $400 million of Forstmann's funding still rested on a
  best-efforts syndication, and Drexel had a firm commitment for Pantry Pride's
  balance by October 18.
- *Forstmann's $57.25 was simply the higher bid.* The margin of superiority
  shrinks once the Forstmann price is adjusted for the time value of money, so
  the board ended the auction in return for very little real improvement.
- *Unocal permits a board to consider constituencies other than stockholders, and
  the noteholders were such a constituency.* The prerogative is real but limited
  by the requirement of a rationally related benefit to stockholders, and it
  falls away entirely once the object is no longer to maintain the enterprise but
  to sell the enterprise to the highest bidder.
- *Revlon owed the noteholders contractual and good-faith obligations.* Any such
  duty extends only to the principle that one may not interfere with contractual
  relationships by improper action. Nothing in the Notes' terms was violated, the
  covenants specifically contemplated waiver to permit a sale at a fair price,
  and the holders accepted the Notes on that basis including the risk of an
  adverse market effect.
- *Favouritism toward a white knight is permissible in a takeover fight.*
  Favouritism may be justifiable where the hostile bid adversely affects
  stockholder interests, but not where the rival bids are relatively similar or
  the dissolution of the company has become inevitable. Market forces must then
  be allowed to operate freely to bring stockholders the best price available.

## Context

Revlon is assigned twice. Session 14 reads Revlon as an application problem
alongside [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] and [[In re Trulia]], before the
takeover doctrine has been laid down; session 18 reads Revlon again in sequence
with [[Cheff v. Mathes]], [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] and
[[Moran v. Household International]]. The first pass is about what the rule does;
the second is about where the rule came from.

Revlon is a **Unocal case**, not a separate standard bolted onto Unocal. The
opinion applies Unocal's enhanced scrutiny throughout and holds that the Rights
Plan and the self-tender passed it while the lock-up, no-shop and cancellation
fee failed it. What changes at the moment of sale is the content of the second
Unocal prong: with the enterprise being sold, the only cognisable corporate
objective left is price, so a measure defended as protecting something other than
price is by definition disproportionate.

The trigger is the hard part, and Delaware spent the following decade narrowing
it. [[Paramount v. Time]], excerpted immediately after this case, holds that
entering a stock-for-stock merger with Warner did **not** put Time up for sale
even with an unequal share exchange, so no Revlon duty arose and the original
merger plan drew only business judgment review.
[[Paramount v. QVC]] then holds the opposite where the
transaction hands a controlling stockholder the company, because the public
stockholders permanently lose the control premium. The working line is **cash-out
or change of control triggers Revlon; stock-for-stock among dispersed holders
does not**.

Two things Revlon does not hold, both of which are commonly misstated. Revlon
does not require an auction — [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] records the
Chancery's premise that Revlon attaches when a company "initiates an active
bidding process seeking to sell itself," and later cases make clear that the
obligation is reasonableness in pursuit of the best value reasonably available,
not any single blueprint. Revlon also does not create a freestanding cause of
action for damages: [[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]] explains that Unocal and
Revlon "are primarily designed to give stockholders and the Court of Chancery the
tool of **injunctive relief**... in real time, before closing," which is why a
fully informed stockholder vote washes out a post-closing Revlon claim.

The opinion's own doctrinal labels are unstable and worth noticing rather than
smoothing over. The Court of Chancery found a breach of the **duty of loyalty**;
the Supreme Court says the directors "breached their primary duty of loyalty,"
then concludes two pages later that no defensive measure survives "when it
represents a breach of the directors' fundamental duty of **care**." The
substance is a conflict — directors buying off their own noteholder exposure with
stockholder money — and the care language is a vestige of the Chancery's
alternative framing.

[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] is the immediate ancestor. Van Gorkom is a sale of
control decided before either Unocal or Revlon existed, and the informational
failure Van Gorkom describes is the raw material these standards operationalise.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Expedited interlocutory appeal from a preliminary injunction issued by the
  Court of Chancery against the lock-up option, the no-shop provision and the
  $25 million cancellation fee.
- The nominal plaintiff, MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, is Pantry Pride's
  controlling stockholder; the court treats the two as one.
- Posture matters here. This is a **pre-closing injunction on a preliminary
  record**, which is the only setting in which Revlon does its real work, and the
  reason the remedy discussion at the end of the opinion is not an afterthought.

### Facts

Ronald Perelman, chairman and chief executive of Pantry Pride, opened in June
1985 with a friendly overture to Michel Bergerac at Revlon in the $40–50 range.
Bergerac dismissed the figures as far below Revlon's intrinsic value and rebuffed
everything that followed, in part out of a strong personal antipathy to Perelman.
By August 14 Pantry Pride's board had authorised Perelman to acquire Revlon
either by negotiation at $42–43 or by hostile tender offer at $45. Bergerac
conditioned any further talks on a standstill agreement.

Revlon's board met specially on August 19. Felix Rohatyn and William Loomis of
Lazard Freres told the directors that $45 was grossly inadequate and that Pantry
Pride's plan was a junk-bond-financed bust-up that, properly timed, could return
$60 to $70 per share to Pantry Pride, while a sale of the whole company would
land in the mid-fifties. Martin Lipton, Revlon's special counsel, recommended a
repurchase of up to 5 million of the roughly 30 million outstanding shares and a
Note Purchase Rights Plan, under which each share carried a right to exchange a
common share for a $65 one-year note at 12%, triggered at 20% beneficial
ownership, unavailable to the acquiror, and redeemable by the board at ten cents
before the trigger. Both measures passed unanimously.

Pantry Pride went hostile on August 23 at $47.50 per common share, conditioned on
financing and on the Rights being neutralised. Revlon responded on August 29 with
its own exchange offer, swapping for each share tendered a $47.50 Senior
Subordinated Note at 11.75% due 1995 plus a tenth of a share of $9.00 cumulative
convertible preferred valued at $100. Stockholders tendered 87% of the
outstanding shares and Revlon took 10 million pro rata. The Notes carried the
covenants that later become the centre of the case.

With the Rights and the Note covenants both in the way, Pantry Pride bid $42 on
September 16 conditioned on 90%, indicating willingness to pay more for less if
the Rights came down; Lazard called the two bids essentially equal given the
completed exchange offer. The board rejected that bid on September 24 and opened
negotiations with others. Pantry Pride then bid $50 on September 27, $53 on
October 1 and $56.25 on October 7.

Forstmann Little and Adler & Shaykin both came forward, and on October 3 the
board unanimously agreed to a Forstmann leveraged buyout at $56 cash: management
would roll golden parachutes into equity of the new company, Forstmann would
assume the $475 million of Note debt, and Revlon would redeem the Rights and
waive the covenants for Forstmann or for any superior offer. Forstmann planned to
sell Norcliff Thayer and Reheis to American Home Products for $335 million, and
Revlon was to sell cosmetics and fragrance to Adler & Shaykin for $905 million
before the merger.

The covenant waiver sank the Notes, the noteholders threatened suit, and on
October 12 Forstmann returned with $57.25 conditioned on the lock-up, the
no-shop, the cancellation fee, removal of the Rights and covenants, no management
participation, and immediate acceptance — in exchange for supporting the Notes at
par. The board approved unanimously, citing better financing, noteholder
protection and the higher price. Pantry Pride, which had sued over the Rights
Plan back on August 22, amended its complaint on October 14 and raised its bid to
$58 on October 22. Chancery blocked further asset transfers on October 15 and
enjoined the lock-up, no-shop and cancellation fee eight days later.

### Issue

- Whether the Rights Plan and the self-tender exchange offer were valid defensive
  measures under Unocal when adopted.
- Whether, and at what moment, the board's duty changed from defending the
  corporate enterprise to obtaining the best price for stockholders.
- Whether a board pursuing the best price may grant an auction-ending lock-up and
  no-shop in order to protect noteholders and, with them, the directors' own
  exposure.

### Holding

- **On the early defensive measures: valid.** The Rights Plan and the exchange
  offer were good-faith, reasonably investigated, proportionate responses to a
  bid the board justifiably found grossly inadequate, and the Rights Plan was in
  any event mooted by the board's own redemption resolutions.
- **On the trigger: the duty changed when the break-up became inevitable.** By
  the $50 and $53 bids the sale of Revlon was a certainty the board had itself
  embraced, and the authorisation to negotiate with third parties confirmed it.
  From that point the board's obligation was to maximise the sale price.
- **On the lock-up, no-shop and cancellation fee: invalid as applied.** The
  measures ended an active auction for a nominal improvement in price, rested on
  the impermissible objective of protecting noteholders whose rights were already
  fixed by contract, and incidentally shielded the directors from personal
  liability. Injunction affirmed.

### Rule

- When a board implements anti-takeover measures there arises "the omnipresent
  specter that a board may be acting primarily in its own interests," which
  places on the directors the burden of showing reasonable grounds to believe a
  danger to corporate policy and effectiveness existed.
- That burden is satisfied by a showing of good faith and reasonable
  investigation, and the response must be reasonable in relation to the threat
  posed.
- Once the break-up of the company is inevitable, the duty of the board changes
  from preservation of the corporate entity to maximisation of the company's
  value at a sale for the stockholders' benefit.
- At that point defensive measures become moot as a category, because there is no
  longer any threat to corporate policy to defend against.
- A board may consider non-stockholder constituencies only where rationally
  related benefits accrue to stockholders, and never once an auction among active
  bidders is underway.
- Lock-ups and no-shops are not per se illegal. They are permissible when they
  draw a bidder into a contest and impermissible when they end one.

### Reasoning

*(Moore, J.)*

- The business judgment rule's presumption does not attach automatically to
  defensive action, because the board's own tenure is at stake, so Unocal puts
  the initial burden on the directors.
- The Rights Plan cleared that burden on the facts of its adoption, and the court
  measures the Plan by what the Plan actually did: it raised the price from $42
  to $58, which is the proper result of a pill rather than an abuse of one.
- The inevitability of break-up is treated as an objective fact the board had
  accepted, not as a legal election the board made. That framing matters, because
  it means a board cannot avoid Revlon by declining to announce an auction.
- Once price is the only objective, the noteholder rationale collapses on its own
  terms. The noteholders held contract rights that contemplated exactly the
  waiver that occurred, so protecting them produced no benefit to stockholders
  and therefore no rationally related benefit at all.
- The court then locates the real interest served: the directors avoided personal
  liability to a class of creditors to whom they owed no further duty. That is the
  sentence doing the work in the holding, and it is why the case is in substance
  a loyalty case.
- On the lock-up specifically, the court distinguishes by effect rather than by
  form. The same instrument is lawful when it brings a bidder in and unlawful
  when it shuts an auction down, which is why "lock-ups are not per se illegal"
  and the Forstmann lock-up was still enjoined.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

### Cold-call notes

- **The trigger is the question, not the remedy.** Expect to be asked when Revlon
  duties attached. The answer is the moment the break-up became inevitable, which
  the court locates at the $50 and $53 bids and confirms by the September 24
  authorisation to negotiate with third parties. Do not answer "when the board
  signed with Forstmann" — by then the duty had been in force for weeks.
- **Do not say Revlon requires an auction.** The court says the directors became
  auctioneers on these facts, where an auction already existed and the lock-up
  killed it. The obligation is to obtain the best value reasonably available, and
  Paramount v. Time and later cases make the no-single-blueprint point explicit.
- **Be ready on care versus loyalty.** The opinion uses both labels and the
  conclusion says "duty of care" while the analysis describes a conflict. If
  pushed, say the substance is loyalty — directors spending stockholder value to
  extinguish their own exposure — and note that the care label is inherited from
  the Chancery's framing. This is also why § 102(b)(7) would not have saved these
  directors and why the remedy sought was an injunction rather than damages.
- **Know why the Rights Plan survived.** A common error is to read Revlon as
  hostile to pills. The pill is upheld, and the court praises the pill for having
  raised the price by $16 a share. What failed was the lock-up granted after the
  company was already for sale.
- **Have the constituency limit precise.** Other constituencies may be considered
  where there are rationally related benefits accruing to stockholders, and not
  at all once an auction among active bidders is in progress. Buccola may push on
  whether this is a real limit or a nullity; the honest answer is that the
  rationally-related-benefit qualifier does most of the work and almost never
  favours the non-stockholder group.
- **The noteholder point is the one students miss.** The Notes covenants
  contemplated waiver for a sale at a fair price, so the noteholders were not
  wronged and there was nothing left to protect. Without that fact the board's
  rationale is at least coherent, which makes it a good place for a hypothetical.
- **Numbers worth holding.** $45 hostile threat, $47.50 first tender, $58 final
  Pantry Pride bid, $57.25 Forstmann, $525 million lock-up price against a
  $625–700 million valuation, $25 million cancellation fee, 40% trigger.
