## Rule

Where a board acts for the **primary purpose** of preventing or impeding the effective
exercise of the stockholder franchise, the business judgment rule does not apply **even if
the board acts in good faith and with due care**, and the board "bears the heavy burden of
demonstrating a **compelling justification** for such action."

## Facts

- Blasius delivered a written consent that would have **amended the bylaws to expand the
  board from seven to fifteen** — the charter maximum — **and elected eight new directors**,
  giving Blasius a board majority in a single action if holders of a majority joined.
- The board's response, **the very next day and by emergency telephone meeting**, was to
  expand from seven to nine and fill both seats. A regularly scheduled meeting was one week
  away. The speed and the vehicle are what mark the action as a response.
- Two new directors turned a one-step consent into a **two-election problem**: Blasius would
  now have to win twice to control the board.
- **The board's principal motivation was to preclude the shareholders from acting.** "[T]he
  members of the board realized that they were thereby precluding the holders of a majority
  of the Company's shares from placing a majority of new directors on the board... Indeed the
  evidence establishes that that was the **principal motivation** in so acting."
- **The board was not selfishly motivated.** "I cannot conclude that the board was acting out
  of a self-interested motive in any important respect," and it "acted... in a good faith
  effort to protect its incumbency, not selfishly, but in order to thwart implementation of
  the recapitalization that it feared, reasonably, would cause great injury to the Company."
  That finding is what makes the case hard and is the reason Schnell alone could not decide
  it.
- The new directors were genuinely qualified — Winters an expert in mining economics, Devaney
  a company financial expert — and their addition "strengthened the Atlas board." *Argued and
  rejected as the principal motive; the court accepts the appointments were sensible and
  prudent while finding they were made for a different reason.*
- Blasius held only **9.1%** and was making a **consent solicitation**, not a takeover bid.
  "The board was **not faced with a coercive action taken by a powerful shareholder** against
  the interests of a distinct shareholder constituency."
- The board **had time** — "and understood that it had time" — to inform the stockholders of
  its views on the merits before any vote. The availability of the legitimate alternative is
  what defeats the justification.
- Blasius's leveraged recapitalisation proposal was, in the Chancellor's own assessment, "not
  a sound proposal." *Assumed in the board's favour and still insufficient.*
- The **second case**, on the consent solicitation itself, found several errors by the judges
  of election but concluded that correcting them did not change the result: Blasius failed to
  win a majority.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Where the board acts "in good faith and with appropriate care, **for the primary purpose of
  preventing or impeding an unaffiliated majority of shareholders from expanding the board
  and electing a new majority**," the action "constituted an offense to the relationship
  between corporate directors and shareholders that has traditionally been protected in
  courts of equity." The December 31 action was invalid and is voided.
- If the board had been **selfishly** motivated — if "there was no policy dispute or issue
  that really motivated this action, but that asserted policy differences were pretexts for
  entrenchment for selfish reasons" — "one would not need to inquire further. The action taken
  would constitute a breach of duty." That is the Schnell branch, and it is not this case.
- If the board had acted "**completely independently** of the consent solicitation, which
  merely had an incidental impact upon the possible effectuation of any action authorized by
  the shareholders, it is very unlikely that such action would be subject to judicial
  nullification. The board, as a general matter, is **under no fiduciary obligation to suspend
  its active management** of the firm while the consent solicitation process goes forward."
- **Why the business judgment rule does not apply.** "The shareholder franchise is the
  **ideological underpinning** upon which the legitimacy of directorial power rests."
  Shareholders have only two protections against inadequate performance — sell, or vote to
  replace the board — and whether the vote is "an unimportant formalism, or... an important
  tool of discipline, it is clear that it is critical to the theory that legitimates the
  exercise of power by some... over vast aggregations of property that they do not own."
- The second reason is **allocation of authority**. A decision to act for the primary purpose
  of preventing the effectiveness of a stockholder vote "inevitably involves the question who,
  as between the principal and the agent, has authority with respect to a matter of internal
  corporate governance." It "does not involve the exercise of the corporation's power over its
  property"; it involves "allocation, between shareholders as a class and the board, of
  effective power with respect to governance." That "is not... a question that a court may
  leave to the agent finally to decide so long as he does so honestly and competently."
- **No per se rule.** A rule of automatic invalidity would have "clarity and predictability"
  and would most vigorously enforce corporate democracy, but "it may sweep too broadly." The
  court's "inability to foresee now all of the future settings in which a board might, in good
  faith, paternalistically seek to thwart a shareholder vote, counsels against" it. "It may be
  that some set of facts would justify such extreme action. This, however, is not such a case."
- **The compelling justification is missing here.** The board faced a 9% holder's consent
  solicitation, not coercion of a distinct constituency, and it had time to make its case.
  "The only justification that can, in such a situation, be offered for the action taken is
  **that the board knows better than do the shareholders** what is in the corporation's best
  interest. While that premise is no doubt true for any number of matters, it is **irrelevant**
  ... when the question is who should comprise the board of directors."
- "The theory of our corporation law confers power upon directors as the **agents** of the
  shareholders; it does not create **Platonic masters**."
- **Informing is not foreclosing.** The board could "expend corporate funds to inform
  shareholders and seek to bring them to a similar point of view. But there is a **vast
  difference between expending corporate funds to inform the electorate and exercising power
  for the primary purpose of foreclosing effective shareholder action**."
- The action, "even finding [it] was taken in good faith,... constituted an **unintended
  violation of the duty of loyalty** that the board owed to the shareholders." Set aside.
- **On the second case:** the judges of election properly confined their count to the written
  ballots before them and made several errors, but correcting those errors does not reverse
  the announced result. The consent solicitation failed to garner a majority.

**Rejected**

- *The December 31 action was a selfishly motivated effort to protect the incumbent board from
  a perceived threat to its control.* The evidence that the board was chiefly motivated to
  forestall the consent solicitation is "powerful, indeed compelling," but the more subtle
  question — whether the directors did so out of a good faith belief that shareholder action
  would be self-injurious — resolves in the board's favour. "On balance, I cannot conclude that
  the board was acting out of a self-interested motive in any important respect."
- *The board acted without a conflicting interest, with due care and in good faith, and so is
  entitled to the business judgment rule.* All three premises are largely accepted and none
  answers the question. The issue "is not one of intentional wrong (or even negligence), but
  one of **authority** as between the fiduciary and the beneficiary."
- *The principal motive was to strengthen the board by adding a mining economist and a
  financial expert, and the December 31 action merely implemented a pre-existing expansion
  plan.* The additions "would be found to be sensible and prudent" and "would, under other
  circumstances, be clearly appropriate as an independent step," but the court "cannot
  conclude... that the strengthening of the board by the addition of these men was the
  principal motive."
- *Even if some substantive review applies, the action was fair, measured and appropriate in
  light of the circumstances, and survives fairness review or Unocal.* Against a 9% holder's
  consent solicitation, with time available to inform stockholders, the only justification left
  is that the board knows better — which is no justification on the question of who should sit
  on the board.
- *Unocal supplies the governing standard, because a board may take steps that defeat a
  threatened change in control when taken advisedly, in good faith and reasonably in relation
  to a threat.* That rule does not extend to action designed for the primary purpose of
  interfering with a vote. "[T]he central importance of the franchise to the scheme of
  corporate governance, requires that, in this setting, that rule **not** be applied and that
  **closer scrutiny** be accorded."

## Context

Blasius takes the question [[Schnell v. Chris-Craft Industries]] leaves open. Schnell condemns
a board that manipulates the corporate machinery **for selfish reasons**; Blasius asks what
happens when the board is sincere and still forecloses the vote. Chancellor Allen's answer —
good faith is not enough, and the board must show a compelling justification — rests on two
grounds worth keeping separate. The **legitimacy** ground is that directorial power over
property the directors do not own is justified by the franchise. The **allocation** ground is
narrower and more lawyerly: this kind of decision is not about the corporation's property at
all, it is about who decides, and an agent cannot be the final judge of the scope of his own
authority against his principal.

The framework sits uneasily beside [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]], decided three years earlier,
and Delaware spent thirty-five years working out the relationship. **Stroud v. Grace** first
recognised that a proxy fight coupled with a tender offer invokes both. **MM Companies v.
Liquid Audio** — a board expanding from five to seven to defeat an insurgent, the same move as
here — formally placed Blasius "within Unocal," requiring a compelling justification "as a
condition precedent to any judicial consideration of reasonableness and proportionality."
**Chesapeake v. Shore** proposed merging them outright, applying Unocal "with a gimlet eye out
for inequitably motivated electoral manipulation." **Mercier v. Inter-Tel** abandoned the
compelling-justification label for a modified Unocal review. And **Pell v. Kill** kept the
label while explaining that "the shift from 'reasonable' to 'compelling' requires that the
directors establish a **closer fit between means and ends**."

[[Coster v. UIP Companies]] completes the arc in 2023, and is blunt about why: "[t]he Blasius
'compelling justification' standard of review turned out to be **unworkable in practice**. Once
the court required a compelling justification to justify the board's action, the outcome was,
for the most part, **preordained**." Courts avoided that result by narrowing "primary purpose"
or redefining "compelling." Coster therefore folds both Schnell and Blasius into Unocal-shaped
enhanced scrutiny — a real, non-pretextual threat with proper and non-selfish motivations, and
a response reasonable in relation to the threat and neither preclusive nor coercive.

**What survives is the substance rather than the name.** Coster preserves Blasius's core
holdings inside the new test: the board bears the burden; the threat cannot be pretextual; and
"the threat **cannot be justified on the grounds that the board knows what is in the best
interests of the stockholders**." That last sentence is Blasius, quoted in 2023 as still good
law. Coster also confines the heightened inquiry to its proper domain, echoing Mercier's view
that a more muscular Unocal analysis should not apply outside election interference and control
contests, where "more traditional tools are available."

The safe harbour in the opinion is as important as the rule. A board is "under no fiduciary
obligation to suspend its active management of the firm while the consent solicitation process
goes forward," and action taken independently with merely incidental effect is very unlikely to
be nullified. Coster restates it as the clear-day principle: "[i]f the Atlas board had acted on
a clear day to establish new seats and to fill the vacancies, the circumstances would have been
different. But for the Atlas board, the skies were cloudy, and it was raining."

Note finally what makes the remedy work. The consent solicitation ran under **8 Del. C.
§ 228(a)**, which lets stockholders act "without a meeting, without prior notice and without a
vote" on consents signed by holders of the minimum number of votes needed at a meeting. That
mechanism is what allowed Blasius to expand the board and elect eight directors in a single
step — and it is why adding two seats overnight was decisive rather than merely inconvenient.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Two consolidated cases tried together, both about who is entitled to sit on the Atlas board.
- The **first** challenges the validity of board action at a telephone meeting on December 31,
  1987 adding two directors. The **second** arises from the consent solicitation itself and
  asks the court to determine its outcome.
- Findings are made on a preponderance of the admissible evidence after trial.
- The posture is what gives the opinion its unusual shape. Because the Chancellor makes
  findings about **motivation** on a full record, he can hold both that the board acted
  sincerely and that its principal purpose was to preclude the stockholders — a combination a
  pleading-stage ruling could not produce, and the combination that forces the new standard.

### Facts

Blasius Industries began buying Atlas stock in July 1987 and filed a Schedule 13D on October 29
disclosing that with affiliates it owned 9.1% of the common stock. The filing said Blasius
intended to encourage management to consider a restructuring or other value-enhancing
transaction, and that it was exploring the feasibility of obtaining control, including a tender
offer or "appropriate" board representation.

Blasius had recently come under the control of Michael Lubin and Warren Delano, who after
experience in commercial banking had for a short time run a venture capital operation for a
small investment banking firm. Atlas management did not welcome their involvement. Atlas had a
new chief executive, Weaver, who had overseen a restructuring: three of five divisions sold, the
once-important domestic uranium operation being closed, the company refocused on gold mining. By
October 1987 the structural changes were largely complete.

Immediately after the 13D, Lubin and Delano met Atlas management and proposed a leveraged
recapitalisation — sell assets, borrow significantly, make a large one-time cash distribution,
leaving shareholders with cash and equity in a smaller, more highly leveraged company.
Management's immediate internal reaction was that the proposal was infeasible. Lubin sent a
written proposal on December 7; it went to the board on December 9 and Goldman Sachs was
directed to review it. Blasius tried on December 14 and December 22 to arrange further meetings
without success.

On December 30 Blasius delivered a signed written consent doing three things: adopting a
precatory resolution recommending a restructuring, amending the bylaws to expand the board from
seven to fifteen — the charter maximum — and electing eight named persons to the new seats. It
also sued that day, seeking a declaration that bylaws the board had adopted on September 1 were
an unlawful restraint on the stockholders' right to act by consent.

The reaction was immediate. Weaver conferred with Masinter, the company's outside counsel and a
director, who viewed the consent as an attempt to take control. They called an emergency board
meeting even though a regular meeting was a week away, on January 6. The purpose was to act on
their conclusion "that we should add at least one and probably two directors to the board." At a
telephone meeting the next day, December 31, the board amended the bylaws to expand from seven
to nine and appointed John Devaney and Harry Winters to the new positions.

There was testimony that the board was implementing an expansion plan predating Blasius's
emergence. The Chancellor found the two men strengthened the board and that their selection
would be judged sensible and prudent, but concluded the strengthening was not the principal
motive. The principal motive was to impede or preclude a majority of the shareholders from
adopting the course Blasius proposed.

On January 6 the board convened for its scheduled meeting, heard a full report from its financial
advisor on the feasibility of the Blasius proposal, and voted to reject it.

### Issue

- Whether a board acts consistently with its fiduciary duty when it acts, in good faith and with
  appropriate care, for the primary purpose of preventing an unaffiliated majority of
  stockholders from expanding the board and electing a new majority.
- Whether the business judgment rule applies to board action taken for the primary purpose of
  interfering with a stockholder vote.
- If not, whether such action is per se invalid or subject to an intermediate standard.
- Whether the Atlas board demonstrated sufficient justification on these facts.

### Holding

- **On motivation: principally to preclude, but not selfishly.** The evidence that the board
  acted chiefly to forestall the consent solicitation is compelling; the evidence that it acted
  from self-interest is not. The board genuinely feared great injury to the company.
- **On the standard: the business judgment rule does not apply.** Where the primary purpose is to
  interfere with a stockholder vote, the question is one of authority between principal and
  agent, not of care or intentional wrong, and closer scrutiny is required.
- **On the rule adopted: not per se invalidity.** A per se rule may sweep too broadly given
  unforeseeable future settings; the board instead bears the heavy burden of demonstrating a
  compelling justification.
- **On this record: no justification.** Against a 9% holder's consent solicitation, with no
  coercion of a distinct constituency and with time to inform the stockholders, the only
  remaining justification is that the board knows better — which is irrelevant to who should
  comprise the board.
- **Disposition:** the December 31 action is an unintended violation of the duty of loyalty and
  is set aside. Separately, the consent solicitation failed to obtain a majority.

### Rule

- A board is under no fiduciary obligation to suspend its active management of the firm while a
  consent solicitation goes forward, and action taken independently with merely incidental effect
  on a stockholder vote is very unlikely to be nullified.
- Board action taken for the primary purpose of interfering with the effectiveness of a
  stockholder vote is not protected by the business judgment rule, even if taken advisedly, with
  due care and in good faith.
- Such action presents a question of the allocation of authority between the board and the
  shareholders as a class, which may not be left to the agent's business judgment.
- There is no per se rule of invalidity; the board bears the heavy burden of demonstrating a
  compelling justification.
- That the board believes it knows better than the shareholders what is in the corporation's best
  interest is not a compelling justification when the question is who should comprise the board.
- A board may expend corporate funds to inform the electorate, but not to exercise power for the
  primary purpose of foreclosing effective shareholder action.
- Where policy differences are pretexts for entrenchment for selfish reasons, no further inquiry
  is needed and the action is a breach of duty.

### Reasoning

*(Allen, Ch.)*

- The Chancellor separates three questions that are easily merged: whether the board was
  **selfish** (Schnell's question), whether the board was **principally motivated** to block the
  vote, and whether, being sincere, it may nonetheless do so. He answers the first no, the second
  yes, and the third no — and the third is the case's contribution.
- The refusal to find self-interest is deliberate and generous. He calls the motivational question
  "more subtle," acknowledges that a good-faith finding "will typically, if not always, be a
  contestable or debatable judicial conclusion," and resolves it for the defendants — which makes
  the holding harder to reach and much more durable.
- The legitimacy argument is institutional rather than doctrinal. The franchise matters whether or
  not it is practically effective, because it is what licenses the exercise of power over property
  the directors do not own; so interference with it "involve[s] consideration not present in any
  other context in which directors exercise delegated power."
- The allocation argument is the lawyer's version of the same point, and it is the one that does
  the analytical work. Ordinary business judgment protects decisions about the corporation's
  **property**. A decision to block a vote is about **governance authority** as between principal
  and agent, and agency law does not let the agent settle the boundaries of his own authority.
- The rejection of a per se rule is an exercise in judicial humility, expressly resting on an
  inability to foresee every future setting — and the court reserves that "some set of facts would
  justify such extreme action."
- The dispositive distinction at the end is between **persuasion and preclusion**. The board had
  the time and the resources to argue its case to the stockholders; choosing instead to make their
  decision impossible is what converts disagreement into usurpation.
- Calling the result "an unintended violation of the **duty of loyalty**" is the opinion's most
  striking phrase. Loyalty ordinarily implies conflicted self-interest, and the Chancellor has just
  found none; he uses the label because the wrong is a taking of the beneficiary's authority, not a
  failure of care.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The standard announced proved harder to live with than the reasoning suggests, and Coster says so
directly: requiring a compelling justification made outcomes "for the most part, preordained," and
courts responded by narrowing "primary purpose" or diluting "compelling." There is also an
unresolved tension inside the opinion. The Chancellor accepts that a board may spend corporate
funds to persuade stockholders and may take independent action with incidental electoral effect,
but almost every meaningful defensive step in a control contest has some electoral effect — so
everything turns on the characterisation of "primary purpose," which the opinion treats as a
finding of fact rather than a test.

### Cold-call notes

- **State the holding with its hardest premise attached.** Good faith **and** due care **and** no
  self-interest, and the board still loses, because the primary purpose was to prevent the
  stockholders from electing a majority. Leaving out the good-faith finding makes the case sound
  like Schnell and misses the point.
- **Give both reasons the business judgment rule does not apply.** Legitimacy — the franchise is
  the ideological underpinning of directorial power. And allocation — this is a question of
  authority between principal and agent, not an exercise of power over corporate property.
- **Quote the two lines that do the most work.** "[I]t does not create Platonic masters," and the
  "vast difference between expending corporate funds to inform the electorate and exercising power
  for the primary purpose of foreclosing effective shareholder action."
- **Know the safe harbour.** No obligation to suspend active management during a consent
  solicitation; independent action with incidental effect is very unlikely to be nullified. Coster
  restates it as the clear-day principle.
- **Be ready on why "we know better" fails.** It may well be true on any number of matters, and it
  is irrelevant on the one question the stockholders are entitled to decide — who sits on the
  board.
- **Explain the mechanism.** Section 228(a) consents let a majority amend the bylaws, expand the
  board and elect eight directors in one action. That is why two extra seats, added overnight,
  converted a single step into two elections.
- **Trace the doctrine to today.** Stroud recognised both standards applied; Liquid Audio put
  Blasius "within Unocal"; Mercier and Pell reworked it; Coster folded Schnell and Blasius into
  Unocal-shaped enhanced scrutiny while keeping Blasius's substance — burden on the board, no
  pretextual threat, and "we know better" is still no justification.
- **Numbers worth holding.** 9.1% stake; seven seats expanded to nine overnight against a proposed
  fifteen with eight new directors; consent delivered December 30, board acted December 31, regular
  meeting due January 6; proposal formally rejected January 6.
