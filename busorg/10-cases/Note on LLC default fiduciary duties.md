## What it is

A two-page casebook note recounting how Delaware settled whether LLC managers owe **default**
fiduciary duties — the question [[Elf Atochem North America v. Jaffari]] does not answer. It
excerpts the Delaware Supreme Court's 2012 per curiam rebuke in **Gatz Properties, LLC v. Auriga
Capital Corp.** and then reports the **2013 legislative answer**, an amendment to 6 Del. C.
§ 18-1104.

The note matters less for the doctrine, which is now settled, than for what the episode shows
about **who decides** questions of Delaware entity law, and how quickly the General Assembly moves
when the courts leave a commercially important question open.

## What to notice

- **The state of play before 2013.** "Until 2013, it was not clear whether the default fiduciary
  duties for managers of an LLC were the same as the fiduciary duties for directors of a
  corporation. By 2013 the Chancellor and Vice Chancellors had made it clear that they believed the
  default duties were the same. The Delaware Supreme Court had not answered the question."
- **The rebuke.** In Gatz the Supreme Court held it "improvident and unnecessary for the trial court
  to reach out and decide, **sua sponte**, the default fiduciary duty issue as a matter of statutory
  construction," and declared that "court's statutory pronouncements **must be regarded as dictum
  without any precedential value**." The footnote explains why it bothered: "because it could be
  misinterpreted in future cases as a correct rule of law," when "the question remains open."
- **The stare decisis exchange, which is the sharpest part.** The Chancellor had suggested that "a
  judicial eradication of the explicit equity overlay in the LLC Act could tend to erode our state's
  credibility with investors in Delaware entities." The Supreme Court read that as hubris: "once a
  trial judge decides an issue, other trial judges on that court are entitled to rely on that
  decision as stare decisis. Needless to say, as an appellate tribunal and the **court of last
  resort** in this State, **we are not so constrained**."
- **The reprimand about judicial opinions**, which is quotable and unusual: "the obligation to write
  judicial opinions on the issues presented is **not a license to use those opinions as a platform
  from which to propagate their individual world views** on issues not presented." Judges wishing to
  ruminate have "appropriate platforms, such as law review articles, the classroom, continuing legal
  education presentations, and keynote speeches."
- **The casebook's own pushback**, which is the exam-relevant question. "If the Chancery court has
  repeatedly stated that there are default fiduciary duties, does it matter what the statute says?
  Does the fact that parties **did not address the issue in the LLC Agreement** allow us to infer a
  **contractual intent** regardless of the technically correct default rule?" That is a real point:
  in a contractarian regime, settled expectations are themselves evidence of what the parties meant.
- **The legislative answer, in the bolded words added in 2013.** Section 18-1104 now reads: "In any
  case not provided for in this chapter, the rules of law and equity, **including the rules of law
  and equity relating to fiduciary duties** and the law merchant, shall govern."
- **The amendment codifies the Chancellor's view, not the Supreme Court's caution.** The legislature
  answered the question the Supreme Court had refused to reach, and answered it the way Chancery had
  been answering it all along.
- **Members remain free to contract out.** "The parties still remain free to contract out of those
  duties." Section 18-1101(c) preserves one exception: an LLC agreement "may not eliminate the
  implied contractual covenant of good faith and fair dealing."
- **The question is not settled everywhere.** "The issue remains open in several other
  jurisdictions" — worth remembering, because Delaware is dominant in LLC law but less dominant than
  in corporate law, with "more competition nipping at its heels on the LLC front."

## Where it goes

- [[LLCs - freedom of contract, default duties and the implied covenant]] — the module this note
  anchors, and the place the § 18-1104 and § 18-1101(c) text lives.
- [[Elf Atochem North America v. Jaffari]] — establishes freedom of contract and the mandatory/default
  line, and leaves this question open.
- [[Miller v. HCP & Co]] — the practical consequence. Because default duties exist, they must be
  **explicitly waived**, and once waived the implied covenant is all that remains.
- The legislature-answers-the-court pattern running through the course, alongside Van Gorkom →
  § 102(b)(7), Moelis → § 122(18), MFW → § 144(c), and Yahoo! → § 220. This is the same move in the
  alternative entity statute, and the only one where the legislature resolves a question the Supreme
  Court had deliberately declined to reach.
