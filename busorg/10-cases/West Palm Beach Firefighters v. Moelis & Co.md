## Rule

An **internal governance arrangement** that does not appear in the charter and
deprives the board of a significant portion of its authority violates § 141(a).
Under the *Abercrombie* test, restrictions fail when they "have the effect of
removing from directors in a very substantial way their duty to use their own best
judgment on management matters" or "tend to limit in a substantial way the freedom
of director decisions on matters of management policy."

## Facts

- Ken Moelis founded, ran and chaired Moelis & Company. **One day before the shares
  began trading publicly**, Moelis, three affiliates, and the Company signed a
  Stockholder Agreement. *The timing and the parties are what mark it internal
  rather than commercial.*
- **Pre-Approval Requirements**: the Board must obtain Moelis' prior written consent
  before taking **eighteen categories** of action, which "encompass virtually
  everything the Board can do."
- **Board Composition Provisions**, six in all: a Size Requirement capping the Board
  at eleven; a Designation Right letting Moelis name a majority of seats; a
  Nomination Requirement; a Recommendation Requirement; an Efforts Requirement; and
  a Vacancy Requirement filling any departing designee's seat with another designee.
- **Committee Composition Provision**: every committee must carry Moelis designees
  in proportion to the full Board, so the Board cannot form an independent committee
  without his consent.
- **Moelis does not hold a majority of voting power.** The provisions force his
  designees onto the Board regardless. *Control here is contractual, not
  electoral — which is the whole problem.*
- The agreement is indefinite, is not tied to any underlying commercial transaction,
  **binds the Board rather than the Company**, and the Board cannot terminate it; it
  lapses only if Moelis triggers a condition.
- The Challenged Provisions "resemble the type of governance rights associated with
  **preferred stock**." *This observation supplies the court's own cure.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- § 141(a) is "the source of Delaware's board-centric model," and "director primacy
  remains the centerpiece of Delaware law, **even when a controlling stockholder is
  present**."
- The Challenged Provisions are an internal governance arrangement, not an external
  commercial contract, and are therefore subject to § 141(a).
- **Pre-Approval Requirements: invalid collectively.** "With the Pre-Approval
  Requirements in place, the Board is not really a board." Recasting them as
  eighteen vetoes changes nothing: "the power to review is the power to decide."
  The business and affairs are managed under Moelis' direction, not the Board's.
- **Three Board Composition Provisions invalid**: the Recommendation Requirement
  (compels the Board to endorse his candidates), the Vacancy Requirement (compels
  the Board to refill with his designees), and the Size Requirement (lets him block
  board expansion).
- **Three survive facial challenge**: the Designation Right (identifying candidates
  is not deciding anything), the Nomination Requirement (Moelis could nominate at a
  meeting anyway), and the Efforts Requirement (legitimate efforts include proxy
  card inclusion and disclosure). As-applied challenges remain available.
- **Committee Composition Provision: invalid** under both § 141(a) and § 141(c).
  Committee composition is board authority; a stockholder cannot determine it.
- **The cure is the charter.** Moelis "could have accomplished the vast majority of
  what he wanted through the Company's certificate of incorporation," and even now
  the Board could use its blank check authority to issue him preferred stock — "a
  single golden share" — carrying voting and appointment rights. The certificate of
  designations becomes part of the Charter as a matter of law, at which point the
  provisions comply with § 141(a). That the DGCL forbids one route and permits
  another "is what the doctrine of **independent legal significance** contemplates."

**Rejected**

- *A contract is a contract is a contract. Corporations can contract, contracts
  necessarily constrain boards, and no court can distinguish an internal governance
  arrangement from an external commercial one — so either all contracts fail under
  § 141(a) or none do.* The court calls this "a version of the **soritical
  paradox**," which draws no distinction between a grain of wheat and a heap. "The
  soritical paradox bedevils logicians, but not mere humans. We can recognize
  prototypes and draw distinctions by comparing and contrasting an example with a
  prototype. ... Cases regularly turn on whether the facts are more like one
  precedent or another. That's how legal reasoning works." On these facts the line
  is easy: "No one would mistake the Stockholder Agreement for a supply agreement,
  credit agreement, or some other external commercial contract."
- *Moelis holds only veto rights, so the Board retains its powers.* "That is not
  true. Moelis has **pre-approval** rights. The Board cannot approve, authorize, or
  even plan to take a covered action without Moelis' prior written approval." And
  reframing as vetoes would not help, because eighteen vetoes leave the Board
  proposing options for Moelis to approve — an inversion of the management team's
  role.
- *The facial challenge fails because the provisions operate validly whenever Moelis
  and the Board agree.* "But when Moelis and the Board agree, the provisions are not
  operating at all." Directors are always free to follow the CEO's advice; that is
  different from being contractually prevented from acting. "There is no setting
  where Moelis could invoke the provisions without triggering a Section 141(a)
  violation."

## Context

Read directly against [[Gorman v. Salamone]], assigned the same day. Gorman kills a
**bylaw** that moved a management function to stockholders; Moelis kills a
**stockholder agreement** that did the same by contract. Together they establish
the instrument hierarchy this session is about: the **charter** may vary board
authority because § 141(a) says so; **bylaws** reach process but not substance;
**stockholder agreements** cannot do internal governance at all.

The deeper move is the **internal/external** line. External commercial contracts
constrain boards constantly and lawfully — an exclusive supply agreement, a credit
agreement making a dividend an event of default. Laster's answer is prototype
reasoning rather than a bright-line rule, and the markers he uses are worth keeping
as a checklist: who the parties are, when it was signed relative to the offering,
whether it binds the Board or the Company, whether it is tied to an underlying
commercial transaction, whether it is indefinite, and whether the Board can
terminate it.

**The legislative sequel matters and the syllabus signals it.** § 122(18) is
assigned immediately after this case, which is the same pattern as
[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] followed by 8 Del. C. § 102(b)(7): Delaware courts invalidate
a widespread market practice, and the General Assembly responds by amending the
DGCL. *The text of § 122(18) is not in this vault — see the note in
[[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]].*

Note also that the case is a pure question of **power**, not of duty. Nobody alleges
Moelis breached a fiduciary duty or that the arrangement harmed anyone. The
provisions are void because the corporation lacked the power to grant them in that
instrument, which is why a facial challenge works at all.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Cross-motions for summary judgment in the Court of Chancery on a **facial**
  challenge to the Stockholder Agreement's provisions.
- The facial posture drives the analysis: the Company's principal argument is that
  the provisions can operate lawfully in some circumstances, which is exactly the
  argument a facial challenge must defeat. The court's answer — that the provisions
  are not "operating" when the parties agree — is a direct product of that framing.

### Facts

Moelis & Company is a global investment bank built by Ken Moelis as a private firm.
When he decided to raise public capital, he created a new holding company,
reorganised the underlying entities, and the day before trading began signed a
Stockholder Agreement among himself, three entities he controlled, and the Company.

The agreement did three things. It required his prior written consent before the
Board could take any of eighteen categories of action. It obliged the Board to keep
itself at no more than eleven seats, to let Moelis designate a majority of them, to
nominate and recommend those designees, to use reasonable efforts to get them
elected and keep them serving, and to refill their vacancies with more of his
designees. And it required every committee to mirror the full Board's proportion of
Moelis designees.

The practical effect was that Moelis controlled the Board without controlling the
vote. He held less than a majority of outstanding voting power. The Board could not
act without him, could not be enlarged past him, and could not form a committee
without him.

The plaintiff, a stockholder, challenged the provisions as facially invalid under
§ 141(a), and the Committee Composition Provision as also violating § 141(c).

### Issue

- Whether provisions of a stockholder agreement requiring board pre-approval by a
  stockholder, dictating board composition, and dictating committee composition are
  facially invalid under § 141(a).
- Whether a court can distinguish an internal governance arrangement from an
  external commercial contract for this purpose.
- Whether the Committee Composition Provision separately violates § 141(c).

### Holding

- **Pre-Approval Requirements: facially invalid**, taken collectively. They leave the
  Board able to act only with Moelis' permission, so the corporation is managed under
  his direction rather than the Board's. The court expressly does not decide whether
  some lesser combination would survive.
- **Size, Recommendation and Vacancy Requirements: facially invalid.** Each compels
  a specific board action and removes the directors' judgment.
- **Designation Right, Nomination Requirement and Efforts Requirement: not facially
  invalid.** Identifying candidates, facilitating a nomination Moelis could make
  himself, and assisting with proxy mechanics do not remove board judgment.
  As-applied challenges survive.
- **Committee Composition Provision: invalid** under § 141(a) and § 141(c).
- **The internal/external question: courts can draw the line**, by comparison to
  prototypes, and these provisions are nowhere near the commercial-contract end.

### Rule

- § 141(a): the business and affairs of every corporation shall be managed by or
  under the direction of a board of directors, **except as may be otherwise provided
  in this chapter or in its certificate of incorporation**.
- Internal governance arrangements not in the charter that deprive the board of a
  significant portion of its authority contravene § 141(a).
- *Abercrombie* test: restrictions fail where they remove from directors "in a very
  substantial way their duty to use their own best judgment on management matters,"
  or "tend to limit in a substantial way the freedom of director decisions on matters
  of management policy."
- The presence of a controlling stockholder does not alter the board-centric
  framework.
- The power to review is the power to decide; pre-approval rights and vetoes are
  equivalent in effect.
- A provision is not saved by the possibility of agreement between the holder and
  the board, because the provision only operates when they disagree.
- Delaware favours private ordering, but private ordering remains subject to the
  DGCL's limits, including §§ 141(a) and (c).
- Under the doctrine of independent legal significance, the same economic result may
  be lawful through one instrument and unlawful through another.

### Reasoning

*(Laster, V.C.)*

- The opening frames the whole opinion: "What happens when the seemingly irresistible
  force of market practice meets the traditionally immovable object of statutory law?
  A court must uphold the law, so the statute prevails."
- Six Delaware Supreme Court formulations of § 141(a) are stacked at the outset to
  establish that director primacy is not a policy preference the court is free to
  balance away.
- The soritical paradox is answered by prototype reasoning rather than by a test. The
  housecat and the lion both have paws; a bobcat is a close case and courts handle
  close cases routinely. The Stockholder Agreement is not a close case — it "look[s]
  like something a law professor dreamed up for students to use as a prototypical
  Section 141(a) violation."
- The markers of internality are applied one by one: parties limited to the Company
  and Moelis entities, signed on the eve of the IPO, resembling preferred-stock
  governance rights, drafted to bind the Board rather than the Company, untethered
  to any commercial transaction, indefinite, and not terminable by the Board.
- The veto reframing is dismantled functionally. With eighteen vetoes the Board
  becomes the management team proposing options and Moelis becomes the board.
- The remedy discussion is unusually practical and is where the doctrine of
  independent legal significance does its work: a single golden share of preferred,
  with a certificate of designations that becomes part of the charter by operation
  of law, achieves almost everything lawfully.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; single Vice Chancellor.
- The strongest counter-position is the Company's own, stated better than it stated
  it. If Moelis can get the identical result through a golden share, § 141(a) is
  policing form rather than substance, and the cost of that formalism falls on the
  many companies whose counsel used the market-standard instrument. Laster
  acknowledges the objection — "some might find it bizarre" — and answers that
  independent legal significance is a deliberate feature of the DGCL, not an
  accident. The General Assembly's response in § 122(18) suggests the legislature
  found the objection substantial.

### Cold-call notes

- **The question to expect** is how Moelis should have papered this. Two answers,
  both in the opinion: put the provisions in the certificate of incorporation, or
  have the Board issue a single golden share of preferred whose certificate of
  designations carries the rights and becomes part of the charter by operation of
  law.
- **Know which three provisions survived and why.** Designation, Nomination, Efforts.
  The unifying principle: they let Moelis *do* things — identify candidates, nominate,
  be assisted — without compelling the Board to *decide* anything. Recommendation,
  Vacancy and Size all compel a board decision. That distinction is the most likely
  fine-grained question.
- **The trap** is treating this as a fiduciary duty case. No duty claim is pleaded.
  It is a **power** case: the corporation could not grant these rights in this
  instrument, which is why a facial challenge succeeds without any showing of harm.
- **Be ready on "he didn't even have the votes."** Moelis held less than a majority of
  voting power, so his board control was purely contractual. That makes the case
  cleaner than a controller case, and the court says director primacy holds even when
  a controller *is* present.
- **Have the phrases:** "the Board is not really a board"; "the power to review is the
  power to decide"; "when Moelis and the Board agree, the provisions are not
  operating at all."
- **Expect the § 122(18) follow-up.** The legislature responded to this decision, the
  same way it responded to Van Gorkom with § 102(b)(7). Buccola has assigned both
  pairs, so the pattern — court invalidates market practice, General Assembly
  amends the DGCL — is very likely a theme rather than a coincidence.
