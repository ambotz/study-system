## Rule

A stockholder is a controller if the stockholder owns more than 50% of the voting power
**or** owns less and "exercises control over the business affairs of the corporation."
For a minority blockholder the question is whether the stockholder "exercised **actual
domination and control** over... [the] directors," with power "so potent that independent
directors... [could not] freely exercise their judgment."

Control may be pleaded **generally** or **transaction-specifically** — either that the
blockholder dominated the corporation or board at large, or that the blockholder dominated
the board or committee with respect to the challenged transaction.

## Facts

- **Musk held 22.1%** of Tesla's common stock — "relatively low," reflecting "a small
  block." He was Chairman since 2004, CEO since 2008, Chief Product Architect, and led the
  pre-IPO funding rounds.
- He held **21.9% of SolarCity**, chaired its board since 2006, and the acquisition
  converted his SolarCity holdings into **over $500 million** of Tesla shares. *He was on
  both sides.*
- **Tesla's own SEC filings** state the company is "highly dependent on the services of
  Elon Musk" and that losing him would disrupt operations and cause the stock to decline;
  they also acknowledge Musk is **not an independent director**. Musk said publicly that
  without him as CEO "the company wasn't going to make it," and on the announcement call
  repeatedly called Tesla "**my company**."
- **Six of seven directors were alleged to be interested.** Kimbal is Musk's brother and a
  cousin of SolarCity's founders, earning $4,964,381 as a Tesla director in 2015. Buss was
  SolarCity's CFO, paid $32 million. Gracias, the **Lead Independent Director**, sat on
  SolarCity's board, held SolarCity stock, and received the second Tesla Roadster ever made
  from Musk. Jurvetson held 417,450 SolarCity shares and received the first Model S and
  second Model X from Musk. Ehrenpreis's venture partner Pfund sat on SolarCity's special
  committee. *The gifts are pleaded facts, not colour.*
- Musk had **forced out founder-CEO Eberhard** and appointed himself CEO — history the
  board "conceivably was not lost on" when he proposed the acquisition.
- Tesla's **bylaws require 66⅔%** of voting power for certain mergers and for changes to
  board composition, which the complaint alleges magnifies a 22.1% block.
- Musk described Tesla, SolarCity and SpaceX as a **"pyramid"** on which he sat, warning it
  was "important that there not be some sort of house of cards that crumbles if one element
  ... falters."
- SolarCity was in a **liquidity crisis** revealed by due diligence. The deal valued it at
  about **$2.6 billion** ($25.37 per share) and **nearly doubled Tesla's debt**.
- **The stockholder vote excluded Musk, Gracias and Straubel — but not Kimbal, Jurvetson,
  Ehrenpreis, Buss, or other Tesla holders who owned SolarCity stock.** *This is why
  cleansing failed.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Motion to dismiss **denied**. It is "reasonably conceivable that Musk, as a controlling
  stockholder, controlled the Tesla Board in connection with the Acquisition," so entire
  fairness applies and the claims survive against all defendants.
- **The *Kahn v. Lynch* framework**: a controller either owns more than 50% of voting power,
  or owns less but exercises control over the business affairs of the corporation.
- **Two routes for a minority blockholder**: actual domination and control of the
  corporation, board or deciding committee **as to the challenged transaction**, or actual
  domination and control of the **majority of the board generally**.
- **There is no percentage threshold.** "[T]here is no absolute percentage of voting power
  that is required," and "[a]ctual control over business affairs may stem from sources
  extraneous to stock ownership." *Crimson Exploration* found **no** "linear, sliding scale
  approach whereby a larger share percentage makes it substantially more likely" a
  controller will be found — the decisions range from a 17.3% controller (*Zhongpin*) to a
  33.7% stake being an "aggressive" case, and 23.1% held **not** controlling (*Larkin*).
- Control is "intensely factual [and] a difficult [question] to resolve on the pleadings,"
  and the plaintiff's burden is only to "show it is **reasonably conceivable**" that Musk
  controlled Tesla.
- The four factors argued, each "tied directly to our controlling stockholder
  jurisprudence": influence over the stockholder vote; influence over the board as visionary,
  CEO and Chairman; connections to a board a majority of which was interested; and Tesla's
  and Musk's own acknowledgements of his control in public filings.

**Rejected**

- *Musk's 22.1% cannot dominate a contested election, and the 28% gap to majority control
  ends the inquiry.* The ability to influence a contested election is "a significant
  consideration" whose relevance "cannot credibly be challenged" — but "alleged control of
  the ballot box is not always dispositive." The focus is "the **de facto power** of a
  significant (but less than majority) shareholder, which, when coupled with other factors,
  gives that shareholder the ability to dominate the corporate decision-making process."
- *Public investors backing Musk's vision could close that gap.* The court is candid that
  "it is perhaps conceivable that, of all people, Musk might be the minority blockholder who
  could rally other stockholders to bridge that gap" — and equally candid that "this dynamic
  alone, even if true, would not be enough." **"But there is more."** The holding rests on
  accumulation, not on any single fact.
- *A fully informed, uncoerced vote of disinterested stockholders mandates business judgment
  review.* Cleansing does not reach a conflicted-controller transaction, and in any event the
  exclusion list omitted four directors and every other Tesla holder who owned SolarCity
  stock.

## Context

The companion to [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]] and the reason the syllabus pairs them. Sinclair
asks **what a conceded controller may do**; Tesla asks **who is a controller at all**.
Sinclair held 97% and conceded the point; Musk held 22.1% and litigated it to a close call.

**SB 21 rewrites this inquiry**, which is why the syllabus returns to it in session 10 with
[[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 2)]] and § 144(b), (c) and (e). Post-SB 21 § 144(e)(2) defines a
controlling stockholder by three specified routes — majority voting power; a contractual
right to elect a board majority; or **at least one-third** of voting power **plus** "power to
exercise managerial authority over the business and affairs." That floor replaces the
open-ended, factor-driven inquiry this opinion applies, and it is worth asking directly
whether Musk at 22.1% would qualify under the statute at all.

The cleansing point runs forward too. The vote here failed partly because the exclusion list
was drawn too narrowly, and post-SB 21 § 144(e)(5) now defines a **disinterested stockholder**
as one without a material interest in the transaction or a material relationship with the
controller — a statutory answer to exactly the question of who should have been excluded.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Consolidated derivative and putative class actions challenging Tesla's 2016 acquisition of
  SolarCity, on the defendants' **motion to dismiss**.
- **Posture drives everything.** The standard is "reasonably conceivable," all inferences run
  to the plaintiffs, and the court calls the result "a close call." Nothing is found — the
  holding is only that entire fairness review is conceivable enough to proceed.
- That matters because the practical consequence of denying dismissal in a controller case is
  enormous: entire fairness plus discovery is where these cases settle.

### Facts

Tesla acquired SolarCity in 2016 in an all-stock deal valuing it at roughly $2.6 billion.
SolarCity had been founded by Musk and his cousins Lyndon and Peter Rive, who ran it as CEO
and CTO; Musk chaired its board and was its largest stockholder. He was simultaneously
Tesla's Chairman, CEO, Chief Product Architect and largest stockholder.

The overlap ran through the whole board. Kimbal Musk is Elon's brother and the Rives' cousin.
Buss had been SolarCity's CFO. Gracias sat on SolarCity's board while serving as Tesla's Lead
Independent Director. Jurvetson's firm had invested at least $18.9 million in SolarCity and
his funds held 3.3% of it. Ehrenpreis's venture partner Pfund sat on SolarCity's special
committee — the committee negotiating against Tesla. Five of the seven Tesla directors
personally held SolarCity stock.

SolarCity was, by the time of the deal, in a liquidity crisis. The complaint characterises
the acquisition as a bailout benefiting six of seven Tesla directors or their families,
businesses or partners. Tesla's debt nearly doubled at closing.

The board was not required to put the deal to a stockholder vote but did. The merger
agreement excluded Musk, Gracias and Straubel from the tally — and no one else.

### Issue

- Whether a complaint adequately pleads that a 22.1% blockholder is a controlling stockholder,
  either generally or with respect to a particular transaction.
- Whether a stockholder vote cleanses the transaction and restores business judgment review.

### Holding

- **On controller status: reasonably conceivable.** No percentage threshold governs; control
  may stem from sources extraneous to stock ownership; and here the accumulation — voting
  influence magnified by supermajority bylaws, indispensability acknowledged in the company's
  own filings, a demonstrated willingness to remove a founder-CEO, an interested board
  majority, and Musk's own statements — supports the inference.
- **On cleansing: unavailable.** The conflicted-controller framing takes the transaction
  outside business judgment review regardless of the vote, and the exclusion list was in any
  event incomplete.
- **Disposition:** motion to dismiss denied; entire fairness review applies.

### Rule

- A controlling stockholder owns more than 50% of voting power, or owns less and exercises
  control over the corporation's business affairs.
- For a minority blockholder, the test is **actual domination and control** over the
  directors, with power so potent that independent directors cannot freely exercise judgment.
- Control may be shown **generally** or **as to the particular transaction**.
- There is no absolute percentage required, and no linear relationship between block size and
  controller status.
- Actual control may stem from sources extraneous to stock ownership.
- The inquiry focuses on **de facto power** which, coupled with other factors, permits
  domination of the corporate decision-making process.
- Controller status is intensely factual and difficult to resolve on the pleadings; the
  plaintiff need only make it **reasonably conceivable**.

### Reasoning

*(Slights, V.C.)*

- The opinion works factor by factor and concedes the weakness of each in isolation, which is
  what makes it persuasive. The 28% gap to majority control is "quite wide"; the
  rally-the-retail-investors theory "alone, even if true, would not be enough."
- **"But there is more."** The method is accumulation: voting influence plus supermajority
  bylaws plus the Eberhard precedent plus an interested board plus the company's own
  admissions plus Musk's own words.
- The court gives real weight to **self-characterisation** — SEC filings acknowledging
  dependence and non-independence, and Musk calling Tesla "my company" on the announcement
  call. Admissions against interest do heavy lifting because they need no inference.
- The personal-tie evidence is treated as bearing on the board's capacity to resist rather
  than on venality. Gifts of the second Roadster, the first Model S and the second Model X
  are facts about relationships.
- On the vote, the reasoning is structural: cleansing doctrines presuppose that the
  decision-makers and voters were free of the conflict, and here neither the board nor the
  excluded-share list satisfied that premise.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; single Vice Chancellor.
- The counter-position is strong enough that the court calls the case "a close call." A 22.1%
  holder cannot elect a board, cannot carry a 66⅔% vote, and cannot remove a director without
  substantial support. On the defendants' view, a founder-CEO who is charismatic and
  indispensable becomes a "controller" merely by being good at the job — which would make
  controller status turn on reputation rather than power. *Larkin* found 23.1% **not**
  controlling on comparable numbers. SB 21's one-third floor is essentially the legislature
  siding with this objection.

### Cold-call notes

- **The question to expect is what made 22.1% enough**, and the answer must be the
  accumulation, not any one fact. Have four ready: the company's own SEC filings admitting
  dependence and non-independence; the 66⅔% supermajority bylaws; the ouster of Eberhard;
  and six of seven directors interested.
- **Do not argue the percentage alone.** Both sides lose that way. *Zhongpin* found a 17.3%
  controller; *Larkin* found 23.1% not controlling. Citing the spread shows why the inquiry is
  factual.
- **Keep the two routes distinct.** General control of the corporation, or transaction-specific
  control of the board or committee. The court finds the latter, which is the narrower and
  more commonly available theory.
- **Be ready on the vote.** The exclusion list is the memorable failure: Musk, Gracias and
  Straubel out; Kimbal, Buss, Jurvetson, Ehrenpreis and every other SolarCity-owning Tesla
  holder in. Post-SB 21 § 144(e)(5) now defines who should have been excluded.
- **Anticipate the SB 21 question**, because it is coming in session 10: under § 144(e)(2) a
  controller needs majority voting power, a contractual right to elect a board majority, or
  **at least one-third** plus managerial authority. Musk at 22.1% clears none of them on their
  face. Whether the statute would change this result is the obvious exam hypothetical.
- **Numbers:** 22.1% of Tesla, 21.9% of SolarCity, over $500 million converted, $2.6 billion
  deal value, $25.37 per share, six of seven directors interested, $32 million to Buss,
  $4.96 million to Kimbal in one year, 66⅔% bylaw threshold.
