## Rule

A corporation may buy out a dissident blockholder with corporate funds, at a
premium, if the directors show **reasonable grounds to believe a danger to
corporate policy and effectiveness existed** because of that person's stock
ownership — a burden satisfied by good faith and reasonable investigation — but
not if the directors acted **solely or primarily** to perpetuate themselves in
office.

## Facts

- Maremont told Cheff in June 1957 that he had **no further interest in Holland
  and did not wish to buy any of its stock**, and then bought steadily in the open
  market. The deception is the fact that converts a blockholder into a perceived
  threat, and the court lists it first among the nine grounds supporting the
  board's belief.
- Maremont told Cheff he thought Holland's distribution model "wasn't modern" and
  that "furnaces could be sold as he sold mufflers, through half a dozen salesmen
  in a wholesale way." Holland **directly employed its retail salesmen** — a
  practice unique in the furnace business that management considered vital to the
  company's success, and which supported roughly 8,500 employees across 400 branch
  offices in 43 states.
- Maremont **demanded a seat on the board, was refused, and then substantially
  increased his purchases**. The sequence is what made the threat look like a
  campaign rather than an investment.
- Key employees began leaving. Cheff testified that about **25 key men** were lost
  and that the field organisation was considering departing in large numbers; Staal
  testified branch managers sought reassurance that Maremont would not gain
  control.
- A **Dun & Bradstreet report** described Maremont's practice of achieving quick
  profits by sales or liquidations of the companies he acquired, and Motor Products'
  income statement showed a 1957 loss of $336,121.
- **Merrill Lynch recommended the purchase**, and the board received competent
  advice that Holland was **over-capitalised** — a separate corporate reason to
  reduce outstanding equity.
- The purchase price of **$14.40 per share exceeded the prevailing market price**,
  against a book value of about $20 and a net quick asset value of about $14. *The
  premium is challenged and upheld as an ordinary control premium.*
- Only **Cheff, as an executive drawing a $77,400 salary, and Trenkamp, as the
  company's attorney**, held a clear pecuniary interest. The other directors' large
  shareholdings did not create one, because "all shareholders would presumably
  share the benefit."
- The August 30 resolution authorised open-market purchases **ostensibly for a
  stock option plan, which was never implemented**. *A bad fact the court does not
  engage with, and the strongest evidence of pretext in the record.*
- Mrs. Cheff told the board that she or Hazelbank would buy the block if Holland
  did not. *Argued and rejected as a ground of liability — the availability of a
  non-corporate alternative is irrelevant in both directions.*
- The 1957 decline in sales and profits **had already reversed** after a completed
  reorganisation, and the adverse Federal Trade Commission decision came only
  after the transaction. *Argued and rejected — neither could explain the employee
  unrest.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Under **8 Del. C. § 160** a corporation has statutory power to purchase and sell
  shares of its own stock, and "[t]he charge here is not one of violation of
  statute" but that the true motive was perpetuation of control.
- If the board acted on "a sincere belief that the buying out of the dissident
  stockholder was necessary to maintain what the board believed to be proper
  business practices," it is not liable "even though hindsight indicates the
  decision was not the wisest course." If it "acted solely or primarily because of
  the desire to perpetuate themselves in office, the use of corporate funds for such
  purposes is improper."
- Directors must **justify their actions**, but only those with a **personal
  pecuniary interest** — Cheff as an executive and Trenkamp as counsel — are held
  to a heightened standard of proof. A large shareholding alone does not create
  such an interest, because every shareholder shares in the benefit.
- The operative question is whether the directors showed **reasonable grounds to
  believe a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness existed** because of
  another person's stock ownership. "[T]he directors satisfy their burden by showing
  good faith and reasonable investigation," and "will not be penalized for an honest
  mistake of judgment, if the judgment appeared reasonable at the time the decision
  was made."
- The Vice Chancellor erred in finding no substantial evidence of a threatened
  liquidation, because the board's fear "was not limited to the possibility of
  liquidation; it included the alternate possibility of a **material change in
  Holland's sales policies**, which the board considered vital to its future
  success."
- Nine items of unrebutted testimony support the board's belief: Maremont's
  deception about his intentions; his stated view of the retail sales force; his
  demand for a board seat; his increased purchases after refusal; the employee
  unrest; the Dun & Bradstreet report; the Merrill Lynch recommendation; the advice
  of over-capitalisation; and the informal investigations by Staal and Cheff.
- The board was entitled to rely on that investigation, because **8 Del. C. § 141(f)**
  — the reliance provision as then numbered, now § 141(e) — "allows the directors to
  reasonably rely upon a report provided by corporate officers."
- The evidence "leads inevitably to the conclusion" that the board believed with
  justification in a reasonable threat to the continued existence of Holland "or at
  least existence in its present form." Reversed.

**Rejected**

- *Paying above market for the block was unfair to the corporation, and a control
  premium is meaningless when a company buys its own shares.* A substantial block
  normally sells above the open-market price, "the increment being attributable to a
  'control premium'," and a holder of a large block expects to receive it as part of
  his selling price. "[I]f the corporation desired to obtain the stock, it is
  unreasonable to expect that the corporation could avoid paying what any other
  purchaser would be required to pay."
- *The employee unrest was attributable to Holland's own business troubles or to the
  pending Federal Trade Commission proceeding.* The adverse FTC decision came after
  the transaction; the downward trend in sales and profits had already reversed; and
  every witness who testified on the point attributed the unrest to Maremont.
- *The directors should be liable because they could have used non-corporate funds,
  since Mrs. Cheff was willing to buy the block herself.* The reasoning is
  internally inconsistent. "[I]f the actions were in fact improper because of a
  desire to maintain control, then the presence or absence of a non-corporate
  alternative is irrelevant." Conversely, a decision made in good faith in the
  corporate interest "[is] not rendered improper by the fact that some individual
  directors were willing to advance personal funds if the corporation did not."
- *Excess corporate funds should have gone to finance a subsidiary instead.* That
  would not have solved Holland's over-capitalisation, and the choice was in any
  event "a matter of business judgment, which furnishes no justification for holding
  the directors personally responsible."

## Context

Cheff is the ancestor of [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]], excerpted immediately after
it and assigned the same day, and the continuity is verbatim. Unocal takes Cheff's
formulation — reasonable grounds to believe a danger to corporate policy and
effectiveness existed, satisfied by good faith and reasonable investigation — and
makes it the **first prong** of enhanced scrutiny. What Unocal adds is the second
prong: proportionality, "reasonable in relation to the threat posed." Cheff supplies
the threat inquiry and stops there.

Unocal also quotes Cheff's premise about why the inquiry exists at all: "We must
bear in mind the inherent danger in the purchase of shares with corporate funds to
remove a threat to corporate policy when a threat to control is involved. The
directors are of necessity confronted with a conflict of interest, and an objective
decision is difficult." That sentence is the seed of the "omnipresent specter."

Read against modern law, three features of Cheff have not survived intact.

**The primary purpose test has been absorbed.** Cheff asks whether the board acted
"solely or primarily" to entrench, and treats that as the whole of the inquiry.
Unocal retains the entrenchment limit but adds proportionality, and Unitrin then
converts the second prong into the preclusive-or-coercive screen. A board today can
lose without any finding of an entrenchment motive at all —
[[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] is the clearest example.

**The greenmail it blesses is now disfavoured.** Unocal itself notes the irony that
Mesa, a serial greenmailer, was complaining about selective treatment, and treats
the **forestalling of greenmail** as a legitimate corporate objective. The federal
excise tax on greenmail profits and the rise of the pill have since made the
practice largely obsolete, which is why Cheff survives as doctrine rather than as
practice.

**The threat is defined broadly and generously.** Cheff accepts a change in *sales
policy* as a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness, which is about as far
from price as a threat can get. That breadth runs straight through
[[Paramount v. Time]] and reaches its limit in [[Air Products v. Airgas]], where
inadequate price alone, as substantive coercion, is enough.

The one fact the opinion never confronts is the stock option plan. The August 30
resolution authorised open-market purchases "ostensibly for use in a stock option
plan," and the plan "has never been implemented." On a primary purpose test, an
unimplemented stated purpose is the most direct evidence of pretext available, and
the court passes over it in a clause. That silence is the best argument the
plaintiffs had and the sharpest question to be ready for.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Derivative suit by holders of **60 shares**, seeking rescission of the Holland–
  Motor Products transaction and an accounting from the individual directors.
- The Vice Chancellor held the directors liable, finding the purpose was an improper
  desire to maintain control, while exonerating those directors he believed unaware
  of the possibility of using non-corporate funds.
- The Delaware Supreme Court reversed.
- Posture shapes the analysis in a way worth noticing. Because this is a **motive**
  case tried on a full record, the court is reviewing findings about what directors
  believed, which is why the opinion spends nine numbered items on what the board
  was told rather than on what was objectively true.

### Facts

Holland Furnace Company manufactured warm air furnaces, air conditioning equipment
and other home heating equipment. Its distinguishing feature was that it employed
its retail salesmen directly — unique in the furnace business, and something
management regarded as vital to its success. That model supported roughly 8,500
employees and 400 branch sales offices across 43 states. Sales had fallen from over
$41 million in 1948 to under $32 million in 1956, which the defendants attributed to
the artificial post-war demand of 1946–48. The sales department had been reorganised
and unprofitable branches closed, and by 1957 management believed the changes were
working.

The board was the seven individual defendants. Cheff had been chief executive since
1933, drew $77,400 a year, owned 6,000 shares and sat on the board. His wife, a
daughter of Holland's founder, had been a director since 1922, owned 5,804 shares
personally and 47.9% of Hazelbank United Interest, the investment vehicle for the
Cheff-Landwehr family group, which held 164,950 of Holland's 883,585 outstanding
shares.

Trading volume in Holland stock, which had run between 10,300 and 24,200 shares a
month, jumped to 37,800 in the last week of June 1957 with a corresponding price
rise. That month Arnold Maremont — president of Maremont Automotive Products and
chairman of Motor Products and Allied Paper — asked Cheff about a merger with Motor
Products. Cheff said the difference in sales practices made a merger infeasible, and
Maremont replied that he therefore had no further interest in Holland and did not
wish to buy any of its stock.

Nobody on the board connected Maremont to the trading. Trenkamp and Staal, the
treasurer, investigated informally and got nowhere. The mystery ended in July when
Maremont telephoned the director Ames to say he owned 55,000 shares. He asked for
nothing — no policy change, no board seat.

The board investigated Maremont and his companies. Staal testified that "leading
bank officials," of whom he named only the vice president of the First National Bank
of Chicago, said Maremont "had been a participant, or had attempted to be, in the
liquidation of a number of companies." Cheff testified that throughout the
Kalamazoo–Battle Creek–Detroit area Maremont "is well known and not highly regarded
by any stretch." All of this was reported to the board.

On August 23 Maremont told Cheff that Motor Products then held about 100,000 shares
and demanded a seat on the board. Cheff refused. In the same conversation Maremont
made clear what he thought of Holland's distribution: he "indicated immediately that
he had no interest in that type of distribution, that he didn't think it was modern,
that he felt furnaces could be sold as he sold mufflers, through half a dozen
salesmen in a wholesale way."

Employee reaction followed. Cheff testified the field organisation was considering
leaving in large numbers and that roughly 25 key men were lost; Staal said branch
managers approached him for reassurance. The company also received a Dun & Bradstreet
report describing Maremont's practice of achieving quick profits through sales or
liquidations, and an income statement showing Motor Products had lost $336,121 in
1957.

On August 30 the board was told of the demand, of Maremont's view of the sales
organisation, and of the investigation, and authorised open-market purchases of
Holland stock with corporate funds "ostensibly for use in a stock option plan." Mrs.
Cheff made parallel personal purchases. Prices rose as Maremont, Holland and Mrs.
Cheff all bought. On September 13 Maremont wrote to every director asking for a broad
engineering survey for the benefit of all stockholders. Motor Products' annual report
that month described the Holland position as a "special situation" rather than its
normal practice of investing in "an active company." Maremont offered to sell at
$14.00 on September 4 and withdrew the offer when Holland was slow to respond.

On September 30 Motor Products made a buy-sell offer to Hazelbank. Hazelbank's board
took no action and referred it to its finance committee, which referred it on to
Holland as the company primarily concerned, even though a majority of the Hazelbank
board favoured buying. Trenkamp then met Maremont in Chicago on October 14–15 —
knowing already that Mrs. Cheff and Hazelbank would buy if Holland did not — and
reached a tentative agreement for Motor Products to sell 155,000 shares at $14.40.

The Holland board approved on October 23 with all directors but Spatta present. The
dangers were reviewed again, Mrs. Cheff repeated that she or Hazelbank would buy
otherwise, and the board was told that substantial commercial borrowing would be
needed. The price exceeded market; book value was about $20 a share against a net
quick asset value of about $14. The stock option plan mentioned in the August minutes
was never implemented. Holland stock reached $15.25 in 1959.

### Issue

- Whether directors may use corporate funds under 8 Del. C. § 160 to repurchase a
  dissident blockholder's shares at a premium, and on what showing.
- Which directors bear a heightened burden of justification, and whether a large
  shareholding alone creates a disqualifying pecuniary interest.
- Whether the Holland board had reasonable grounds to believe Maremont's stock
  ownership posed a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness.
- Whether the availability of non-corporate funds to accomplish the same purchase
  bears on the directors' liability.

### Holding

- **On authority: the purchase was within § 160.** The statute permits a corporation
  to deal in its own shares, and the claim is about motive rather than power.
- **On the test: reasonable grounds, shown by good faith and reasonable
  investigation.** A purchase made on a sincere belief that removing the dissident
  was necessary to preserve proper business practices is protected even if unwise in
  hindsight; a purchase made solely or primarily to entrench is improper.
- **On burden: only Cheff and Trenkamp** — an executive and the company's attorney —
  carried a personal pecuniary interest and the heightened standard. A substantial
  shareholding alone does not, since the benefit flows to all shareholders alike.
- **On the merits: the board had reasonable grounds.** Nine unrebutted items support
  a justified belief in a threat to Holland's continued existence, "or at least
  existence in its present form."
- **On the alternative source of funds: irrelevant.** An improper purpose is not
  cured by the absence of a non-corporate alternative, and a proper one is not
  spoiled by its presence.
- **Disposition:** reversed.

### Rule

- Section 160 grants a corporation statutory power to purchase and sell shares of
  its own stock, long recognised in Delaware.
- Where that power is used to remove a threat to corporate policy while a threat to
  control is involved, the directors are "of necessity confronted with a conflict of
  interest, and an objective decision is difficult."
- Directors must therefore show reasonable grounds to believe a danger to corporate
  policy and effectiveness existed because of another person's stock ownership.
- That burden is satisfied by a showing of good faith and reasonable investigation.
- Directors are not penalised for an honest mistake of judgment if the judgment
  appeared reasonable when made.
- A purchase made solely or primarily to perpetuate the directors in office is
  improper regardless of price.
- A substantial block ordinarily commands a control premium, and a corporation
  buying it cannot expect to avoid paying what any other purchaser would.
- Directors may reasonably rely upon reports provided by corporate officers.

### Reasoning

- The court separates **power** from **purpose** at the outset, which is what keeps
  the case from being about § 160 at all. Because the statute plainly authorises the
  purchase, everything turns on the motive behind it.
- The conflict is acknowledged rather than assumed away. Directors buying out a
  threat to control are buying out a threat to themselves, and the court says so —
  then sets a burden of justification rather than a prohibition.
- That burden is deliberately **process-shaped**: good faith and reasonable
  investigation. The court is asking whether the directors had reason to believe
  what they believed, not whether they were right, which is why nine items of what
  the board was *told* carry the holding.
- The interest analysis is narrow in a way that matters later. Only a salary and a
  legal retainer count; equity does not, because equity aligns a director with the
  other holders. Unocal repeats the point when Mesa argues that the Unocal directors
  were interested by virtue of their shareholdings.
- On the premium, the court reasons from the market rather than from the corporation's
  perspective. A block has a control premium in anyone's hands, so a buyer who happens
  to be the issuer cannot claim an exemption from the going price.
- The non-corporate funds passage is the sharpest piece of reasoning in the opinion,
  because it exposes the Vice Chancellor's holding as self-contradictory. Liability
  turned on a fact that is irrelevant under either branch of the court's own test.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion leaves one thing conspicuously unexamined. The August 30 resolution
authorised the repurchases "ostensibly for use in a stock option plan," and the court
notes in a single clause that the plan "has never been implemented." Under a test that
asks whether the directors acted solely or primarily to entrench, a stated corporate
purpose that was never carried out is the most direct evidence of pretext the record
could contain, and it goes unaddressed. The court's implicit answer is that the
October purchase was separately considered and separately justified, but it never says
so.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the two-part motive test.** Sincere belief that the buyout was necessary
  to preserve proper business practices, versus acting solely or primarily to stay in
  office. Then say how the first is proved: good faith and reasonable investigation.
- **Know that this is Unocal prong one.** The sentence about reasonable grounds and
  the sentence about good faith and reasonable investigation reappear verbatim in
  Unocal. What Unocal adds is proportionality. Saying that shows the whole arc of the
  session in one line.
- **Be precise about who bore the heavier burden and why.** Cheff had a salary,
  Trenkamp had the legal work. Stock ownership alone does not count, because the
  benefit is shared with all holders — a point Unocal repeats when Mesa makes the same
  argument.
- **Have the threat characterisation ready.** The board's fear was not only liquidation
  but "a material change in Holland's sales policies." Notice how far that is from
  price. A threat to the *business model* counts, which is the broad conception of
  threat the takeover line inherits.
- **Expect the pretext question.** The stock option plan was never implemented. The
  honest answer is that it is the best evidence the plaintiffs had, that the court does
  not engage with it, and that the October purchase was separately deliberated on a
  record the court found sufficient.
- **Be ready on the premium.** A block carries a control premium in any buyer's hands,
  so the corporation cannot expect a discount for being the issuer. The trap is
  arguing that control is meaningless to a company buying its own shares — the court
  answers that the question is what the *seller* can command.
- **Numbers worth holding.** 883,585 shares outstanding; 164,950 held by the family
  group; Maremont at 55,000 then about 100,000; 155,000 shares bought at $14.40
  against a $20 book value; roughly 25 key men lost; 60 shares held by the plaintiffs.
