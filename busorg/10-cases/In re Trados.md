## Rule

Directors must "strive in good faith and on an informed basis to **maximize the value of
the corporation for the benefit of its residual claimants**, the ultimate beneficiaries of
the firm's value, **not for the benefit of its contractual claimants**" — so where the
interests of preferred and common diverge, "it will be the duty of the board... to **prefer
the interests of the common stock**... to the interests created by the special rights,
preferences, etc. of preferred stock."

## Facts

- **Six of seven directors were not disinterested and independent**, which is what makes
  entire fairness the standard. The court reaches that conclusion director by director.
- **Campbell and Hummel** took personal benefits. Campbell received **$2.34 million** from
  the MIP — "23% to 47% of his net worth" and "nearly ten times what he would make annually
  by continuing to manage Trados as a stand-alone entity" — plus the presidency of SDL and a
  seat on SDL's board.
- **Gandhi, Scanlan and Stone** were **dual fiduciaries** for VC funds holding preferred
  stock entitled to a liquidation preference. "If the interests of the beneficiaries to whom
  the dual fiduciary owes duties are aligned, then there is no conflict... But if the
  interests of the beneficiaries diverge, the fiduciary faces an inherent conflict."
- **Prang**, nominally an outside director, was neither independent nor disinterested. A
  history with Sequoia going back to Aspect Development, roughly **$300,000** invested in
  three Sequoia funds including the one holding Trados preferred, and a job as chief
  executive of a Sequoia-backed company where Gandhi sat on the board, produced "a sense of
  '**owingness**'." The **$220,633** he received was "nearly double [his] annual salary and
  3.7%–5.5% of his estimated net worth."
- **The MIP's cutback feature** reduced a participant's MIP payout by any consideration
  received as an equity holder, which "ensured that management would focus **exclusively** on
  proceeds received through the MIP rather than from their status as common stockholders."
  That single design feature converts management from common-aligned to preferred-aligned.
- **The arithmetic of the waterfall.** On $60 million: without the MIP, preferred take $57.9
  million and common $2.1 million. With it, management take $7.8 million, preferred $52.2
  million, common **zero**. "[T]he common stockholders contributed **100%** of their ex-MIP
  proceeds while the preferred stockholders only contributed **10%**."
- The **break-even deal value was $66.5 million**, and even above it the common funded the
  MIP disproportionately — at $70 million the common keep 25% of what they would otherwise
  receive "[and] [t]he preferred stockholders would not lose a dime."
- **Nobody considered the common.** Laidig volunteered at deposition that the directors never
  considered the common stockholders; at trial the others "recit[ed] in lockstep that they
  considered all of the Company's stakeholders," a "chorus [that] sounded well-rehearsed."
  "When pressed, the directors could not recall any specific discussion of the common stock."
- **No special committee and no fairness opinion.** "[T]he defendants did not consider forming
  a special committee to represent the interests of the common stockholders. They also chose
  not to obtain a fairness opinion."
- **The deal was never conditioned on a majority-of-the-disinterested-common vote.** The vote
  came from the preferred, who controlled a majority on an as-converted basis, plus "[l]arge
  [f]riendlies" such as Hummel — whose MIP share was **raised from 12% to 14%** when he
  "seemed to be having second thoughts," after which he was "obviously a lock."
- **Microsoft, one of the largest common holders, abstained** because it could not stomach
  "the economic result."
- **The common stock had no economic value** as a stand-alone matter. No VC firm would invest
  more and none was obliged to; no outside firm would invest without the incumbents; the
  venture debt market was exhausted; and Trados could not grow fast enough to "escape the
  gravitational pull of the large liquidation preference and cumulative dividend" earning 8%.
  *This is the fact that saves the defendants.*
- Jarrell's DCF, on "reasonable and plaintiff-friendly assumptions," generated nothing for
  the common. *The plaintiff's expert did not provide "similarly persuasive testimony."*
- The directors had repeatedly minuted a **good faith determination that the common was worth
  $0.10 per share** for option grants, and testified at trial that those minutes were false.
  *Argued and — remarkably — accepted; the court declines to hold them to it.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **The standard of conduct.** Directors must "seek 'to promote the value of the corporation
  for the benefit of its stockholders'," and specifically must "strive in good faith and on an
  informed basis to maximize the value of the corporation for the benefit of its **residual
  claimants**, the ultimate beneficiaries of the firm's value, not for the benefit of its
  **contractual claimants**."
- "[I]t is the duty of directors to pursue the best interests of the corporation and its
  common stockholders, **if that can be done faithfully with the contractual promises owed to
  the preferred**." Where discretionary judgment is exercised, the board must "prefer the
  interests of the common stock—as the good faith judgment of the board sees them to be—to the
  interests created by the special rights, preferences, etc. of preferred stock."
- "This principle is not unique to preferred stock; it applies equally to **other holders of
  contract rights** against the corporation."
- **Standard of conduct and standard of review are distinct.** "The standard of conduct
  describes what directors are expected to do... The standard of review is the test that a
  court applies when evaluating whether directors have met the standard of conduct."
- **Entire fairness applies** because the plaintiff proved that a majority of the board was
  not disinterested and independent, on a **director-by-director** analysis. "This finding
  does not mean that the six directors necessarily breached their fiduciary duties, only that
  entire fairness is the lens."
- **Interest** means a personal financial benefit "not equally shared by the stockholders,"
  "of a sufficiently material importance, in the context of the director's economic
  circumstances, as to have made it improbable that the director could perform her fiduciary
  duties... without being influenced by her overriding personal interest." Post-transaction
  board membership "standing alone, would not be sufficient."
- **Why VC preferred creates divergence.** "[W]hile each of the... contracting techniques helps
  VC investors minimize agency risk, they also give rise to the possibility that the venture
  capitalist may use the contract rights **opportunistically**." Because of liquidation
  preferences, preferred holders "sometimes gain less from increases in firm value than they
  lose from decreases," so "preferred dominated boards may favor immediate 'liquidity
  events'... **even if operating the firm as a stand-alone going concern would generate more
  value**." The distortion is "most likely to arise when... the firm is **neither a complete
  failure nor a stunning success**."
- **The VC business model reinforces the divergence.** "Before venture capitalists invest, they
  plan for exit," they seek "usually a ten-fold return of capital over a five-year period," and
  "will sometimes liquidate an otherwise viable firm, if its expected returns are not what they
  ... expected."
- **Fair dealing "weighed decidedly in favor of the plaintiff."** There was "no contemporaneous
  evidence suggesting that the directors set out to deal with the common stockholders in a
  procedurally fair manner." Initiation, negotiation and structure, and director approval all
  showed unfair dealing; the MIP "converted the management team from holders of equity
  interests aligned with the common stock to claimants whose return profile and incentives
  closely resembled those of the preferred."
- "**Conflict blindness and its lesser cousin, conflict denial, have long afflicted the
  financially sophisticated.**"
- **Fair price carried the case.** The test of fairness is whether "the minority stockholder
  shall receive the **substantial equivalent in value of what he had before**." Because "Trados
  did not have a reasonable prospect of generating value for the common stock," the common
  "received the substantial equivalent in value of what they had before."
- **Unfair process alone is not a separate breach.** "As the Delaware Supreme Court has
  recognized, an unfair process can infect the price, result in a finding of breach, and warrant
  a potential remedy. On these facts, such a finding is not warranted." The consequence of
  skipping a special committee was that the defendants "[were] forced to prove at trial that
  the Merger was entirely fair. Having done so, they have demonstrated that they did not commit
  a fiduciary breach."
- Judgment for the defendants. The fair value of the common for appraisal purposes was **zero**.

**Rejected**

- *The Board had a fiduciary duty to reject the Merger and continue operating Trados
  independently to generate value for the common.* The claim is legally sound and factually
  unsupported. Trados could not raise outside capital, could not return to the venture debt
  market, and could not grow past an 8% cumulative dividend on a $57.9 million preference.
- *Preferred and common are both equity, so their interests are aligned in maximising firm
  value.* "[T]he fact that preferred and common 'may have incentives to pursue different exit
  strategies is not obvious'," but preferred "carries special rights that create specific
  economic incentives that differ from those of common stock."
- *The outside directors were independent, so the board majority was clean.* Prang's history
  with Sequoia produced a sense of owingness, and his $220,633 was material against a $125,000
  salary and a $4–6 million estimated net worth. The court notes the scholarly point that
  "so-called 'independent directors'" on VC-backed boards "are often not truly independent of
  the VCs," while insisting that "the plaintiff could not rely on general characterizations of
  the VC ecosystem."
- *The preferred voting their own shares in their own interest is evidence of unfair dealing.*
  "Stockholders in Delaware corporations have a right to control and vote their shares in their
  own interest... limited only by any fiduciary duty owed to other stockholders." Nor is the
  failure to condition the deal on a disinterested common vote itself unfair — "it simply
  deprives the defendants of otherwise helpful affirmative evidence of fairness."
- *The directors should be held to their own minuted determinations that the common was worth
  $0.10 per share.* "[I]t is difficult to countenance a 'believe-me-now-that-I-was-lying-then'
  defense," but VC boards "often use rough, even arbitrary rules of thumb," and the era was one
  of widespread option backdating and "laxity and sloppiness (at a minimum)."
- *Trados was a failing company with no cash, no business plan and no market.* "Each contention
  had a kernel of truth, but the directors **exaggerated to the point of caricature**." The
  court treats these as "risks rather than mortal crises," and finds Trados "more bullish than
  the gloomy picture painted by the defendants."

## Context

Trados is the venture capital analogue of the controller cases, and the structural insight
transfers directly. A liquidation preference is a **contract right**, and the board's duty runs
to the **residual claimants** — so a preferred holder's special rights do not entitle the
holder to have the board exercise discretion in their favour. The dual fiduciary problem the
court identifies comes from Weinberger: "[t]here is no dilution of [fiduciary] obligation where
one holds dual or multiple directorships," and "[t]here is no 'safe harbor' for such divided
loyalties."

The **middle case** is where the conflict lives, and the casebook frames the whole session
around it. On a stunning success everyone wins; on a total failure everyone loses; in between,
"selling the company off, even for a not-very-high price, will guarantee a return for the
preferred," while "[f]or these common shareholders, hope springs eternal" — the common own an
option, and an option is worth something only if the company keeps rolling the dice.

The **MIP is the mechanism worth studying**, because it does not look like self-dealing on its
face. Incentive plans of this kind are standard in VC portfolio companies; what made this one
evidence of unfair dealing was the **cutback feature**, which stripped management of any reason
to push for a price at which the common would see value. The board never asked how to allocate
"incremental dollars above the liquidation preference," so above the preference the common paid
100% and the preferred 10%.

**What saved the defendants is the counterfactual, not the process.** Entire fairness is a
unitary test, and Laster finds fair dealing decisively against the defendants and price
decisively for them. That is only possible because the common stock was worth nothing anyway —
"the substantial equivalent in value of what he had before" was zero. The practical lesson runs
the other way: a board that skips a special committee and a fairness opinion buys itself the
burden of proving fairness at trial, and here that meant years of litigation and a trial record
the court describes as "replete with contradictions and less-than-credible testimony."

The doctrine travels to other divergences. [[Quadrant Structured Products v. Vertin]], assigned
the following day, calls its own facts "a **reverse-Trados theory**" — an insolvent company
where the residual claimants are creditors and the directors are dual fiduciaries for the
equity — and reaches the crucial limit: the theory applies to transactions conferring "direct
and specific benefits," not to "business decisions that generally affect the value of the entity
as a whole."

[[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]], assigned the same day, is the contractual answer to
Trados. If preferred holders cannot make the board prefer them, sophisticated investors can at
least contract for the next best thing — a drag-along with a **covenant not to sue** — and Rich
holds that such a covenant is not facially invalid, within limits.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Post-trial opinion in a class action by common stockholders, consolidated with an appraisal
  proceeding, following an eight-year path from the 2005 merger.
- **Burden structure matters and is stated expressly.** Entire fairness did not apply ab initio.
  The plaintiff bore the initial burden "to prove facts sufficient to rebut one of the elements
  of the business judgment rule"; once he proved that a disinterested and independent board
  majority did not approve the Merger, the burden shifted to the defendants to prove entire
  fairness.
- Judgment for the defendants; appraisal value of the common fixed at zero.

### Facts

Trados developed translation software. It raised venture capital in 2000 to support a growth
strategy aimed at an initial public offering, issuing preferred stock in successive rounds —
Wachovia put in $5 million for 3,640,000 Series A shares, Invision AG $2 million for 2,350,174
Series F — with most investors also obtaining the right to designate directors. Revenue grew
year over year, but not enough to satisfy the backers, and by 2004 the VC directors were looking
to exit.

On July 7, 2004 the board hired Joseph Campbell as chief executive. The Wachovia-designated
director suggested a plan to incentivise executives to pursue a sale, and the board agreed,
recognising that the executives "may not have sufficient incentives to remain in the Company's
service and to pursue a potential acquisition of the Company, **due to the high liquidation
preference of the Company's preferred stock**."

Campbell and Budge presented the Management Incentive Plan in December 2004 — though they and
Hummel were its three largest recipients — and the entire board, including Campbell and Hummel,
approved it unanimously. The MIP gave senior management an escalating percentage of sale
proceeds depending on valuation, with a cutback reducing any MIP payout by consideration
received as an equity holder. Campbell got 30%, Hummel 12%, Budge 10%.

Campbell's tenure produced record profits. On February 11, 2005 he agreed with Mark Lancaster of
SDL to sell Trados for $60 million — $50 million cash and $10 million in SDL stock. The board
reviewed and approved the terms on an April 8 conference call and a letter of intent followed on
April 11. Lancaster agreed that Campbell would become SDL's President and Chief Strategy Officer
and join its board.

The distribution was mechanical. The MIP took the first 13% — $7.8 million — of which Campbell's
30% was $2.34 million. The total liquidation preference including accumulated dividends was $57.9
million; the remaining $52.2 million went to the preferred, who each received less than their
full preference but more than their initial investment. The common received nothing.

The board formally approved on June 15. Two stockholder votes were needed, one by the preferred
and one by the common, and management expected both easily because the preferred held enough
common to carry it. Hummel's MIP share was raised from 12% to 14% when he wavered, and two days
later he was described internally as "obviously a lock." Microsoft, a large common holder,
abstained. The stockholders approved on June 17, 2005.

Marc Christen, who owned about 5% of the common, sought appraisal and in July 2008 filed a class
action alleging that the directors breached their duty of loyalty by approving the Merger. The
case went to trial.

### Issue

- What standard of conduct governs directors when the interests of preferred and common
  stockholders diverge.
- Whether a majority of the Trados board was disinterested and independent, which determines
  whether entire fairness or the business judgment rule supplies the standard of review.
- Whether the Merger was entirely fair as to both dealing and price.
- Whether an unfair process is itself a breach where the price is fair.

### Holding

- **On the standard of conduct: prefer the common.** Directors must maximise value for the
  residual claimants, not for contractual claimants, and where discretion is exercised must
  prefer the common over rights created by the preferred's special terms, consistent with the
  contractual promises actually owed.
- **On the standard of review: entire fairness.** Six of seven directors were interested or
  conflicted — two by MIP payments and post-transaction employment, three as dual fiduciaries for
  preferred-holding funds, one by a combination of owingness to Sequoia and a material payment.
- **On fair dealing: unfair.** The sale was initiated by directors who wanted an exit and not by
  anyone evaluating Trados from the common's perspective; the MIP was designed, presented and
  approved by its own largest beneficiaries and realigned management with the preferred; approval
  was obtained from a conflicted board that never discussed the common; and no special committee,
  fairness opinion or disinterested common vote was used.
- **On fair price: fair.** The common stock had no economic value before the Merger, so receiving
  nothing was the substantial equivalent of what they had.
- **On the unitary test: no breach.** An unfair process can infect price and produce liability,
  but on these facts it did not; the consequence of the procedural failures was the burden of
  proof, which the defendants carried.
- **Disposition:** judgment for the defendants; appraisal value zero.

### Rule

- Directors must strive in good faith and on an informed basis to maximise the value of the
  corporation for the benefit of its residual claimants, not its contractual claimants.
- It is the duty of directors to pursue the best interests of the corporation and its common
  stockholders if that can be done faithfully with the contractual promises owed to the preferred.
- Where discretionary judgment is exercised, the board must prefer the interests of the common to
  the interests created by the preferred's special rights.
- The principle applies equally to other holders of contract rights against the corporation.
- A director is interested where he receives a personal financial benefit not equally shared by
  the stockholders, of sufficient material importance in the context of his economic circumstances.
- A dual fiduciary faces an inherent conflict where the interests of his beneficiaries diverge.
- Entire fairness requires proof of both fair dealing and fair price, examined as a whole rather
  than bifurcated, and perfection is neither possible nor expected.
- The test of fairness is whether the minority stockholder receives the substantial equivalent in
  value of what he had before.

### Reasoning

*(Laster, V.C.)*

- The opinion is organised around the distinction between **standard of conduct** and **standard
  of review**, and the whole result depends on keeping them apart. The directors' conduct was
  poor; the standard of review put the burden on them; and they met it anyway because of a fact
  about value rather than a fact about behaviour.
- The conduct rule is derived from **who owns the residual**. Preferred holders have contract
  rights and are paid according to them; the board's discretionary judgment belongs to the people
  whose claim has no ceiling. That is why the rule "applies equally to other holders of contract
  rights."
- The conflict analysis is deliberately concrete. The court refuses to rest on the academic
  critique of VC-backed "independent" directors — "the plaintiff could not rely on general
  characterizations of the VC ecosystem" — and instead makes findings about Prang's particular
  history, investments and employment.
- The **materiality** findings are shaped by the defendants' own litigation conduct. Counsel
  blocked discovery into net worth and let the witnesses give only ranges, and the court draws
  the inference against them, computing percentages off the estimates the witnesses supplied.
- On the MIP the reasoning is arithmetic rather than moral. The court works out who funds each
  incremental dollar above the preference, finds the common funding 100% against the preferred's
  10%, identifies a break-even of $66.5 million, and shows that even far above it the common
  surrender 75% of their proceeds while the preferred "would not lose a dime."
- The cutback feature is the hinge. Without the MIP, management's options and common stock would
  have aligned them with the common and given them "strong reasons to evaluate critically" a
  stand-alone future. With it, "[t]he combination eliminated any financial incentive for senior
  management to push for a price at which the common stock would receive value."
- On fair price the court sets the trial testimony aside almost entirely — the directors
  "exaggerated to the point of caricature," their minutes were false, their chorus was rehearsed —
  and decides on a defence expert's DCF built on plaintiff-friendly assumptions. The finding is
  not that Trados was doomed but that it could not outrun an 8% cumulative dividend on a $57.9
  million preference.
- The closing move is the practical one. Skipping a special committee did not create liability;
  it created a **burden**, and the defendants paid for it with a trial rather than with damages.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The unsatisfying part is how much the result depends on a counterfactual the directors never
examined. Laster finds that nobody on the board considered the common stockholders at all, that
the MIP was designed to make sure nobody would, and that the process was unfair in every
component — and then concludes there was no breach because a valuation performed years later in
litigation shows the common were worthless. The doctrine permits this, since entire fairness is
unitary and price can carry the day. But it means the directors' vindication rests on a fact they
never bothered to establish, and the opinion's own treatment of the $0.10 minutes shows how
willing the court was to disregard the contemporaneous record in both directions.

### Cold-call notes

- **State the conduct rule precisely, including its limiting clause.** Maximise value for the
  **residual claimants**, and prefer the common "**if that can be done faithfully with the
  contractual promises owed to the preferred**." The preferred get their contract; they do not get
  the board's discretion.
- **Separate conduct from review.** Six conflicted directors put the case into entire fairness.
  That is a statement about the **lens**, not about breach — the opinion says so explicitly.
- **Explain the middle case.** Stunning success and total failure produce no conflict. In between,
  a preference guarantees the preferred a return on a mediocre sale while extinguishing the
  common's option value. That is the structural insight of the whole session.
- **Know why the MIP was evidence of unfair dealing, and be specific.** The **cutback feature**
  reduced MIP payouts by any equity consideration, so management had no reason to fight for a
  price above the preference. Then give the arithmetic: at $60 million the common fund 100% of the
  MIP and the preferred 10%; break-even is $66.5 million.
- **Do not say the VCs were wrong to vote their own shares.** They were entitled to, "limited only
  by any fiduciary duty owed to other stockholders." What was wrong was the **directors'** conduct.
- **Be ready for "so what should they have done?"** Form a special committee of directors aligned
  with the common, get an outside analysis of the stand-alone alternative, and condition the deal
  on a majority-of-the-disinterested-common vote. None of these was legally required; all would
  have shifted or discharged the burden.
- **Have the reason they won ready in one sentence.** The common stock was worthless before the
  Merger, so receiving nothing was "the substantial equivalent in value of what he had before."
- **Connect forward.** Quadrant calls its facts a "reverse-Trados theory" and limits the doctrine
  to transactions conferring direct and specific benefits, not ordinary business strategy.
- **Numbers worth holding.** $60 million deal; $57.9 million preference; $7.8 million MIP; $52.2
  million to preferred; zero to common; $66.5 million break-even; Campbell's $2.34 million at 23–47%
  of net worth; Prang's $220,633 against a $125,000 salary; six of seven directors conflicted; 8%
  cumulative dividend.
