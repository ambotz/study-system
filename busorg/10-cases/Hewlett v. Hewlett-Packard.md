## Rule

Vote buying is **illegal per se** where "the object or purpose is to defraud or in some
way disenfranchise the other stockholders," and otherwise is a **voidable transaction
subject to a test for intrinsic fairness** — and while stockholders may sell their own
votes freely, **management may not use corporate assets** to buy votes in a contested
proxy fight over an extraordinary transaction unless it can show no deleterious effect on
the franchise.

## Facts

- The merger required a **stockholder vote**, and HP's management supported it. "The
  extraordinary transaction at issue in this case is one of the limited types of
  transactions a corporate board **cannot unilaterally cause its corporation to
  consummate**." That is the fact that makes the interference structural rather than
  incidental.
- Deutsche Bank's proxy committee conducted an **independent review**, decided to vote
  against, and on March 15 submitted its proxies voting **25 million shares against** the
  merger.
- **On that same day, March 15**, HP closed a new multi-billion dollar credit facility to
  which Deutsche Bank had just been added as a **co-arranger**. The coincidence of dates
  is the inducement half of the claim.
- By **March 18** Deutsche Bank feared that HP's reaction to the adverse vote "would be to
  end the ongoing, and desired future, business dealings between HP and Deutsche Bank."
- **Allegedly at HP management's demand**, a telephone conference was held on the morning
  of the March 19 vote, and afterwards Deutsche Bank **switched as many as 17 million
  votes** in favour.
- Fiorina **delayed the scheduled opening** of the meeting to wait for word from Deutsche
  Bank, and **closed the polls promptly** after apparently receiving word on the podium
  that the vote had switched. The choreography is what makes the inference reasonable.
- HP had **1,941,391,000 shares** outstanding and eligible to vote, and management
  reported approval "by an extremely thin margin." If the margin was under 1% of shares
  voted, the 17 million switched votes "could have determined the outcome."
- The Hewlett Parties and their allies held about **18%** of HP's voting shares and had
  waged a full proxy contest since November 2001.
- HP's stock fell **18.7%** on announcement, from $23.21 to $18.87 — about $8.5 billion of
  stockholder value — and was down 27.2% by early November, against a 9.9% gain in an
  index of comparable companies.
- **HP took no protective step** such as a ratifying vote of independent stockholders.
  *The absence of a cleansing device is what leaves the claim standing.*
- There was **no binding contract** obliging Deutsche Bank to vote any particular way.
  *Argued and rejected as a required element.*
- The votes bought were **less than a majority** of the outstanding shares. *Argued and
  rejected as a required element.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The governing standard is Schreiber v. Carney. Vote buying "is **illegal per se** if
  'the object or purpose is to defraud or in some way disenfranchise the other
  stockholders'," and absent those purposes, "because vote-buying is so easily susceptible
  of abuse it must be viewed as a **voidable transaction subject to a test for intrinsic
  fairness**."
- The older rule that vote buying was illegal per se as a matter of public policy — on the
  theory that each stockholder is entitled to rely on the independent judgment of his
  fellows — is "obsolete because it is both impractical and impossible of application to
  modern corporations," and the General Assembly has codified the permissibility of voting
  agreements at **§ 218(c)**. What survives is per se illegality **for deleterious
  purposes**.
- The critical distinction is **who is buying**. "Shareholders are free to do whatever they
  want with their votes, including **selling them to the highest bidder**. Management, on
  the other hand, **may not use corporate assets** to buy votes in a hotly contested proxy
  contest about an extraordinary transaction that would significantly transform the
  corporation, unless it can be demonstrated... that management's vote-buying activity does
  not have a deleterious effect on the corporate franchise."
- **No binding contract is required.** "[A] contractually binding obligation between parties
  to an agreement to vote shares in a particular manner is not a prerequisite to a
  vote-buying claim." The focus belongs on "[p]rotection of unsuspecting shareholders who
  are at risk of being defrauded or disenfranchised," not on whether the bad actors were
  contractually bound to each other.
- The threshold pleading standard: the plaintiff must plead facts from which it is
  reasonable to infer that "in exchange for '**consideration personal to the
  stockholder**,' a stockholder has agreed to vote, **or has in fact voted**, his shares as
  directed by another."
- **No majority is required.** A plaintiff need not show that a majority of outstanding
  shares was obligated to vote a particular way. "Less than a majority of votes can be
  decisive in tipping the results of an election," and if voiding the tainted votes would
  change the result, the claim lies.
- The alleged facts support a reasonable inference that the switch "was the result of the
  enticement or coercion of Deutsche Bank by HP management" — the credit facility four days
  before, the expressed fear of losing future business, the demanded call on the morning of
  the vote, the delayed opening and the prompt closing of the polls.
- **The use of corporate funds is the decisive wrong.** HP management "purportedly used the
  shareholders' own money (in the form of corporate funds) to buy votes in opposition to HP
  shareholders who did not favor the merger," which "tipped the balance in favor of HP
  management's view of how the vote should turn out and made it proportionally more
  difficult for shareholders opposing the merger to defeat the transaction. In my opinion,
  that is an improper use of corporate assets by a board to **interfere with the
  shareholder franchise**."
- "Whether the shareholders disagreed with, did not believe, or even did not understand the
  information presented to them by HP management about the proposed merger, it was **the
  right of the shareholders to cast their votes** on the proposed merger without
  impermissible interference from HP management."
- Each case is evaluated on its own merits, and "[i]t is certainly possible for management
  to enter into vote-buying arrangements with **salutary purposes**." Motion to dismiss
  **denied**; at trial the plaintiffs carry "the significant burden" of proving coercion and
  that the switch was not made for independent business reasons.

**Rejected**

- *Vote-buying claims are disfavoured in Delaware as a basis for disenfranchising
  stockholders and overturning a vote.* "There does not... appear to be an obvious
  predisposition on the part of the Court one way or another toward vote-buying claims" —
  some have succeeded and some have not.
- *It is the plaintiffs who seek to disenfranchise stockholders, by asking the court to void
  Deutsche Bank's proxies.* The court's focus is on the possible deleterious effect on the
  franchise of **the other** stockholders, who are the ones at risk of being defrauded.
- *A cognisable claim requires a binding voting agreement — something contractual — between
  the buyer and the stockholder.* "Of course there must be **some kind of agreement**...
  for the issue to arise in the first place," but it need not be contractual. Cognisability
  is tied to **actual voting** in accordance with the purportedly illegal arrangement.
- *A plaintiff must show that a majority of all outstanding shares was obligated to vote in
  favour as a result of the buying.* Less than a majority can be decisive, and if voiding
  the tainted votes changes the result, the defrauded holders may sue.
- *No corporate funds were used, because all that is alleged is a promise of "future
  business."* "That future business with Deutsche Bank would necessarily have to be paid for
  with corporate funds."

## Context

Hewlett is the outer boundary of the freedom [[Ringling Brothers v. Ringling]] announces.
Ringling says a stockholder may vote from whim or caprice or for personal profit "so long as
he violates no duty owed his fellow shareholders," and Hewlett is the case about that final
clause. The reconciliation the opinion offers is clean: **a stockholder selling his own vote
spends his own asset; management buying votes spends everyone's.**

That is also how the case coexists with **§ 218(c)**, which expressly validates voting
agreements. Section 218(c) is about holders binding themselves to one another. Schreiber's
intrinsic fairness test polices arrangements where a stockholder receives "consideration
personal to the stockholder" in exchange for a vote — the personal-consideration element is
what separates a pooling agreement from vote buying.

Note what the court does **not** do. It does not hold vote buying unlawful, does not hold
that management may never buy votes, and does not resolve anything on the merits. Schreiber
itself upheld a management vote-buying arrangement because the transaction was submitted to
a ratifying vote of disinterested stockholders. **The absence of any such cleansing device
here is what leaves the claim alive**, and it is the practical lesson: a management
arrangement that touches the franchise needs an independent ratification to survive.

The case belongs to the franchise-protection line that dominates the next session.
[[Schnell v. Chris-Craft Industries]] condemns using the corporate machinery for inequitable
purposes; [[Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp]] condemns board action whose primary purpose
is to thwart a stockholder vote; Hewlett condemns spending corporate money to change how a
vote comes out. Chancellor Allen's formulation in Blasius describes exactly the line Hewlett
draws: "there is a vast difference between expending corporate funds to **inform** the
electorate and exercising power for the primary purpose of **foreclosing** effective
shareholder action."

Two features of the posture limit how much the opinion settles. It is a **motion to
dismiss**, so every allegation is assumed true, and the court says twice that the plaintiffs
face a significant evidentiary burden at trial. And the inspector had not certified the
result, so nobody yet knew the margin. The case is therefore best used for its **standard**
rather than for its outcome.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Action filed nine days after the March 19, 2002 special meeting, seeking a declaration
  that the merger was not validly approved because votes had been procured by coercion,
  intimidation or enticement.
- Decided on **HP's motion to dismiss**, so the well-pleaded allegations are taken as true.
- Motion denied as to the vote-buying claim.
- The posture matters twice. The court is deciding only whether a claim exists, and the
  independent inspector had not certified the vote, so the actual margin — and therefore
  whether the 17 million votes mattered — remained unknown.

### Facts

Hewlett-Packard and Compaq, the second and third largest computer makers in the United
States, agreed on September 4, 2001 to combine. Compaq stockholders would receive 0.6325 HP
shares for each Compaq share, ending with roughly 35.7% of the combined company; HP holders
would keep their shares but see their ownership percentage considerably diluted.
Consummation required a majority vote of HP stockholders voting at a meeting where a
majority of outstanding shares was present.

The market reacted badly. HP's stock fell 18.7% on announcement, from $23.21 to $18.87 —
about $8.5 billion — and by early November was down 27.2%, against a 9.9% gain in an index
of comparable companies.

Walter Hewlett, son of one founder, announced on November 6 that he, the William R. Hewlett
Revocable Trust, the William and Flora Hewlett Foundation and his two sisters would all vote
against. David Woodley Packard, son of the other founder, said the same that day, and the
Packard Foundation followed. Together that bloc held about 18% of HP's voting shares. The
Hewlett Parties solicited proxies in opposition by a definitive proxy statement dated
February 5, 2002, and waged a vigorous contest.

The disputed events span four days. On or before March 15 the proxy committee of Deutsche
Asset Management completed an independent review and decided to vote against the merger, and
Deutsche Bank submitted its proxies voting 25 million shares against. That same day, HP
closed a new multi-billion dollar credit facility to which Deutsche Bank had been added as a
co-arranger. By March 18 Deutsche Bank was concerned that HP's reaction to the adverse vote
would end its ongoing and hoped-for business with HP. On the morning of March 19, allegedly
at HP management's demand, a telephone conference was held between Deutsche Bank and HP
management, after which Deutsche Bank switched as many as 17 million votes to favour the
merger.

The plaintiffs pointed to the choreography at the meeting itself: Fiorina, HP's chief
executive and board chair, delayed the scheduled opening while waiting for word from
Deutsche Bank, and announced the closing of the polls promptly after apparently receiving
word on the podium that the vote had switched.

HP had 1,941,391,000 shares outstanding as of the record date and reported approval by an
extremely thin margin. The independent inspector had not informed either party of any
preliminary results and was not expected to certify until late April.

### Issue

- What standard governs a claim that votes were bought, given that § 218(c) expressly
  validates stockholder voting agreements.
- Whether a plaintiff must plead a contractually binding obligation to vote in a particular
  way.
- Whether a plaintiff must show that a majority of outstanding shares was obligated to vote
  in favour as a result of the arrangement.
- Whether the alleged facts state a claim that HP management procured Deutsche Bank's switch
  in a way that had a materially adverse effect on the franchise of the other stockholders.

### Holding

- **On the standard: Schreiber governs.** Per se illegality where the object is to defraud
  or disenfranchise other stockholders; otherwise voidable, subject to intrinsic fairness —
  with a sharp distinction between a stockholder selling his own vote and management buying
  votes with corporate assets.
- **On the contract requirement: none.** Some agreement or understanding must exist, but it
  need not be contractually binding; what matters is that shares were in fact voted as
  directed in exchange for consideration personal to the stockholder.
- **On the majority requirement: none.** Less than a majority can decide an election, and a
  claim lies if voiding the tainted votes would change the result.
- **On the merits of the pleading: a claim is stated.** The sequence supports a reasonable
  inference of enticement or coercion, and management's use of corporate funds to buy votes
  on a transaction it was required to submit to stockholders, with no protective device in
  place, is an improper interference with the franchise.
- **Disposition:** motion to dismiss denied; the plaintiffs bear a significant burden at
  trial.

### Rule

- Vote buying is illegal per se if the object or purpose is to defraud or in some way
  disenfranchise the other stockholders.
- Absent such a purpose, vote buying is a voidable transaction subject to a test for
  intrinsic fairness, because it is so easily susceptible of abuse.
- Stockholders are free to do whatever they want with their own votes, including selling
  them.
- Management may not use corporate assets to buy votes in a hotly contested proxy contest
  about an extraordinary transaction, unless it can show the activity has no deleterious
  effect on the corporate franchise.
- A contractually binding obligation is not a prerequisite; the threshold is that in exchange
  for consideration personal to the stockholder, a stockholder agreed to vote or in fact
  voted as directed by another.
- A plaintiff need not show that a majority of outstanding shares was obligated to vote in a
  particular way.
- Management may enter into vote-buying arrangements with salutary purposes, and each case is
  evaluated on its own merits.

### Reasoning

*(Chandler, Ch.)*

- The court begins by refusing to let § 218(c) swallow Schreiber, and resolves the apparent
  conflict by identity rather than by doctrine: § 218(c) validates arrangements **among
  stockholders**, and Schreiber concerned **management** buying votes with corporate money.
- That distinction carries the whole opinion. A stockholder selling a vote disposes of
  something he owns; management buying votes spends an asset in which every stockholder has a
  common interest, and spends it to defeat some of those same stockholders.
- On the contract requirement the court reasons from the purpose of the doctrine. The
  protected persons are the **unsuspecting other stockholders**, and their injury does not
  depend on whether the bad actors made their bargain enforceable against each other.
  Cognisability therefore attaches to **actual voting**, not to formal obligation.
- The majority requirement is rejected on the same logic, with an arithmetic point: a
  decisive bloc need not be a majority, and a rule keyed to majorities would immunise exactly
  the close contests in which buying matters most.
- The inference of coercion is built from timing rather than from any admission — the credit
  facility on the very day of the adverse vote, the expressed fear three days later, the
  demanded call on the morning of the meeting, and the delay and prompt closing of the polls.
- The structural aggravator is that the merger was one of the few decisions a board **cannot
  make alone**. If the allegations are true, management suspected it would lose the one
  decision the statute reserved to the stockholders, and spent their money to win it.
- The absence of a ratifying vote of independent stockholders is noted specifically, which
  points back to how Schreiber itself came out and tells practitioners what would have saved
  the arrangement.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Single-judge Chancery opinion; no dissent.

The standard the court applies is easier to state than to run. Schreiber's intrinsic
fairness test asks whether a vote-buying arrangement was fair, but fairness to whom, measured
how, is left open — and Chandler's own formulation shifts between "deleterious effect on the
corporate franchise" and "materially adverse effect on the franchise of the other
shareholders." On these alleged facts the answer is clear enough, because corporate money was
spent to defeat the holders who were paying for it. On softer facts, where management offers
an accommodation with a genuine business rationale, the test gives little guidance, and the
court's concession that "[i]t is certainly possible for management to enter into vote-buying
arrangements with salutary purposes" is an acknowledgment of the gap rather than a resolution
of it.

### Cold-call notes

- **Lead with the who-is-buying distinction.** Stockholders may sell their own votes;
  management may not buy votes with corporate assets in a contested fight over an
  extraordinary transaction. That single line reconciles Hewlett with Ringling and with
  § 218(c).
- **State Schreiber in two parts.** Per se illegal if the object is to defraud or
  disenfranchise; otherwise voidable and tested for intrinsic fairness. Do not state only the
  per se half.
- **Know the two elements the court refuses to require.** No binding contract, and no
  majority of outstanding shares. Both rejections follow from the same premise — the injury
  runs to the other stockholders, not between the parties to the arrangement.
- **Have the threshold formulation ready.** Facts supporting a reasonable inference that in
  exchange for "consideration personal to the stockholder," a stockholder agreed to vote or
  in fact voted as directed by another.
- **Name the missing safeguard.** No ratifying vote of independent stockholders. Schreiber
  upheld a management arrangement that had one, and the contrast is the practical takeaway.
- **Be ready for "wasn't this just ordinary commercial pressure?"** The honest answer is that
  the court never decides it — this is a pleading-stage ruling, the plaintiffs bear a
  significant burden at trial, and HP is entitled to show that Deutsche Bank switched for
  independent business reasons.
- **Answer the corporate-funds objection.** HP argued only "future business" was promised.
  The court's reply is one sentence: that future business "would necessarily have to be paid
  for with corporate funds."
- **Connect to the franchise cases the next day.** Schnell, Blasius and Hewlett are three
  routes to the same principle — informing the electorate is legitimate, foreclosing or
  purchasing its decision is not.
- **Numbers worth holding.** 1,941,391,000 shares outstanding; 25 million voted against, 17
  million switched; the Hewlett bloc at about 18%; a margin reported as under 1%; an 18.7%
  price drop worth roughly $8.5 billion; four days between the credit facility and the vote.
