## Rule

Stockholders may validly contract with each other about how they will vote their own
shares, including by agreeing to submit deadlocks to an arbitrator — such a **pooling
agreement** is not a voting trust and need not comply with the voting trust statute —
but absent an express grant, the agreement creates only a **promise between the
parties**, not a power in one to vote the other's shares.

## Facts

- The three holders of 1,000 shares were Edith Ringling (315), Aubrey Haley (315) and
  John North (370), and the shares **could be voted cumulatively** for a seven-member
  board. The arithmetic is the whole case: acting alone each woman could elect two
  directors; acting together they could elect **five**, and North could elect only two.
- To elect five, the two women had to divide their combined 4,410 votes among **five
  different candidates**, with at least one candidate receiving votes from both. That
  requirement is what makes partial compliance destructive rather than partial.
- Paragraph 2 obliged each party to "consult and confer" and to "**act jointly** in
  exercising such voting rights in accordance with such agreement as they may reach."
- Paragraph 3 provided that on failure to agree, "the question in disagreement shall be
  submitted for arbitration to Karl D. Loos... **and his decision thereon shall be
  binding upon the parties**."
- **Nothing in the agreement authorised Loos to vote the shares or to compel anyone to
  vote them.** Loos was not a party, took no shares, and could be replaced at will. The
  absence of any enforcement machinery decides the remedy.
- The parties had voted their **own** shares by mutual understanding in 1943, 1944 and
  1945, and at the 1946 meeting Mrs. Ringling "did not attempt to cast a ballot in
  exercise of any voting rights of Mrs. Haley." Course of performance confirms that
  neither held a power over the other's shares.
- Loos directed each woman to cast 882 for herself, 882 for her nominee, and **441 for
  Dunn** — the shared fifth candidate. Mrs. Ringling complied; Mr. Haley instead cast
  1,103 and 1,102 for the Haleys and **nothing for Dunn**.
- The stock had been in a **voting trust** from 1938 until 1947, so the parties knew the
  statutory form and chose a different one. *Background, but it defeats any suggestion
  that the pooling agreement was an unwitting evasion.*
- The agreement ran **ten years** and recited the object of "assuring for the respective
  corporations good management and such participation therein by the members of the
  Ringling family as the experience, capacity and ability of each may warrant."
- North was **not a party** to the agreement. That fact shapes the remedy: his votes
  stand untouched.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The agreement does **not empower the arbitrator to carry his directions into effect**.
  There is "no express delegation or grant of power to do so, either by authorizing him
  to vote the shares or to compel either party to vote them in accordance with his
  directions," and "[t]he power to enforce a decision does not seem a necessary or usual
  incident of such a function."
- Consequently "no decision of the arbitrator could ever be enforced if both parties to
  the agreement were unwilling that it be enforced," so any control reposed in the
  arbitrator "is substantially limited... in that it is subject to the **overriding power
  of the parties themselves**."
- Nor did either party acquire power to vote the **other's** shares. "[E]ach party
  promised the other to exercise **her own** voting rights in accordance with the
  arbitrator's decision." The agreement's language, and the parties' own practice at
  earlier meetings, both point to action by each rather than by one for the other.
- The voting trust statute "does not purport to deal with agreements whereby shareholders
  attempt to bind each other as to how they shall vote their shares. Various forms of such
  **pooling agreements**, as they are sometimes called, have been held valid and have been
  distinguished from voting trusts."
- The agreement is valid and not an attempted evasion. "Generally speaking, a shareholder
  may exercise **wide liberality of judgment** in the matter of voting, and it is not
  objectionable that his motives may be for personal profit, or determined by whims or
  caprice, **so long as he violates no duty owed his fellow shareholders**. The ownership
  of voting stock imposes no legal duty to vote at all."
- "A group of shareholders may, without impropriety, vote their respective shares so as to
  obtain advantages of concerted action. They may lawfully contract with each other to
  vote in the future in such way as they, or a majority of their group, from time to time
  determine." Provisions for deadlock "seem unobjectionable," and this one "is plainly
  designed as a deadlock-breaking measure."
- Mutual promises supply consideration, the arbitrator's good faith is unchallenged, and
  Mrs. Haley's failure to vote as directed was a **breach of contract**.
- Partial compliance is no defence. Loos's directions "were part of a **single plan or
  course of action** for the voting of the shares of both parties... calculated to utilize
  an advantage of joint action by them which would bring about the election of an
  additional director," and Mrs. Haley's votes "frustrate[] that plan to such an extent
  that it should not be treated as a partial performance."
- **Remedy: rejection, not specific performance.** "The Court of Chancery may, in a review
  of an election, **reject votes** of a registered shareholder where his voting of them is
  found to be in violation of rights of another person." Mrs. Haley's votes are not
  counted; North's stand, because "her rights arose under a contract to which Mr. North
  was not a party."
- The election is not declared invalid. The inspectors' return is corrected to reject Mrs.
  Haley's votes and to declare elected the six persons for whom North and Mrs. Ringling
  voted, leaving one vacancy the court declines to fill.

**Rejected**

- *The Vice Chancellor was right that the agreement makes the willing party "an implied
  agent possessing the irrevocable proxy of the recalcitrant party."* No such power was
  bargained for. That a power to vote the other's shares "might be a relatively more
  effective or convenient means of enforcing a decision... would not justify implying a
  delegation of the power in the absence of some indication that the parties bargained
  for that means."
- *Delaware law forbids any agreement or device by which voting power is irrevocably
  separated from ownership except a statutory voting trust or a proxy coupled with an
  interest.* That "sweeping formulation would impugn well-recognized means by which a
  shareholder may effectively confer his voting rights upon others while retaining various
  other rights" — including a charter amendment giving another class exclusive voting
  rights when dividends go unpaid. It is also "inconsistent with their concession that
  proxies coupled with an interest may be irrevocable, for the statute contains nothing
  about such proxies."
- *The agreement is an evasion of the voting trust statute.* The statute authorises the
  deposit or transfer of stock in trust to vest the right to vote in a transferee, and its
  requirements must be met to accomplish **that** relationship. This agreement transfers
  nothing and vests nothing in Loos.
- *All votes cast at the meeting were cast by registered holders, so they must be counted
  as cast.* Chancery may reject a registered holder's votes where the voting violates
  another person's rights.

## Context

Ringling is the foundational distinction between a **voting trust** and a **pooling
agreement**, and Delaware has since codified the second. **8 Del. C. § 218(a)** governs
voting trusts — stock is actually deposited with or transferred to a trustee, new
certificates issue, and a copy of the agreement goes to the registered office open to
inspection. **§ 218(c)**, added later, blesses the pooling agreement directly: "[a]n
agreement between 2 or more stockholders, if in writing and signed by the parties thereto,
may provide that in exercising any voting rights, the shares held by them **shall be voted
as provided by the agreement, or as the parties may agree, or as determined in accordance
with a procedure agreed upon by them**." The last clause is Ringling's arbitrator.
Section 218(d) adds that the section does not invalidate any voting agreement or
irrevocable proxy "which is not otherwise illegal."

The practical lesson is a **drafting** lesson, and it is the reason the case is taught.
Mrs. Ringling won on validity and lost on effectiveness, because the agreement said what
the parties must do and not who could do it for them. A modern pooling agreement solves
this with an **irrevocable proxy** under § 212(e), which is irrevocable "if it states that
it is irrevocable and if, and only as long as, it is coupled with an interest sufficient
in law to support an irrevocable power" — and which "may be made irrevocable regardless of
whether the interest with which it is coupled is an interest in the stock itself or an
interest in the corporation generally."

The **remedy** is the second lesson and is easy to miss. Chancery did not order Mrs. Haley
to vote as directed; it struck her votes. Rejection is a blunt instrument — it produced a
six-member board and an unfilled seat — but it is the remedy available where no one holds
the power to cast the shares.

Note the limiting phrase in the validity holding: a stockholder may vote for any reason
"so long as he **violates no duty owed his fellow shareholders**." That reservation is the
doorway to [[Hewlett v. Hewlett-Packard]], assigned the same day, where the question is
whether votes were procured by corporate management in a way that injures the franchise of
everyone else. Ringling says stockholders own their votes; Hewlett says management may not
buy them with the stockholders' own money.

The case also sits against the board-centric default that runs through the rest of the
course. Stockholders may contract freely about **their own** voting, which is why the
agreement here is enforceable at all, but they may not by bylaw take over what § 141(a)
gives the board — see [[Gorman v. Salamone]] and
[[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]]. The dividing line is that a
pooling agreement allocates the stockholders' own rights among themselves.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Review in the Court of Chancery of a contested election of directors at the 1946 annual
  meeting, on Mrs. Ringling's petition.
- The Vice Chancellor upheld the agreement as a lawful stock pooling agreement and held
  that on a refusal to comply it made the willing party "an implied agent possessing the
  irrevocable proxy of the recalcitrant party," ordering a new election before a master.
- The Supreme Court affirmed validity, **reversed on the agency theory**, and modified the
  relief to a rejection of Mrs. Haley's votes.
- The posture explains why the case is about remedies as much as about validity. An
  election review lets Chancery correct the inspectors' return, which is a different and
  narrower tool than specific performance.

### Facts

The corporation had 1,000 shares outstanding, held by three people: Edith Conway Ringling
with 315, Aubrey Ringling Haley with 315 individually and as executrix and legatee of her
deceased husband, and John Ringling North with 370. The 1946 annual meeting was to elect
the entire seven-member board, and the shares could be voted cumulatively.

The arithmetic governed everything. With seven seats, each woman controlled 2,205 votes and
North 2,590. Acting alone, each woman could elect two directors. Acting together they
commanded 4,410 votes, enough to give 882 votes to each of five candidates — and North's
2,590 could not be split to give more than two candidates 882 each. But the five-director
outcome required their combined votes to be divided among **five different candidates**,
with at least one candidate supported by both.

The two women signed their agreement in 1941, replacing an earlier 1934 arrangement. They
recited that each owned 300 shares deposited under a 1938 voting trust due to terminate in
1947, plus 15 shares individually, and that they wished "to continue to act jointly in all
matters relating to their stock ownership." The operative terms were a right of first
refusal on any sale; an undertaking to consult, confer and act jointly in exercising voting
rights; submission of any disagreement to Karl Loos as arbitrator, whose "decision thereon
shall be binding upon the parties"; an undertaking to execute such voting trusts or other
instruments as they might later think advisable; and a ten-year term. Loos was an attorney
who had represented both women since 1937.

The arrangement worked for three years. In 1943, 1944 and 1945 the parties voted their
shares by mutual understanding and elected five of the seven directors each time.

Weeks before the 1946 meeting they agreed that Mrs. Ringling would elect herself and her
son and Mrs. Haley would elect herself and her husband, but they could not agree on a fifth
director. On the eve of the meeting Mr. Haley, representing his wife who was ill, indicated
he would move to adjourn for sixty days to allow more time; the next morning he said that
because of something Mrs. Ringling had done, he would not consent to a postponement.

Mrs. Ringling then demanded that Loos arbitrate. At the meeting Loos read the demand and
directed that both blocks be voted for a sixty-day adjournment. Mrs. Ringling so voted; Mr.
Haley and North voted against; the chairman ruled the Haley shares could not be voted
contrary to the direction and declared the adjournment carried. The meeting nevertheless
proceeded to an election, Mrs. Ringling participating "without prejudice to her position."

Loos directed each woman to cast 882 for herself, 882 for her nominee, and 441 for Dunn, a
long-serving director. Mrs. Ringling complied. Mr. Haley cast 1,103 for Mrs. Haley and
1,102 for himself, giving Dunn nothing. North cast 864, 863 and 863 for Woods, Griffin and
himself.

The chairman declared elected the five Loos candidates plus Woods and North. The Haley–
North group disputed Dunn's election and insisted Griffin had won instead. A directors'
meeting followed in which Dunn and Griffin each purported to vote as directors for
competing slates of officers.

### Issue

- Whether an agreement between two stockholders to act jointly in voting, with deadlocks
  submitted to a binding arbitrator, is valid, or whether it is void as an attempt to
  separate voting power from ownership outside the voting trust statute.
- Whether the agreement empowers the arbitrator to enforce his own decision, or empowers
  either party to vote the other's shares.
- What relief is available where one party votes contrary to the arbitrator's direction.

### Holding

- **On validity: the agreement is a lawful pooling agreement.** It is not a voting trust,
  does not evade the statute, and offends no rule of law or public policy; a deadlock-
  breaking arbitration provision is reasonable.
- **On the arbitrator's power: none beyond deciding.** He was given no authority to vote
  the shares or to compel anyone to vote them, so his decision binds only as a contractual
  promise and is unenforceable if both parties refuse to act on it.
- **On agency: neither party may vote the other's shares.** Each promised to exercise her
  own voting rights in accordance with the decision, and the parties' own practice
  confirms it.
- **On breach: Mrs. Haley breached**, and voting for two of the three directed candidates
  is not partial performance, because the direction was a single plan whose value depended
  on the shared fifth candidate.
- **On remedy: rejection of her votes.** The election stands, North's votes stand, Mrs.
  Haley's votes are struck, the six candidates supported by Mrs. Ringling and North are
  declared elected, and one seat is left vacant.

### Rule

- A shareholder may exercise wide liberality of judgment in voting, for personal profit or
  from whim or caprice, so long as he violates no duty owed his fellow shareholders, and
  owns no duty to vote at all.
- Shareholders may vote their respective shares so as to obtain the advantages of concerted
  action, and may lawfully contract to vote in the future as they or a majority of their
  group determine.
- Reasonable provisions for breaking a deadlock, including submission to an arbitrator, are
  unobjectionable.
- The voting trust statute governs the deposit or transfer of stock to vest voting rights in
  a trustee, and does not reach agreements by which shareholders bind each other as to how
  they will vote their own shares.
- An agreement that does not expressly delegate the power to vote another's shares, or to
  enforce an arbitrator's decision, confers no such power by implication.
- A promise to vote in accordance with an arbitrator's decision is a valid contract
  supported by mutual promises.
- The Court of Chancery reviewing an election may reject a registered holder's votes where
  the voting violates another person's rights.

### Reasoning

*(Pearson, J.)*

- The court reads the agreement functionally rather than formally, asking first what role
  the parties actually assigned Loos. Because paragraph 2's object is joint action, the
  arbitrator exists only to break stalemates, and enforcement "does not seem a necessary or
  usual incident of such a function."
- Four facts confirm the narrow reading: Loos is not a party; no shares or interest were
  transferred to him; the parties could replace him at will; and what he does is for their
  benefit, not his. "[T]he parties sought to bind each other, but to be bound only to each
  other."
- On the agency question the court declines to imply a power merely because it would be
  convenient. Convenience is not a substitute for bargaining, and the parties' own conduct
  across three prior meetings — each voting her own shares — settles the construction.
- The attack on validity is answered by showing where the defendants' rule would lead. A
  ban on every separation of voting power from ownership would invalidate class voting
  provisions in charters, and cannot be squared with the concededly valid irrevocable proxy
  coupled with an interest, which the statute never mentions.
- The statute is then read for what it does rather than for what it forbids: it prescribes
  requirements for accomplishing a particular relationship, so those requirements bind
  anyone seeking that relationship — and nobody here sought it.
- The refusal to treat Mrs. Haley's votes as partial performance rests on the cumulative-
  voting arithmetic. The plan's entire value lay in concentrating both blocks on a shared
  fifth candidate, so voting for the first two and abandoning the third destroys the benefit
  rather than delivering part of it.
- On remedy the court keeps the contract's boundaries. Mrs. Ringling's rights run against
  Mrs. Haley alone, so North — a stranger to the agreement — keeps every vote he cast, and
  the correction is confined to striking the breaching party's ballot.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The remedy is the unsatisfying part, and the court half-acknowledges it by leaving the
seventh seat unresolved and inviting a later application. Striking the breaching party's
votes vindicates the contract by subtraction: it denies Mrs. Haley the two directors she
tried to elect, but it cannot produce the five directors the agreement was designed to
deliver, because nobody had the power to cast her shares for Dunn. A plaintiff who wanted
that outcome needed an irrevocable proxy in the document, not a better argument in court.

### Cold-call notes

- **Separate validity from enforceability immediately.** The agreement is valid; the
  arbitrator's decision is binding as a promise; and yet nothing in the document lets
  anyone make Mrs. Haley's shares vote. That gap is the case.
- **Know the voting trust / pooling distinction and the modern statute.** A voting trust
  under § 218(a) transfers the stock to a trustee with filing and inspection requirements;
  a pooling agreement under § 218(c) leaves ownership alone and binds the holders to each
  other. Ringling predates § 218(c), which codifies exactly what it approved — including
  "as determined in accordance with a procedure agreed upon by them."
- **Have the drafting fix ready.** An **irrevocable proxy** under § 212(e), stated to be
  irrevocable and coupled with an interest — which may be an interest in the corporation
  generally, not only in the stock. That single addition would have changed the outcome.
- **Be able to do the cumulative voting arithmetic.** 315 shares × 7 seats = 2,205 votes
  each; 4,410 combined, enough for 882 to each of five candidates; North's 2,590 covers at
  most two at that level. Then say why it requires a **shared** fifth candidate — that is
  what makes partial compliance destructive.
- **Quote the liberality passage with its limit.** A stockholder may vote from whim or
  caprice "so long as he violates no duty owed his fellow shareholders." The final clause is
  where Hewlett begins.
- **Expect a question on the remedy.** Rejection of votes in an election review, not
  specific performance. Note that North, a non-party, keeps his votes, and that the
  correction left a vacancy the court would not fill.
- **Numbers worth holding.** 1,000 shares; 315 / 315 / 370; seven seats; 2,205 and 2,590
  votes; 882 and 441 as directed; 1,103 and 1,102 as cast; a ten-year agreement; a voting
  trust running 1938 to 1947.
