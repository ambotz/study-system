## Rule

A board may adopt a shareholder rights plan **before any threat exists**, under
8 Del. C. §§ 157, 151 and 141(a), and its adoption receives the business judgment
rule — but the plan is not self-executing: the decision whether to redeem the rights
when an actual offer arrives is judged separately, under the same fiduciary
standards.

## Facts

- Household adopted the plan by a **fourteen to two vote**, "not... during a battle
  with a corporate raider, but as a **preventive mechanism** to ward off future
  advances." The absence of a pending bid is the fact that frames the entire
  authority question.
- Management had begun considering the company's takeover vulnerability as early as
  **February 1984**, initially by looking at charter amendments. The deliberation
  predates any specific threat and supports the informed-adoption finding.
- Two triggers: announcement of a **tender offer for 30%** of Household's shares, and
  **acquisition of 20%** by any single entity or group.
- On the **30% trigger** the rights issue, become immediately exercisable to buy
  1/100 of a share of new preferred for $100, and remain **redeemable by the board
  for $.50 per right**. Redeemability is what keeps the pill a bargaining device
  rather than a bar.
- On the **20% trigger** the rights issue and become **non-redeemable**.
- The **flip-over**: if a right is not exercised for preferred and a merger or
  consolidation then occurs, each right buys **$200 of the offeror's common stock for
  $100**. That provision "is at the heart of this controversy" — the dilution falls on
  the acquiror, not on the target.
- The plan was documented in a **48-page Rights Agreement**.
- The plan "does not destroy the assets of the corporation," produces no outflow of
  money, does not impair financial flexibility, does not dilute earnings per share,
  carries no adverse tax consequences, and had not affected the market price. *The
  absence of corporate cost is what distinguishes the pill from every earlier
  defence.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- The business judgment rule governs the adoption, and "[m]uch of this issue has been
  decided by our recent decision in **Unocal**."
- Adoption **in advance of any threat does not forfeit the rule** — to the contrary,
  "pre-planning for the contingency of a hostile takeover might reduce the risk that,
  under the pressure of a takeover bid, management will fail to exercise reasonable
  judgment. Therefore, in reviewing a pre-planned defensive mechanism it seems even
  more appropriate to apply the business judgment rule."
- The rule "can only sustain corporate decision making or transactions that are
  within the power or authority of the Board," so **authority must be established
  first**.
- **Statutory authority exists.** The rights are authorised by **8 Del. C. § 157**,
  and the preferred stock underlying them by **§ 151**; the court also notes "the
  inherent powers of the Board conferred by 8 Del. C. § 141(a), concerning the
  management of the corporation's 'business and affairs'," as additional authority.
- The plan does not usurp the stockholders' right to receive tender offers and does
  not fundamentally restrict proxy contests. "[T]here are numerous methods to
  successfully launch a hostile tender offer."
- **The plan is not absolute.** "When the Household Board of Directors is faced with a
  tender offer and a request to redeem the Rights, they will not be able to
  arbitrarily reject the offer. They will be held to the same fiduciary standards any
  other board of directors would be held to in deciding to adopt a defensive
  mechanism, the same standard as they were held to in originally approving the
  Rights Plan."
- Compared with other defences, the pill "does less harm to the value structure of the
  corporation," since other mechanisms increase corporate debt — the court names
  selling a prize asset, paying greenmail in [[Cheff v. Mathes]], and the
  discriminatory self-tender in [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]].
- "There is little change in the governance structure as a result of the adoption of
  the Rights Plan. The Board does not now have unfettered discretion in refusing to
  redeem the Rights. The Board has no more discretion in refusing to redeem the Rights
  than it does in enacting any defensive mechanism."
- The directors adopted the plan in the good faith belief that it was necessary to
  protect Household from **coercive acquisition techniques**, were informed as to its
  details, and demonstrated it was reasonable in relation to the threat posed.
- The holding is expressly provisional as to future conduct: "the ultimate response to
  an actual takeover bid must be judged by the Directors' actions at that time, and
  nothing we say here relieves them of their basic fundamental duties to the
  corporation and its stockholders." **Affirmed.**

**Rejected**

- *No provision of the DGCL authorises the issuance of these rights.* Sufficient
  authority exists in § 157 for the rights and § 151 for the underlying preferred,
  reinforced by the board's inherent § 141(a) powers.
- *The board is unauthorised to usurp stockholders' rights to receive hostile tender
  offers.* The plan does not strip that right; numerous methods remain by which a
  hostile offer can succeed, and the board cannot arbitrarily refuse to redeem.
- *The board is unauthorised to fundamentally restrict stockholders' right to conduct
  a proxy contest.* Rejected on the same basis.
- *The plan works a "fundamental transfer of power from the stockholders to the
  directors," and that transfer is itself unauthorised.* "The Rights Plan will result
  in no more of a structural change than any other defensive mechanism adopted by a
  board of directors," and it does less damage to the corporation than the
  alternatives, which cost real money.
- *The plan is so effective that it makes the corporation completely safe from hostile
  tender offers, and therefore differs in kind from other defences.* "[L]ikewise
  without merit," because numerous methods to launch a hostile offer remain.

## Context

Moran is where the modern takeover era begins, and the reasoning is thinner than its
importance. The court validates a device the DGCL nowhere mentions by assembling
§ 157 (rights and options), § 151 (blank-check preferred) and § 141(a), which is the
same move [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] makes months earlier when it says Delaware law
"is not static" and that statutory silence "does not mean that it is prohibited."

**The mechanics worth knowing.** A rights plan issues one right per common share as a
dividend, exercisable only on a trigger and redeemable by the board for pennies until
then. Moran's pill is a **flip-over**: once a merger follows the trigger, each right
buys the *acquiror's* stock at half price. The later and now-standard feature is the
**flip-in**, under which the rights buy the *target's* own stock at half price,
diluting the acquiror without requiring it to merge. The economic point is the same
either way — the pill makes crossing the threshold ruinously expensive, so nobody
ever triggers one, and the pill's entire function is to force the bidder to negotiate
with the board or run a proxy contest.

**The two assurances Moran gives are the ones that matter later, and only one held
up.** The first is that redemption is separately reviewable, which remains true and is
the hook for every later pill case including [[Air Products v. Airgas]]. The second is
that "there are numerous methods to successfully launch a hostile tender offer" — and
this is the claim that did not survive contact with the **staggered board**. As the
casebook note puts it, acquirors responded to the pill by coupling tender offers with
proxy contests to install a board that would redeem; targets responded by classifying
their boards so that gaining a majority takes two annual meetings. The combination is
"an enormous obstacle to a takeover," and Selectica squarely holds that a classified
board plus a rights plan is **not preclusive** — a holding Chancellor Chandler follows
in Airgas while writing a footnote saying Delaware law "would be more credible" if the
Supreme Court admitted what its later rulings have done to Moran.

**The limits Delaware did impose came at the far end.** A pill that binds a future
board cannot stand: dead-hand, no-hand and slow-hand provisions, which make the
rights unredeemable by newly elected directors, are draconian, coercive and
preclusive, and were struck down in Carmody v. Toll Brothers and Mentor Graphics v.
Quickturn. The principle is that a bidder must retain some avenue to the stockholders
and the stockholders some mechanism to reverse the defence by electing new directors.
That is what makes "just say never" unlawful even though "just say no for now" is not.

Moran's pill is also the one blessed in [[Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes]] the
following year. Revlon upholds the Note Purchase Rights Plan precisely because it
worked as Moran contemplated — it drove Pantry Pride's bidding from $42 to $58 and
was then redeemed. The pill is not the problem in Revlon; the lock-up granted after
the company was already for sale is.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from a Court of Chancery decision upholding Household's Rights Plan, brought
  by a director and stockholder challenging the plan's adoption.
- Decided on a facial challenge to the **adoption** of the plan, with **no hostile bid
  pending**.
- That posture is the reason the opinion reads as permissive. The court is not
  reviewing a refusal to redeem against a real offer, and it says twice that such a
  refusal will be judged separately when it happens. Everything difficult about the
  pill was deferred to a later case, which turned out to be Airgas twenty-six years
  on.

### Facts

Household International's management became concerned about the company's
vulnerability as a takeover target as early as February 1984, and began by considering
charter amendments that would make a takeover more difficult. On August 14, 1984 the
board adopted the Preferred Share Purchase Rights Plan by a vote of fourteen to two.
Household was not under attack; the plan was adopted as a preventive mechanism against
future advances.

The plan itself ran to a 48-page Rights Agreement. Each common share carried one
right. Two events could activate the rights: the announcement of a tender offer for
30% of Household's shares, or the acquisition of 20% by any single entity or group.

The consequences differ by trigger, and the difference is the design. On the 30%
trigger the rights issue and become immediately exercisable to purchase 1/100 of a
share of new preferred stock for $100, and the board may still **redeem** them for
$.50 each. On the 20% trigger the rights issue and become **non-redeemable**. If a
right is not exercised for preferred and a merger or consolidation then occurs, the
holder may exercise each right to purchase $200 of the common stock of the tender
offeror for $100 — the flip-over provision the court identifies as "at the heart of
this controversy."

Appellants, joined by the Securities and Exchange Commission, argued that the board
lacked authority to adopt the plan at all: that no DGCL provision authorises such
rights, that the board cannot usurp the stockholders' right to receive hostile tender
offers, and that the board cannot fundamentally restrict the right to conduct a proxy
contest. Household answered that § 157 authorises the rights and § 151(g) the
underlying preferred.

The court also catalogued what the plan does not do: it does not destroy corporate
assets, cause any outflow of money, impair financial flexibility, dilute earnings per
share, create adverse tax consequences, or affect the market price of Household stock.

### Issue

- Whether the business judgment rule applies to the adoption of a rights plan adopted
  in advance of any specific takeover threat.
- Whether the board had statutory authority under the DGCL to adopt the plan, a
  prerequisite to the rule's application.
- Whether the plan impermissibly strips stockholders of the right to receive hostile
  tender offers or fundamentally restricts their right to conduct a proxy contest.
- Whether the resulting transfer of power from stockholders to directors is itself
  unauthorised.

### Holding

- **On the standard: the business judgment rule applies, and pre-planning
  strengthens rather than weakens the case for it.** Adopting a defence before the
  pressure of a bid reduces the risk of a hasty judgment later.
- **On authority: the plan is authorised.** Section 157 supports the rights, § 151
  the underlying preferred, and § 141(a) supplies additional inherent power over the
  business and affairs of the corporation.
- **On the stockholders' rights: unimpaired.** Numerous methods remain by which a
  hostile offer can be launched, and the plan does not fundamentally restrict proxy
  contests.
- **On the transfer of power: no greater than any other defence, and less costly.**
  The plan works no structural change beyond what other defensive mechanisms work,
  and unlike them it imposes no cost on the corporation.
- **On future conduct: expressly reserved.** A refusal to redeem when an actual offer
  arrives will be judged by the directors' actions at that time, under the same
  fiduciary standards.
- **Disposition:** affirmed.

### Rule

- The business judgment rule applies to a board's adoption of a defensive mechanism,
  including one adopted in advance of any specific threat.
- The rule can sustain only decisions within the board's power, so statutory authority
  must be established before the rule is applied.
- Sections 151 and 157 authorise the issuance of rights and of the preferred stock
  underlying them, and § 141(a) supplies additional inherent authority.
- A rights plan does not deprive stockholders of the right to receive tender offers,
  because methods of launching a hostile offer remain available.
- A board faced with a tender offer and a request to redeem the rights cannot
  arbitrarily reject the offer, and is held to the same fiduciary standards that
  governed the plan's adoption.
- The ultimate response to an actual takeover bid is judged by the directors' actions
  at that time.

### Reasoning

*(McNeilly, J.)*

- The opinion follows Unocal's architecture: establish power first, then apply the
  standard. Because the pill is a device the statute never names, the authority
  question is the real one, and the court answers it by composition — § 157 for the
  rights, § 151 for the preferred underneath them, § 141(a) as a backstop.
- The pre-planning argument is inverted from what the challengers expected. Adopting a
  defence in calm conditions is treated as evidence of deliberation rather than as
  evidence of entrenchment, so advance adoption earns *more* deference, not less.
- The court's comparative move does most of the persuasive work. Measured against
  greenmail, a discriminatory self-tender, or selling a prize asset, the pill costs the
  corporation nothing — no debt, no asset sales, no dilution of earnings, no tax
  consequences. If those defences are lawful, a costless one is harder to condemn.
- The response to the transfer-of-power objection is that the transfer is one of
  **degree, not of kind**. Every defensive mechanism shifts some decision from
  stockholders to the board; the pill differs in efficiency, not in character.
- The reservation about redemption is what makes the rest sustainable. The court can
  bless the pill in the abstract only because it promises that the hard question —
  refusing to redeem against a real offer — remains open and separately reviewable.
- The weakest link is the assurance that "there are numerous methods to successfully
  launch a hostile tender offer." That sentence is doing load-bearing work and is
  offered without analysis.

### Dissent / concurrence

- The opinion notes the vote below and the SEC's participation as amicus, but the
  Supreme Court panel was unanimous.

The real objection is not to the result but to the empirical premise. Moran upholds
the pill on the express ground that stockholders retain avenues around it. Within a
decade the staggered board had closed the practical avenue, and Delaware's response
was to hold in Selectica that a classified board plus a pill is still not preclusive.
Chancellor Chandler says the quiet part aloud in an Airgas footnote: the law "would be
more credible if the Supreme Court acknowledged that its later rulings have modified
Moran," since "[t]he tender offer is in fact precluded and the only bypass of the pill
is electing a new board."

### Cold-call notes

- **Cite the three statutes together.** Section 157 for the rights, § 151 for the
  blank-check preferred underlying them, § 141(a) for inherent authority. The case is
  as much a statutory-authority case as a fiduciary one, and this is the session where
  §§ 151 and 157 are assigned as readings in their own right.
- **Be precise about the two triggers.** A tender offer announced for **30%**, or an
  acquisition of **20%**. Redeemable at $.50 after the first; non-redeemable after the
  second. The asymmetry is deliberate — it lets a board keep bargaining after an offer
  is announced but ends the option once someone actually accumulates a block.
- **Explain flip-over versus flip-in.** Moran's pill flips *over* into the acquiror's
  stock after a merger. The modern standard pill flips *in* to the target's stock,
  which dilutes the acquiror without requiring a merger. Knowing the difference shows
  you understand why nobody ever triggers a pill.
- **Say why pre-planning helped rather than hurt.** Deliberation without the pressure
  of a pending bid reduces the risk of a bad judgment later. The intuition runs the
  opposite way for most students.
- **Have the reservation ready, because it is the bridge to Airgas.** Adoption gets the
  business judgment rule; **refusal to redeem** is judged separately when an actual
  offer arrives, under the same standards. Airgas is that case, twenty-six years later.
- **Know what undermined the "numerous methods" premise.** The staggered board. Two
  annual meetings to replace a majority, and no bidder has ever run two successful
  proxy contests to get there. Selectica nonetheless holds the combination is not
  preclusive.
- **Name the outer limit.** Dead-hand, no-hand and slow-hand pills, which prevent a
  newly elected board from redeeming, are invalid — Carmody v. Toll Brothers and
  Mentor Graphics v. Quickturn. That is what keeps "just say never" unlawful.
- **Numbers worth holding.** Adopted 14–2; 48-page agreement; 30% and 20% triggers;
  $100 exercise price for 1/100 preferred; $.50 redemption; $200 of the offeror's stock
  for $100 on the flip-over.
