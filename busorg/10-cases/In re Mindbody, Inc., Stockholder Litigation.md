## Rule

Knowing participation requires **substantial assistance**, assessed through the
Restatement (Second) of Torts § 876(b) factors, so a third-party arm's-length buyer
that merely stands by while a fiduciary breaches — even in breach of a contractual
obligation to flag material omissions — has not participated; and the scienter
prong demands that the third party know **its own** conduct was legally improper,
not merely that the fiduciary was breaching.

## Facts

### On the Revlon breach

- Stollmeyer had **never experienced a liquidity event** despite building Mindbody
  past a $1 billion market capitalisation, and had made several million dollars of
  financial commitments he was funding off a line of credit. He described 98% of
  his net worth as "locked inside" a volatile stock he could sell only in "tiny
  bits," "kind of like sucking through a very small straw."
- His super-voting Class B stock carried **19.8% of fully diluted voting power
  until the October 2021 sunset**, after which it would carry under 4%. IVP, the
  largest holder, faced the same sunset and wanted out; Luxor, which would replace
  IVP on the board, opposed a near-term sale. Stollmeyer therefore had a closing
  window in which his ally still held power.
- Stollmeyer **met Vista twice before the board authorised any sale process**,
  telling Vista on September 4 that he wanted to find a "good home" for his company,
  that he was "getting tired," and that he expected to stay two or three more years.
  He gave that information to no other bidder.
- At Vista's CXO Summit he watched presentations quantifying the wealth Vista
  portfolio executives had earned — $488.6 million since 2017 — texted that they
  were "mind blowing/inspiring," and told Mindbody's president that Vista "really
  love[s] me, I love them." He expected to keep his job, reload with equity, and
  "make as much money over the next three years as he did the first go around."
- **The November 6 tip.** Hours after Mindbody's stock fell 16% on earnings, Stahl
  texted Saroya "Should we sprint?"; Saroya called Qatalyst's Chang and then texted
  "Jeff [is] all over it" and "[h]e wants 40 min" — Chang tipping Vista that
  Stollmeyer's minimum was $40 per share. Vista then modelled how to "lean in to get
  there."
- **The November 10 tip.** Stollmeyer called Saroya, in violation of the
  Transaction Committee's Guidelines on outbound communications, to tell him
  Mindbody would be running a sale process — three weeks before Qatalyst formally
  invited Vista in on November 30.
- Stollmeyer **waited eight days** after Vista's October 15 expression of interest
  before telling all the directors, delaying the formal start and extending Vista's
  head start. Vista used it to retain Bain for a four-to-six week outside-in market
  study and was ready to bid firmly in early December, when rival bidders were still
  in early stages. By the December 20 best-and-final, only H&F remained, and it
  "lamented internally that they needed more time."
- **The board did not know any of it** — not Stollmeyer's need for liquidity, not
  IVP's desire to exit, not the September 4 meeting's content, not the CXO Summit
  reaction, not the "good home" remark, not either tip, not Vista's head start.

### On the aiding and abetting claim

- Merger Agreement **§ 6.3(d)** obliged each party that discovered information
  whose omission would render the proxy materially misleading to "promptly notify
  the other." Vista discovered such information and did not notify. The existence of
  that contractual obligation is what the trial court built liability on.
- Merger Agreement **§ 6.3(b)** gave Vista only a right to review and comment on
  draft proxy materials, and obliged Mindbody to give due consideration to suggested
  changes. Vista had no right to change the document.
- Vista personnel reviewed the preliminary proxy on January 5 and the definitive
  proxy on January 21 and **suggested no changes**. The trial court made **no
  finding that Vista drafted or edited anything**.
- **Stollmeyer knew everything Vista knew.** The primary violator was not operating
  in an informational vacuum, so there was nothing for Vista to create and nothing
  for Vista to supply.
- The scienter evidence was Vista's **edits to its own internal Investment Committee
  materials** — Stahl telling Klomhaus not to "tell them about process," the
  transaction-background bullet rewritten from the late-October Qatalyst call to the
  November 30 formal invitation, and a paragraph about Stollmeyer's August-to-
  November interactions deleted — made almost a month before Mindbody drafted the
  proxy. *Enough to show Vista knew its sale-process conduct "was not above
  suspicion," and not enough to show it knew its proxy inaction was wrongful.*
- Plaintiffs **waived** any claim that Vista aided and abetted the **Revlon**
  breach by failing to assert it timely. The claim that would have fit the facts was
  not before the court.
- Plaintiffs brought **no breach of contract claim**, so the § 6.3(d) obligation
  reached the case only as a theory of participation.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Revlon breach affirmed.** The "paradigmatic" Revlon claim involves a conflicted
  fiduciary insufficiently checked by the board who tilts the sale process toward
  his own interests in ways inconsistent with maximising stockholder value, and
  Stollmeyer's conduct is that claim.
- Revlon "did not create a new fiduciary duty or change the nature of existing
  fiduciary duties." In a sale of control, directors **and officers** must exercise
  their duties to maximise value, with no particular path required so long as the
  path chosen is reasonable. Enhanced scrutiny asks about the reasonableness of the
  decision-making process, including the information on which the decision rested,
  and the reasonableness of the action in the circumstances.
- **Corwin cleansing is unavailable.** The trial court's generalisation is endorsed:
  where a plaintiff proves a paradigmatic Revlon claim, "a defendant will not be
  able to show that the stockholder vote was fully informed, precisely because the
  Board did not know about and could not disclose the information about the
  officer's machinations."
- **Disclosure breach affirmed.** Three partial omissions painted Stollmeyer's
  interactions with Vista "in a sterile light," and four complete omissions were
  absent entirely, including both tips. Taken together the proxy materials "create a
  false narrative" and were not an "accurate, full and fair characterization." The
  two tips were material because any reasonable stockholder would find tips given to
  one acquirer and no other "indicative of a potentially flawed sale process."
- **Aiding and abetting reversed.** The Malpiede elements are settled; the case
  turns on knowing participation, which splits into knowledge and participation.
- **On participation: substantial assistance is required, and active conduct is
  required.** Delaware corporate-governance cases have found liability "only where
  there has been overt participation such as active attempts to create or exploit
  conflicts of interest in the board or an overt conspiracy or agreement," and
  "passive awareness... does not constitute substantial assistance." No party cited
  any Delaware case imposing liability on a third-party bidder, and the court
  declines to hold that a failure to act, without some active role, is substantial
  assistance.
- **Where the defendant is an arm's-length acquiror, participation "should be the
  most difficult to prove."** Delaware law protects arm's-length negotiation, and "a
  bidder's attempts to reduce the sale price through arm's-length negotiations
  cannot give rise to liability." Liability still attaches to a buyer that "attempts
  to create or exploit conflicts of interest in the board" or that conspires or
  agrees with the board in the breach.
- **The Restatement § 876(b) factors are adopted as the analytical framework** for
  assessing substantial assistance, knowledge and participation together: the nature
  of the act participated in or encouraged, including severity, clarity and the
  secondary actor's knowledge of them; the amount, kind and duration of assistance;
  the nature of the relationship between the actors; and the secondary actor's state
  of mind.
- **A contractual notification obligation creates no duty of disclosure to the
  target's stockholders.** Section 6.3(d) gave Mindbody a breach of contract claim;
  it did not make Vista a fiduciary. Elsewhere, "a failure to act can constitute
  participation for aiding and abetting liability only where an independent duty
  exists between the alleged aider and abettor and the plaintiff."
- The policy reasons are explicit: such a duty "would collapse the arms'-length
  distance between the third-party buyer and the target, forcing the buyer to
  consider its duty to the target's stockholders instead of to its own," and would
  make a bidder "second-guess the materiality determinations and legal judgment of
  the target's board."
- **On scienter: the second kind of knowledge was not proved.** The aider and
  abettor must know both that the primary party's conduct is a breach and that "their
  conduct was legally improper." Vista's internal scrubbing shows awareness that its
  **sale-process** conduct was not above suspicion; the trial court made no finding
  that Vista knew its failure to notify Mindbody of proxy omissions was wrongful or
  could expose it to liability to stockholders.
- **Revlon damages affirmed** at $1 per share, on a lost-transaction theory, with
  $37.50 supported by the Post-it note recording Vista employees' internal bets plus
  Saroya's and Stahl's confirming texts — "notwithstanding some misgivings about the
  thinness of the evidence."
- Disclosure damages not reached, since Vista is not liable and plaintiffs were
  entitled to only one recovery of $1 per share.

**Rejected**

- *Stollmeyer's own large equity stake aligned him with the other stockholders.*
  The record "overwhelmingly" supported the opposite: he needed liquidity, was tired
  of running a public company, had a limited window before the voting sunset and
  before Luxor replaced IVP on the board, and expected to reload with Vista equity
  and repeat the exercise.
- *Corwin cleansing applies because a disinterested majority approved the merger.*
  The vote was uninformed, because the board itself did not know what Stollmeyer had
  done and so could not disclose it.
- *Vista participated in drafting the proxy materials.* The finding is unsupported.
  Vista reviewed drafts and suggested nothing, and the trial court made no finding
  that Vista contributed to drafting or editing in any way.
- *Vista's contractual obligation to notify converts its silence into knowing
  participation.* The obligation runs to Mindbody, not to Mindbody's stockholders,
  and enforcing it through secondary fiduciary liability would dilute the exacting
  requirements of an aiding and abetting claim, which plaintiffs chose to bring
  instead of a contract claim.
- *Vista's scrubbing of its internal Investment Committee materials establishes
  scienter.* There are many innocent reasons a deal team frames a narrative for its
  own investment committee, and in any event the materials were altered nearly a
  month before the proxy was drafted, so they bear on Vista's view of the sale
  process rather than on its knowledge that its proxy silence was improper.

## Context

The session's two cases are the outer bound and the floor of the same doctrine.
[[RBC Capital Markets v. Jervis]] is the advisor who **created** the informational
vacuum and committed a fraud on the board; Mindbody is the buyer who walked past a
vacuum somebody else had already filled. The Supreme Court calls RBC "perhaps the
most directly relevant precedent" and then distinguishes it in one sentence: "In
that case, RBC knew all of the relevant information and the board knew none of it,"
whereas "Stollmeyer knew everything that Vista knew."

Three doctrinal contributions are worth separating.

**Participation now has a test.** The Restatement § 876(b) substantial assistance
factors, which Chancery had used since Dole, are adopted by the Supreme Court as
the framework. Active conduct is required; passive awareness is not enough; and a
failure to act supports liability only where the third party owed the plaintiff an
independent duty.

**The buyer's status matters.** An arm's-length acquiror sits in the hardest
position for a plaintiff, because Delaware protects arm's-length bargaining and
because deterring bidders with litigation risk hurts the very stockholders the
claim is meant to protect. The two exceptions survive intact — creating or
exploiting board conflicts, and conspiring or agreeing in the breach — and the
first of them is exactly what a timely pleaded claim would have alleged here.

**Contract is not fiduciary duty.** A merger agreement covenant between the buyer
and the target gives the target a contract claim and nothing more. Reading such
covenants as generating duties to the target's stockholders would collapse the
arm's-length distance the rest of the law depends on.

The waiver is the loudest fact in the case. Plaintiffs did not timely plead that
Vista aided and abetted the **Revlon** breach, and that is the claim the facts fit:
Vista took tips it should not have had, sprinted on a head start it knew it was
getting, and scrubbed its own file about it. The court reverses on a
**disclosure**-based theory where Vista's only conduct was silence about facts the
fiduciary already knew. A pleading choice, not a doctrine, produced the reversal.

On the Revlon side the case is the modern restatement of
[[Sale of control - Revlon and the duty to maximise price]] and confirms two things
the older cases leave implicit: **officers** as well as directors bear the
value-maximisation obligation, and the "paradigmatic" claim is a conflicted
fiduciary insufficiently checked by the board. It also supplies the tightest
statement of why the two halves of a paradigmatic claim travel together — a board
kept in the dark cannot disclose what it does not know, so proving the Revlon claim
generally defeats [[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings|Corwin]] cleansing at the same
time. That is a more useful formulation than anything in Corwin itself.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Appeal from a post-trial judgment of the Court of Chancery (Laster, C.) after a
  trial on 1,865 exhibits, eighteen fact and six expert witnesses, twenty-four
  deposition transcripts and 123 stipulations.
- **Appellants challenged none of the trial court's fact-findings** — only the
  conclusions drawn from them. Legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, factual
  findings for clear error, and damages for abuse of discretion.
- Posture explains the split result. The Revlon and disclosure holdings rest on
  findings nobody contested, so they stand; the aiding and abetting holding is a
  legal conclusion about what those same findings add up to, reviewed de novo, and
  it falls.

### Facts

Richard Stollmeyer founded Mindbody and grew it into a software-as-a-service
platform for the fitness, wellness and beauty industries, taking it public in 2015
and past a $1 billion market capitalisation by 2018. He had never had a liquidity
event. He had put nearly $1 million into his wife's wellness company, at least
$300,000 into an entity of his own, lent money to his brothers and a former
partner, and pledged $3 million to a local college of which $2.4 million was
unpaid. He was funding commitments off a line of credit and later described himself
as having been "at or near the precipice of financial ruin." At trial he denied
needing liquidity; the court found his own contemporaneous statements far more
persuasive than his testimony.

He also had a clock. His super-voting Class B shares gave him 19.8% of fully
diluted voting power until they converted in October 2021, after which he would
hold under 4%. IVP, the largest stockholder with about 24.6% of the vote, faced the
same sunset, had internally targeted $200 million of additional liquidity by year
end, and had its own representative, Liaw, on the board. Luxor, which had built a
14% stake and would become the largest holder after the sunset, told Stollmeyer
flatly in August that it would not support a near-term sale. Stollmeyer responded by
having Centerview build a dossier on Luxor. And he was exhausted: two rocky
acquisitions, a sales-strategy shift, and eight months serving as both chief
executive and chief technology officer.

On August 7, 2018 he lunched with Qatalyst's Jeff Chang, who had never found him
"open-minded" about private equity before. Chang emailed Vista's Monti Saroya
within minutes; Saroya replied in seven. Chang's internal note read: "Met with him
today and he immediately talked about how he is tired of being public and wanted me
to re-connect him w[ith] Vista and Thoma. Probably a 2019 deal is my guess." Chang
waited a week before connecting Stollmeyer to Thoma Bravo and Hellman & Friedman,
whom he did not meet until mid-October and early November.

Stollmeyer met Saroya and Nicolas Stahl on September 4. Stahl's contemporaneous
note recorded that Stollmeyer "would like to find a good home for his company," was
"getting tired," expected to stay two or three more years, and had two possible
successors in mind — none of which he had board authorisation to say, and none of
which he reported afterwards. At the board dinner the next day he mentioned meeting
Vista but gave no full account and said nothing about Qatalyst; the board told him
to keep them in the loop and not get "too far advanced."

In October he attended Vista's CXO Summit, a conference Vista uses to prospect for
targets and to dispel "the myth that [Vista] might be a slash-and-burn shop." He
pitched Mindbody to Robert Smith, asked to be connected to a portfolio chief
executive because he wanted "to know what it's like to sell to Vista as a founder,"
watched a presentation quantifying $488.6 million of executive wealth creation
since 2017, and texted that it was "mind blowing/inspiring."

Vista expressed interest on October 15. Stollmeyer waited eight days to tell the
full board. On November 6 Mindbody's stock fell 16% on earnings; Stahl texted
"Should we sprint?"; Saroya called Chang and came back with "he wants 40 min," and
the deal team worked out how to make $40 work on a higher exit multiple. On
November 10 Stollmeyer called Saroya, against the Transaction Committee's
Guidelines, to tell him a process was coming. Vista had already retained Bain in
mid-October for a four-to-six week study. Qatalyst formally invited Vista in on
November 30; no other bidder heard anything until November 19 and no other financial
bidder was contacted until December 3 or 4. Vista bid firmly in early December and
best-and-final on December 20, by which point only Hellman & Friedman remained and
was internally complaining it needed more time. The deal closed at $36.50.

Mindbody's proxy materials, even after supplemental disclosures on February 7,
described the September 4 lunch as a general overview "as was typical for Mr.
Stollmeyer to present to potential investors," the CXO Summit as a "meet and greet"
annual conference, and Vista's October 15 expression of interest in one anodyne
sentence. The two tips, the October 19 reference call with a Vista portfolio chief
executive, and Saroya's invitation to a Miami charity event appeared nowhere.

Vista's contractual role was narrow. Section 6.3(b) let it review and comment;
§ 6.3(d) obliged it to notify Mindbody of information whose omission would make the
proxy materially misleading. Vista's people read both the preliminary and the
definitive proxy and said nothing. Separately, before its own investment committee
met in November, Vista's deal team had rewritten a transaction-background bullet
from the late-October Qatalyst call to the November 30 formal invitation and deleted
a paragraph about Stollmeyer's August-to-November contacts — with Stahl texting
Klomhaus not to "tell them about process."

### Issue

- Whether Stollmeyer, as a conflicted officer who tilted the sale process toward
  the buyer he favoured, breached his duty of loyalty under Revlon, and whether
  Corwin cleansing was available.
- Whether the partial and complete omissions in the proxy materials were material,
  so that Stollmeyer breached his duty of disclosure.
- Whether Vista's failure to correct those omissions, in the face of a contractual
  obligation to notify Mindbody of them, constituted knowing participation in
  Stollmeyer's disclosure breach.
- Whether the $1 per share lost-transaction award for the Revlon breach was within
  the trial court's discretion.

### Holding

- **On Revlon: breach affirmed.** Stollmeyer had disabling conflicts — a need for
  near-term liquidity, a desire to sell fast inside a closing window, and an
  expectation of post-merger employment with fresh equity — and he tilted the
  process by giving Vista informational and timing advantages no other bidder
  received while keeping the board in the dark.
- **On Corwin: unavailable.** The stockholder vote could not be fully informed
  because the board did not know what it would have had to disclose.
- **On disclosure: breach affirmed.** Three partial and four complete omissions
  created a false narrative, and the two tips in particular would have significantly
  altered the total mix.
- **On aiding and abetting: reversed.** Vista neither substantially assisted nor
  knew its own conduct was improper. A contractual notification duty owed to the
  target does not create a disclosure duty to the target's stockholders, and passive
  silence is not participation.
- **On damages: affirmed** at $1 per share for the Revlon breach; disclosure
  damages not reached.

### Rule

- In a change-of-control transaction, Revlon enhanced scrutiny is the presumptive
  standard, and directors **and officers** must exercise their fiduciary duties to
  maximise value for stockholders.
- There is no particular path fiduciaries must follow, so long as the path chosen is
  reasonable; enhanced scrutiny examines the reasonableness of the decision-making
  process, including the information underlying it, and of the action taken.
- The paradigmatic Revlon claim is a conflicted fiduciary insufficiently checked by
  the board who tilts the process toward his own interests.
- Proving a paradigmatic Revlon claim will generally defeat Corwin cleansing,
  because the board could not have disclosed what it did not know.
- Aiding and abetting requires a fiduciary relationship, a breach, knowing
  participation, and proximately caused damages.
- Knowing participation has two components. Knowledge requires that the third party
  know both that the primary party's conduct is a breach and that its own conduct is
  legally improper. Participation requires **substantial assistance**, evaluated on
  the Restatement § 876(b) factors.
- Against an arm's-length acquiror, participation is the most difficult element to
  prove; liability attaches only where the buyer creates or exploits conflicts of
  interest in the board, or conspires or agrees in the breach.
- A failure to act constitutes participation only where the third party owes the
  plaintiff an independent duty; a contractual obligation to the target is not such
  a duty.

### Reasoning

*(Valihura, J.)*

- On Revlon the court does almost no independent work, and says so: the fact
  findings are unchallenged, so the only question is whether they add up to the
  paradigmatic claim, and they plainly do.
- The Corwin holding is presented as a corollary rather than a separate inquiry. A
  fiduciary who hides his conflicts from the board necessarily produces a proxy that
  cannot disclose them, so the same conduct that proves the breach destroys the
  defence.
- On aiding and abetting the court is candid about its own position: "[t]he case law
  is thin and the briefing by the parties was scant on all of these novel issues,"
  which is why it reaches for the Restatement and repeatedly confines the holding to
  this record.
- The participation analysis is essentially a causal one. Vista did not create the
  vacuum, did not mislead Stollmeyer, and did not proximately cause his breach,
  because Stollmeyer "already had all of the information that was omitted."
  Secondary liability needs a contribution, and silence about what the primary actor
  already knows contributes nothing.
- The contract analysis separates the two claims the plaintiffs could have brought.
  Vista's failure to notify exposed it to a **breach of contract** claim Mindbody
  never brought; converting that into secondary fiduciary liability to stockholders
  "would be diluted by implying that contractual rights and obligations... create an
  independent duty of disclosure."
- The policy passage supplies the reason the line is drawn there rather than
  elsewhere: a buyer forced to police the target's disclosure would owe conflicting
  loyalties and would have to second-guess the materiality judgments of a board that
  already owes those duties.
- On scienter the court draws a distinction the trial court did not. The scrubbing
  evidence shows Vista knew its **sale-process** conduct was questionable, which
  would matter to the waived Revlon aiding and abetting claim; it says nothing about
  whether Vista knew its **proxy silence** was improper, which is the claim actually
  pleaded.
- On damages the court affirms while announcing its discomfort. The Post-it note
  alone "likely would not have been enough"; what saves the award is Saroya's reply
  that "37.5 is a good guess" and Stahl's "I thought so too."

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The opinion twice signals that it is deciding less than it appears to. It stresses
the scant briefing, confines itself "in a narrow fashion... without more broadly
foreclosing different outcomes based upon different facts or legal arguments," and
declines to hold that inaction can never be substantial assistance. The genuine
tension it leaves open is that Vista is the beneficiary of a rule protecting
arm's-length bargaining while the findings describe conduct that was not
arm's-length at all — Vista took a price tip, took a process tip, sprinted on a head
start it knew it had been handed, and scrubbed its own file about all of it. The
court's answer is procedural rather than substantive: that conduct belonged to a
Revlon aiding and abetting claim, and plaintiffs waived it.

### Cold-call notes

- **Separate the two prongs of knowing participation before saying anything else.**
  Knowledge splits again into knowledge of the primary breach and knowledge that
  one's own conduct is improper; participation requires substantial assistance.
  Mindbody fails on participation and on the second half of knowledge.
- **Have the RBC distinction in one sentence.** RBC knew everything and the board
  knew nothing; here Stollmeyer knew everything Vista knew, so Vista created no
  vacuum and caused no breach.
- **Know the Restatement factors.** Nature of the act, including severity and
  clarity and the secondary actor's knowledge of them; amount, kind and duration of
  assistance; nature of the relationship between the actors; the secondary actor's
  state of mind. The Supreme Court adopted them here as the framework.
- **Be ready on the contract point, which is the holding with the longest reach.** A
  merger agreement covenant to flag material omissions gives the counterparty a
  contract claim, not a fiduciary duty to the target's stockholders. Add the policy
  reason: the opposite rule collapses arm's-length distance and makes the buyer
  audit the target board's materiality judgments.
- **Name the waiver.** The claim that fits these facts is aiding and abetting the
  **Revlon** breach, and plaintiffs did not timely assert it. If asked whether Vista
  would have lost on that claim, the honest answer is that the tips and the scrubbing
  point toward exploiting a board conflict, which is a recognised route to liability.
- **Do not overstate the reversal.** The court declines to hold that inaction can
  never be substantial assistance, and preserves liability for a buyer that creates
  or exploits board conflicts or conspires in the breach.
- **Use this case for the Revlon-plus-Corwin combination.** Proving a paradigmatic
  Revlon claim generally defeats Corwin cleansing, because the board could not
  disclose what it did not know. It is the cleanest statement of that link in the
  course.
- **Numbers and artefacts worth holding.** $36.50 deal price; $37.50 lost-transaction
  price; $1 per share, over $35 million; 19.8% falling to under 4% at the October
  2021 sunset; eight-day delay; three-week head start; "he wants 40 min"; the Post-it
  note.
