## Rule

**Bad faith remains a necessary predicate to any Caremark claim**, and the standard
does not soften because the defendant is an **officer** rather than a director. An
officer's oversight duty reaches central compliance risks **within her remit** — it does
not make her a guarantor of business outcomes.

## Facts

- Segway was a small company — roughly $35 million of revenue and 80 employees in 2015 —
  acquired that year by a Ninebot subsidiary, after which it kept its own board,
  officers, and financial and accounting systems.
- Judy Cai joined as VP of Finance in 2015, became interim President that December, and
  President in 2018, while continuing to act as **in-house accountant with "complete
  responsibility for [Segway's] tax matters."** *Her remit is conceded and is the only
  reason the claim is even arguable.*
- After the acquisition Segway's sales declined and its customer base shrank; it pivoted
  to selling Ninebot products, downsized to 60 employees, closed its Bedford
  headquarters in 2020 and laid off most staff. Cai was terminated in November 2020.
- Integrating Segway's financials into Ninebot's systems afterwards revealed that "the
  information Cai provided Ninebot did not match the actual numbers," including **an
  excess of $5 million in accounts receivable "not properly recorded and/or booked."**
- **Segway expressly disavowed a duty of care claim** and insisted it was pursuing only
  a duty of oversight theory. *This choice decides the case.* The court says it "assumed
  Segway was claiming that Cai breached her duty of care" and proceeds on the theory
  Segway actually pleaded.
- **No wrongdoing of any kind is alleged** — no accounting improprieties, no fraudulent
  business practices, no material legal violations. Only "issues" with unspecified
  customers, a revenue decrease, and rising receivables.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- Motion to dismiss granted. The claim "rests on the misimpression that an oversight
  claim pursued against an officer is easier to plead than one against a director."
- **Oversight duties arise from good faith, a subsidiary element of the duty of loyalty**,
  so a plaintiff must plead facts supporting a reasonable inference the fiduciary acted
  in **bad faith**. The two Caremark prongs: fiduciaries either "utterly fail to
  implement any reporting or information system or controls," or, "having implemented
  such a system or controls, consciously fail to monitor or oversee its operations."
- **On the officer question**, *McDonald's* held that officers owe context-specific
  "duties of oversight comparable to those of directors," extending — barring extreme
  facts — only to matters within the officer's remit. It "did not... craft a **lower
  standard**" for officers.
- **Prong two requires a red flag.** Segway had to plead that Cai "consciously failed to
  act after learning about evidence of illegality — the proverbial 'red flag.'" Generic
  financial deterioration is not one: "[s]uch generic financial matters are far from the
  sort of red flags that could give rise to Caremark liability if deliberately ignored."
- **No bad faith pleaded.** "Bad things can happen to corporations despite fiduciaries
  exercising the utmost good faith," and oversight duties "are not designed to subject
  [fiduciaries] to personal liability for failure to predict the future and to properly
  evaluate business risk."

**Rejected**

- *McDonald's lowered the bar for oversight claims against officers.* "This is a
  distressing reading of our law." The doctrine's tenets "persist regardless of whether a
  Caremark claim is brought against a director or an officer."
- *Cai was responsible for the finance function, knew about the deteriorating receivables,
  and did nothing.* Responsibility plus awareness plus inaction is not the claim. Caremark
  "is not a tool to hold fiduciaries liable for everyday business problems"; it addresses
  "the extraordinary case" where an utter failure or conscious disregard "gives rise to a
  corporate trauma."
- *An officer who manages day-to-day operations should answer for what goes wrong there.*
  "Officers' management of day-to-day matters does not make them guarantors of negative
  outcomes from imperfect business decisions."

## Context

The negative space around [[Marchand v. Barnhill]]. Marchand is the rare complaint that
clears the bar; Segway is what the overwhelming majority look like. Reading them together
isolates what actually matters: Blue Bell had **no board-level system at all** for its
single mission-critical risk, and repeated regulatory findings and positive listeria tests
that never reached the board. Segway had a functioning finance department, an officer doing
her job imperfectly, and declining sales in a shrinking business.

Two extensions of the doctrine are visible here. **Officers owe oversight duties** at all
— a relatively recent development, from *McDonald's* — and those duties are **remit-bound**
rather than enterprise-wide. And the **red flag** requirement under prong two has content:
it means evidence of **illegality or comparable wrongdoing**, not bad numbers. A fact
pattern supplying only underperformance has supplied nothing.

The pleading choice is itself the lesson. Segway disavowed the duty of care claim that its
allegations naturally described and insisted on loyalty. Care would have been the right
theory and probably a viable one, but it also carries different exculpation consequences —
which is precisely why plaintiffs reach for oversight, and precisely why the court polices
the line. See [[Charter exculpation of director liability]].

## Class layer

### Posture

- Direct action by the corporation against its **former officer**, not a derivative suit —
  Segway is the plaintiff, so no demand futility question arises.
- Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, all well-pleaded facts taken as true and all reasonable
  inferences drawn for Segway. Dismissal nonetheless.
- The court notes it need not "accept every strained interpretation" of the allegations.

### Facts

Segway had been a well-known name that never became a large company — about $35 million in
revenue and eighty employees when Ninebot acquired it in 2015. Under Ninebot it kept its own
governance and financial systems while progressively becoming a distributor of Ninebot's
products rather than a manufacturer of its own.

Judy Cai rose from VP of Finance to President over five years while never really leaving the
finance function: she continued as in-house accountant, retained complete responsibility for
tax, and stayed involved in compiling and reviewing financial information for Ninebot's
management. By 2018 the whole finance department was five or six people including her.

The business declined throughout. Headquarters closed in 2020, most employees were laid off,
and Cai was terminated that November. Only when Ninebot absorbed Segway's financial systems
afterwards did the mismatch surface — most conspicuously more than $5 million of accounts
receivable that had not been properly recorded. Ninebot spent considerable resources trying
to reconcile the discrepancies and could not; Cai declined to help.

### Issue

- Whether a corporation states a Caremark oversight claim against a former officer by
  alleging she knew of and ignored deteriorating financial metrics within her area of
  responsibility.
- Whether the pleading standard for an oversight claim is lower when the defendant is an
  officer rather than a director.

### Holding

- **On the standard: no lower bar for officers.** *McDonald's* extended oversight duties to
  officers and limited them to the officer's remit; it did not reduce what must be pleaded.
  Bad faith remains necessary whatever the defendant's title.
- **On the claim: not stated.** No wrongdoing is alleged at all, so there is no red flag to
  have consciously disregarded, and nothing supporting an inference of bad faith.
- **Disposition:** motion to dismiss granted.

### Rule

- Oversight duties arise from the duty of good faith, itself a subsidiary element of the
  duty of **loyalty**.
- A viable oversight claim requires facts supporting a reasonable inference of **bad faith**.
- Bad faith is established where fiduciaries either utterly fail to implement any reporting
  or information system or controls, or, having implemented them, consciously fail to
  monitor or oversee their operations.
- Prong two requires conscious failure to act **after learning of evidence of illegality** —
  a red flag.
- Officers owe context-specific oversight duties comparable to directors', extending, barring
  extreme facts, only to matters **within the officer's remit**.
- Oversight duties do not impose liability for failing to predict the future or to evaluate
  business risk correctly.

### Reasoning

*(Will, V.C.)*

- The opinion is unusually direct about what the plaintiff is attempting: "Segway appears to
  believe that the high bar to plead a Caremark claim is lowered when the claim is brought
  against an officer. This is a distressing reading of our law."
- The court separates the two things *McDonald's* did — **extending** the duty to officers,
  and **narrowing** its scope to the officer's remit — and points out that Segway has read an
  extension as a relaxation.
- The red-flag analysis is where the case is decided, and it turns on absence rather than
  degree. Not that the flags were small; that there were none, because nothing unlawful is
  alleged.
- The closing framing places oversight where Caremark put it: reserved for "the extraordinary
  case" producing "a corporate trauma," not a remedy for ordinary commercial disappointment.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None.
- Worth two lines on the other side: Segway's underlying grievance is real — more than $5
  million of unreconcilable receivables, reported by an officer who was also the company's
  accountant, who then declined to help sort it out. The court's answer is that this describes
  a **care** claim, which Segway expressly abandoned. The case is therefore as much about
  pleading strategy as about oversight doctrine.

### Cold-call notes

- **The likely question is why Segway lost**, and the best answer is one sentence: it pleaded
  loyalty when its facts described care, and loyalty requires bad faith it could not allege.
- **Have the red-flag definition precise.** Evidence of **illegality** or comparable
  wrongdoing. Declining sales, a shrinking customer base, and rising receivables are "generic
  financial matters," however large the numbers.
- **Know what *McDonald's* did and did not do.** Extended oversight duties to officers;
  confined them to the officer's remit; did not lower the standard. Being able to state all
  three is the distinguishing move.
- **Be ready on why a plaintiff would make Segway's choice.** Oversight is a loyalty claim,
  so it escapes an exculpatory charter provision. That incentive is what makes the doctrine
  a magnet for repleaded care claims, and why courts guard the boundary.
- **Have the quotable line:** "Bad things can happen to corporations despite fiduciaries
  exercising the utmost good faith."
