## Rule

When a stockholder challenges board action that interferes with the election of directors
or a stockholder vote in a **contest for corporate control**, Schnell and Blasius are
**folded into Unocal-style enhanced scrutiny**: the board bears the burden of showing a
**real, non-pretextual threat** to an important corporate interest, with **proper and
non-selfish motivations**, and a response **reasonable in relation to the threat** that is
neither **preclusive nor coercive** to the franchise.

## Facts

- The Stock Sale **diluted Coster from one half to one third** and "negated her ability to
  block stockholder action as a half owner." Eliminating a blocking position in a
  deadlocked company is interference with the franchise in its purest form.
- Coster's custodian complaint under **§ 226(a)(1)** was "extremely broad." She asked the
  court to empower a custodian to "exercise full authority and control over the Company,
  its operations, and management" rather than presenting "a tailored request for relief
  that targeted the stockholder deadlock." The **breadth of her own request** is what
  creates the threat that justifies the board's response.
- A custodian so empowered "would have given rise to **broad termination rights in SPE
  contracts** and threatened UIP's revenue stream, as UIP's business model is dependent on
  the continued viability of those contracts." The Chancellor called it an **"existential
  crisis."**
- Bonnell had been **promised equity on multiple occasions**, promotion had stalled, and he
  and Wilkinson had both considered leaving. The succession plan was one "that Wout had
  favored" and had been proposed **on a clear day**, long before the dispute.
- The Stock Sale was priced from the board's own valuation — a one-third interest for
  $41,289.67 — and the Court of Chancery found it **entirely fair**, both price and
  process.
- The board's motives were mixed and the court said so: "retaining and rewarding Bonnell,
  mooting the Custodian Action, and **undermining [Coster's] leverage**." Yet they "were
  **not pretexts for entrenchment for selfish reasons**" or "post-hoc justifications."
- Coster filed the custodian action "**[w]ithout making any meaningful effort to negotiate
  board composition**."
- **The Stock Sale was not preclusive.** The new three-way ownership "presented a
  potentially more effective way for her to exercise actual control": Schwat and Bonnell
  "are not bound to vote together," so Coster "could cast a **swing vote**" and could join
  forces with either owner in future. "A realistic path to control of UIP negates the
  preclusive impact."
- The board **did not pursue more aggressive options** available to break the deadlock.
  *Tailoring evidence — the response was narrower than it could have been.*
- Two stockholder meetings, on May 22 and June 4, 2018, both **ended in deadlock**, each
  side defeating the other's motions.
- Bonnell told Coster's lawyer that the "companies operate close to even"; her lawyer "did
  not believe that Bonnell was forthcoming about the operating companies' true
  profitability." *Background; the court does not rest on it.*

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Schnell is cabined to selfish motivation.** The Chancellor "was correct in this case to
  cabin Schnell and its equitable review to those cases where the board acts within its
  legal power, but is **motivated for selfish reasons** to interfere with the stockholder
  franchise." Schnell polices action "that, although technically legal, was motivated for
  selfish reasons," and was reserved for instances "that threaten the fabric of the law" —
  a formulation the court calls "a bit hyperbolic" while approving the narrowing.
- **Entire fairness is not a substitute.** Even where a stock sale "satisfie[s] Delaware's
  most onerous standard of review," that is "not [a] substitute for further equitable
  review" under Schnell or Blasius when the board interferes with director elections,
  because "inequitable action does not become permissible simply because it is legally
  possible."
- **Blasius has been folded into Unocal.** "Experience has shown that Schnell and Blasius
  review, as a matter of precedent and practice, **have been and can be folded into Unocal
  review** to accomplish the same ends—enhanced judicial scrutiny of board action that
  interferes with a corporate election or a stockholder's voting rights in contests for
  control."
- Unocal in this setting "can 'subsume[] the question of loyalty that pervades all fiduciary
  duty cases, which is whether the directors have acted for proper reasons' and 'thus
  address[] issues of good faith such as were at stake in Schnell'," and "can also be
  applied with the **sensitivity Blasius review brings**."
- **The compelling justification standard was unworkable.** "The Blasius 'compelling
  justification' standard of review turned out to be **unworkable in practice**. Once the
  court required a compelling justification to justify the board's action, the outcome was,
  for the most part, **preordained**." Courts skirted it "by limiting the 'primary purpose'
  requirement and redefining what it meant to be compelling."
- **The unified test.** The review "is situationally specific and is independent of other
  standards of review," and **the board bears the burden of proof**:
  - **First**, whether the board faced a threat "to an **important corporate interest** or to
    the achievement of a significant corporate benefit. The threat must be **real and not
    pretextual**, and the board's **motivations must be proper and not selfish or
    disloyal**."
  - **Second**, whether the response "was **reasonable in relation to the threat** posed and
    was **not preclusive or coercive** to the stockholder franchise."
- **Tailoring is required.** "[A] board that is properly motivated and has identified a
  legitimate threat must **tailor its response to only what is necessary** to counter the
  threat. The board's response to the threat cannot deprive the stockholders of a vote or
  coerce the stockholders to vote a particular way."
- **The "we know better" justification remains unavailable.** "As Chancellor Allen stated
  long ago, the threat **cannot be justified on the grounds that the board knows what is in
  the best interests of the stockholders**."
- Applied here: the board faced a real existential threat from the deadlock and the broad
  custodian request; its motivations, though mixed, were proper and not pretextual; the
  Stock Sale was appropriately tailored, and more aggressive options went unused; and it was
  neither preclusive — because a realistic path to control survives through a swing vote —
  nor coercive. **Affirmed.**

**Rejected**

- *The Court of Chancery erred by limiting Schnell review to board action totally lacking a
  good faith basis.* Cabining Schnell to selfishly motivated interference is correct. The
  broader equitable inquiry now lives inside the unified enhanced scrutiny test.
- *Having found the Stock Sale entirely fair, the Court of Chancery needed to go no
  further.* Rejected in the first appeal and reaffirmed: entire fairness does not displace
  equitable review where the board interferes with director elections.
- *The Stock Sale was preclusive because it destroyed Coster's ability to block stockholder
  action.* It foreclosed **perpetuating the deadlock**, but the resulting three-way ownership
  gives her a swing vote between two owners who are not bound to vote together, and she may
  join with either in future. A realistic path to control negates preclusion.
- *The board's admitted purpose of undermining Coster's leverage shows entrenchment.* Mixed
  motives are not disqualifying where the problematic purposes "stood alongside" genuine
  ones — rewarding and retaining an essential employee, implementing a succession plan
  favoured by the founder, and mooting a custodian action that threatened key contracts.

## Context

Coster is the end of a thirty-five-year argument about how to review board interference with
the franchise, and the reason it matters is that it **collapses three standards into one**.

The sequence, all recounted in the opinion: [[Schnell v. Chris-Craft Industries]] holds that
inequitable action does not become permissible because it is legally possible.
[[Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp]] adds that good faith is not enough and requires a
compelling justification. **Stroud v. Grace** recognises that a proxy fight coupled with a
tender offer "necessarily invoke[s] both Unocal and Blasius." **Chesapeake v. Shore** proposes
merging them, applying Unocal "with a gimlet eye out for inequitably motivated electoral
manipulation or for subjectively well-intended board action that has preclusive or coercive
effects." **MM Companies v. Liquid Audio** formally places Blasius "within Unocal," making a
compelling justification "a condition precedent to any judicial consideration of reasonableness
and proportionality." **Mercier v. Inter-Tel** drops the label and applies a modified Unocal
review in three parts — a legitimate corporate objective, proper and non-selfish motivations,
and a response reasonable in relation to that objective that neither precludes nor coerces.
**Pell v. Kill** keeps the compelling language while explaining that "the shift from
'reasonable' to 'compelling' requires that the directors establish a **closer fit between means
and ends**." **Lee Enterprises** applies the same enhanced scrutiny to an advance notice bylaw,
"whether labeled as Unocal or Blasius."

What Coster keeps from Blasius is more important than what it discards. The **burden stays on
the board**. The threat must be **real and not pretextual**. Motivations must be **proper and
not selfish**. And the "we know better" defence is still no defence — quoted from Blasius, in
2023, as live law. What goes is the phrase "compelling justification" as an independent gate,
because in practice it decided cases before they were analysed.

The practical consequence for this course is that the **Blasius/Unocal distinction the casebook
draws after [[Air Products v. Airgas]] has collapsed**. The note there observes that
stockholders' ability to receive tender offers "receives little protection under Unocal" while
their right to vote "enjoys robust protection under Blasius," calls the distinction hard to
defend — "[t]here is not much difference between the decision to tender your shares to a hostile
acquiror and the decision whether to vote in favor of hostile slate of directors" — and notes
that it "appears to have collapsed" in Coster. Both now run through
[[Takeover defences - Unocal enhanced scrutiny]], with the franchise cases distinguished by the
**sensitivity** applied rather than by the standard used.

Two limits are worth holding. First, this enhanced review is **situationally specific**: Mercier
warned against a more muscular Unocal outside election interference and control contests,
because elsewhere "more traditional tools are available to police self-dealing or improperly
motivated director action." Second, **preclusion is measured by what remains, not by what was
lost**. Coster lost her blocking position and the court still found no preclusion, because a
swing vote among three equal owners is a realistic path to control. That is the same move as
Selectica and Airgas — delay and difficulty are not preclusion so long as a realistic route
survives.

The case is also a reminder that **a plaintiff's own overreach can supply the threat**. Had
Coster sought a custodian tailored to breaking the deadlock rather than one with full authority
over the company, there would have been no existential crisis, no legitimate objective, and very
likely no Stock Sale to uphold.

## Class layer

### Posture

- **Second** appeal to the Delaware Supreme Court. In the first appeal the Court did not
  disturb the Court of Chancery's entire fairness ruling but **remanded with instructions** to
  review the Stock Sale under Schnell and Blasius.
- On remand the Court of Chancery found no inequitable purpose under Schnell and a compelling
  justification under Blasius, borrowing Unocal's reasonableness and proportionality analysis.
- Affirmed.
- The posture is the opinion's engine. Because the Supreme Court had already **required**
  Schnell/Blasius review on top of entire fairness, it had to say what that review consists of
  — and used the occasion to rebuild the standard rather than apply the old one.

### Facts

UIP Companies is a real estate services company founded in 2007 by Steven Schwat, Cornelius
Bruggen and Wout Coster, operating through subsidiaries that serve investment properties in the
Washington, D.C. area. Many properties are held in special purpose entities UIP owns alongside
third-party investors, and UIP's business model depends on the continued viability of the SPE
contracts.

Each founder began with a third of the shares. Bruggen left in 2011 and tendered his shares at
no cost, leaving Schwat and Wout as half owners.

In 2013 Wout told Schwat and Peter Bonnell, a senior executive, that he had been diagnosed with
leukemia, and the group began negotiating a buyout in which Bonnell and another executive, Heath
Wilkinson, would purchase Wout's shares. Bonnell had been promised equity on multiple occasions;
with promotion stalled, both he and Wilkinson had considered leaving, so the buyout served to
retain them as well as to provide Wout an exit. The parties reached a non-binding term sheet in
April 2014 at $2,125,000 for Wout's half, but kept going back and forth and "[n]o deal was ever
finalized." Wout died on April 8, 2015, and his widow Marion Coster inherited his interest.

Buyout discussions with Coster continued through 2015 without resolution. She became "very
distressed about her financial situation," having received neither income distributions nor the
benefits she expected, and by May 2016 "appeared primarily interested in a lump sum buyout or
arrangement that would provide her with a consistent stream of income." Bonnell identified three
"divorce" options in a July 2016 email — a lump sum buyout, an installment buyout, and a
distribution scheme. When Coster's lawyer Mike Pace asked about profitability, Bonnell said the
companies "operate close to even" and that Schwat had taken no distributions since Wout's death;
Pace did not believe Bonnell was forthcoming.

In August 2017 Coster provided a $7.3 million valuation and demanded to inspect books and
records, following up in October. After much back and forth about the adequacy of the documents,
on April 4, 2018 she called a special stockholders meeting to elect new board members. UIP then
had a five-member board with three sitting directors — Schwat, Bonnell and the chief financial
officer Stephen Cox — and two vacancies from Wout's death and Bruggen's departure.

The May 22, 2018 meeting went nowhere: each of Coster's motions on board size and composition
failed on Schwat's opposition. That same day the board reduced the number of seats to three by
unanimous written consent. A second meeting on June 4 ended in the same deadlock, leaving Schwat,
Bonnell and Cox in place.

Coster then sued in the Court of Chancery seeking a custodian under 8 Del. C. § 226(a)(1). Her
complaint mainly sought a neutral tie-breaker to facilitate director elections but also alleged
a lack of distributions and transparency — and asked for a custodian with "broad oversight and
managerial powers," empowered to "exercise full authority and control over the Company, its
operations, and management."

That breadth was the problem. A custodian with such powers would have triggered broad termination
rights in the SPE contracts and threatened UIP's revenue stream. Facing that, the board decided
to issue the equity long promised to Bonnell. On its own valuation of a 100% non-controlling
interest at $123,869, the board offered and Bonnell purchased a one-third interest for
$41,289.67. The Stock Sale diluted Coster to one third, destroyed her blocking position, and
mooted the custodian action. She sued to cancel it.

### Issue

- Whether Schnell review extends beyond board action motivated by selfish reasons, or is properly
  confined to it.
- Whether a finding of entire fairness forecloses further equitable review of board action
  interfering with director elections.
- What standard governs board action that interferes with a stockholder vote in a contest for
  corporate control, given the practical failure of Blasius's compelling justification test.
- Whether the Stock Sale satisfies that standard.

### Holding

- **On Schnell: properly cabined.** It reaches board action within the board's legal power that is
  motivated for selfish reasons to interfere with the franchise.
- **On entire fairness: not a substitute.** Equitable review is required on top of it where the
  board interferes with director elections.
- **On the standard: Schnell and Blasius are folded into Unocal.** The board bears the burden on
  two questions — a real, non-pretextual threat to an important corporate interest with proper and
  non-selfish motivations, and a response reasonable in relation to the threat that is neither
  preclusive nor coercive.
- **On application: the Stock Sale survives.** The deadlock and the broadly empowered custodian
  request posed an existential threat; the board's mixed motives were genuine and not pretextual;
  the sale was tailored, with more aggressive options left unused; and it was not preclusive,
  because Coster retains a realistic path to control as a swing vote among three equal owners.
- **Disposition:** affirmed.

### Rule

- Board action interfering with the election of directors or a stockholder vote in a contest for
  corporate control is reviewed under enhanced scrutiny, with the burden on the board.
- The court asks first whether the board faced a threat to an important corporate interest or to
  the achievement of a significant corporate benefit; the threat must be real and not pretextual,
  and the board's motivations proper and not selfish or disloyal.
- A threat cannot be justified on the ground that the board knows what is in the stockholders'
  best interests.
- The court asks second whether the response was reasonable in relation to the threat and was not
  preclusive or coercive to the franchise.
- A properly motivated board that has identified a legitimate threat must tailor its response to
  only what is necessary to counter the threat.
- The response may not deprive stockholders of a vote or coerce them into voting a particular way.
- Entire fairness review does not substitute for equitable review of interference with director
  elections.
- Schnell review is confined to cases where the board acts within its legal power but is motivated
  for selfish reasons.

### Reasoning

*(Seitz, C.J.)*

- The opinion is structured as a history because the holding is a **consolidation**. Walking
  through Schnell, Blasius, Stroud, Chesapeake, Liquid Audio, Mercier, Pell and Lee Enterprises
  demonstrates that the courts had already been converging, so folding the standards together is
  presented as recognition rather than innovation.
- The candid diagnosis of Blasius is the hinge: requiring a compelling justification made outcomes
  "for the most part, preordained," and courts responded by manipulating the inputs — narrowing
  "primary purpose," diluting "compelling." A standard that is evaded in practice is not a
  standard.
- Unocal is chosen because it already does both jobs. It "subsume[s] the question of loyalty...
  whether the directors have acted for proper reasons," which is Schnell's concern, and it can be
  applied with the sensitivity Blasius brings to the franchise.
- Blasius's substance is preserved deliberately and quoted directly — the burden, the
  non-pretextual threat, the proper motivation, and the unavailability of "we know better." What is
  discarded is a label, not a protection.
- On the facts, **mixed motives** are handled by asking whether the legitimate ones are genuine
  rather than whether the illegitimate ones are absent. The board admittedly wanted to undermine
  Coster's leverage; what saves it is that the succession plan predated the dispute, was favoured
  by the founder, and "stood alongside the more problematic purposes."
- Tailoring is assessed against the alternatives **not** taken, which is the practical content of
  Pell's "closer fit between means and ends."
- Preclusion is measured prospectively. The court asks what path to control remains, not what
  position was lost — and finds one, because three equal owners who are not bound to vote together
  leave Coster a swing vote.
- The plaintiff's own litigation choice supplies the threat. Had the custodian request been
  tailored to the deadlock, there would have been no risk to the SPE contracts and no legitimate
  objective for the board to serve.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The synthesis is clean but leaves the same soft spot the old law had, relocated. Everything now
turns on whether the board's motivations are "proper and not selfish" where motives are admittedly
mixed, and Coster offers no test for weighing a genuine purpose against an acknowledged desire to
destroy a stockholder's blocking position — only the observation that the genuine ones "stood
alongside" the problematic ones. On these facts the answer is comfortable because the succession
plan was conceived on a clear day, but a board that can point to any clear-day plan now has a
template.

### Cold-call notes

- **State the two-part test with the burden.** Board bears it. First, a real non-pretextual threat
  to an important corporate interest, with proper and non-selfish motivations. Second, a response
  reasonable in relation to the threat, neither preclusive nor coercive. Add the tailoring
  requirement — only what is necessary.
- **Say clearly what happened to Blasius.** The "compelling justification" label is gone as an
  independent gate because it made outcomes preordained; the substance is preserved inside Unocal.
  Do not say Blasius was overruled, and do not say it survives unchanged.
- **Keep Schnell's domain precise.** Board acts within its legal power but is motivated for selfish
  reasons. That is the Schnell branch, now absorbed into the motivation half of the first question.
- **Quote the surviving Blasius line.** The threat "cannot be justified on the grounds that the
  board knows what is in the best interests of the stockholders." It is the clearest proof that the
  substance travelled.
- **Have the entire fairness point ready.** A transaction can be entirely fair in price and process
  and still be set aside for inequitable interference with an election. Fairness and equity are
  different inquiries.
- **Explain the preclusion finding, because it is counterintuitive.** Coster lost her blocking
  half-interest and the court still found no preclusion, because three equal owners not bound to
  vote together leave her a swing vote. Preclusion asks what remains, not what was taken — the same
  move as Selectica and Airgas.
- **Use the overreach point if pushed on fairness to Coster.** She filed without any meaningful
  effort to negotiate board composition and asked for full authority and control over the company.
  Her own request generated the existential threat that justified the dilution.
- **Connect to the Airgas note.** The old sharp line — weak protection for tender offers under
  Unocal, robust protection for the franchise under Blasius — has collapsed. Both now run through
  enhanced scrutiny, differing in sensitivity rather than in standard.
- **Numbers worth holding.** Three founders at a third each, reduced to two halves in 2011; a
  $2,125,000 non-binding term sheet in 2014; a $7.3 million valuation from Coster in 2017 against a
  board valuation of $123,869; a one-third interest sold for $41,289.67; two deadlocked meetings on
  May 22 and June 4, 2018; five seats reduced to three.
