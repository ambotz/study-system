## Rule

Revlon duties arise only where the board initiates an active bidding process seeking
to sell itself or to effect a clear break-up, or where, in response to a bidder, it
abandons its long-term strategy for an alternative involving a break-up — and
**inadequate value is not the only cognisable threat** under Unocal, because
stockholders may be threatened by tendering "in ignorance or a mistaken belief" about
a strategic plan.

## Facts

- The Time–Warner combination was the product of **six years of deliberation**, from
  1983–84 through a 1987 strategy committee, a June 1988 long-term planning document
  circulated to every outside director, and a July 1988 board meeting at which Warner
  was chosen from a field including Paramount, Columbia, M.C.A., Fox, MGM, Disney and
  Orion. The length of the process is what makes the original merger a business
  judgment case rather than a defensive one.
- Time's board was **sixteen directors, twelve of them outside**, and the outside
  directors were the ones driving the corporate-governance demands.
- Time **conceded the economics to protect governance**. It wanted an all-cash or
  cash-and-securities acquisition; Warner insisted on a stock swap; talks collapsed
  in 1988 over whether Ross would commit to retire. The deal revived only when Ross
  agreed to retire in five years with Nicholas succeeding him.
- The exchange ratio was **.465 in Warner's favour**, leaving Warner holders with
  about **62%** of the combined company — even though Warner's own advisors had said
  anything over .400 was fair and anything over .450 was "one hell of a deal." Time
  paid a premium in stock to buy control of the board.
- **Control did not pass to anyone.** Time would be owned by a fluid aggregation of
  unaffiliated stockholders both before and after. This is the fact that defeats the
  Revlon claim.
- The March 3 agreement carried an **automatic share exchange** (Time taking 9.4% of
  Warner, Warner 11.1% of Time), **"confidence" letters** from Time's banks promising
  not to finance a third-party acquisition of Time, and a **no-shop** insisted on by
  Warner. *The Chancellor found the confidence letters inconsequential and futile.*
- Time already had a **staggered board, a 15%-trigger poison pill, a fifty-day notice
  period for stockholder motions, and restrictions on calling meetings or acting by
  consent** before any of this began.
- The special committee of outside directors created to oversee the merger concluded
  **it need not retain independent advisors or even meet**. *A bad fact the opinion
  records without comment.*
- On **June 7, 1989, sixteen days after Time mailed its proxy statement** and sixteen
  days before the scheduled stockholder vote, Paramount announced an all-cash
  all-shares offer at **$175**, later raised to **$200**. Time's stock jumped from
  $126 to $170 the next day. The timing is what the board characterised as designed
  to upset or confuse the vote.
- Time **restructured rather than abandoned**: instead of a stock-for-stock merger
  requiring a shareholder vote, an immediate all-cash offer for 51% of Warner at $70
  with the balance to follow, financed by **$7–10 billion of new debt**. The
  restructuring eliminated the vote, which is why Unocal applies to it.
- The board **refused to negotiate with Paramount at all**, and Munro's response
  letter attacked Davis's personal integrity and called the offer "smoke and mirrors."
- The board's stated concern was preserving the **"Time Culture"** — the journalistic
  integrity the outside directors believed Time embodied, and which they feared an
  entertainment merger or a Paramount acquisition would dissolve.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **No Revlon duty arose from the original merger agreement.** There is no substantial
  evidence that Time's board, in negotiating with Warner, "made the dissolution or
  break-up of the corporate entity inevitable, as was the case in Revlon."
- Two circumstances generally implicate Revlon: "when a corporation initiates an
  active bidding process seeking to sell itself or to effect a business
  reorganization involving a clear break-up of the company"; and where, "in response
  to a bidder's offer, a target abandons its long-term strategy and seeks an
  alternative transaction involving the breakup of the company."
- "If, however, the board's reaction to a hostile tender offer is found to constitute
  only a **defensive response** and not an abandonment of the corporation's continued
  existence, Revlon duties are not triggered, though **Unocal duties attach**."
- The court "decline[s] to extend Revlon's application to corporate transactions
  simply because they might be construed as putting a corporation either 'in play' or
  'up for sale'," and the **subjective intent** of directors who worried the market
  might see it that way is "entirely insufficient" to invoke Revlon.
- "**The adoption of structural safety devices alone does not trigger Revlon.**
  Rather... such devices are properly subject to a Unocal analysis."
- The **original** March 3 merger was a proper exercise of business judgment, resting
  on an exhaustive appraisal beginning in 1983–84 and an extensive survey of
  candidates.
- The **revised** June transaction was defence-motivated and is reviewed under Unocal,
  which applies "to all director actions taken, following receipt of Paramount's
  hostile tender offer, that were reasonably determined to be defensive."
- **Prong one — inadequate value is not the only cognisable threat.** The court
  "disapprove[s] of such a narrow and rigid construction of Unocal" and rejects the
  Chancery decisions suggesting that an all-cash all-shares offer within a reasonable
  range cannot be a threat, because that approach "would involve the court in
  substituting its judgment as to what is a 'better' deal for that of a corporation's
  board of directors."
- Three threats were cognisable here: that stockholders "might elect to tender into
  Paramount's cash offer **in ignorance or a mistaken belief** of the strategic benefit
  which a business combination with Warner might produce"; that the conditions
  attached introduced uncertainty skewing comparison; and that the **timing**,
  following Time's proxy notice, was "arguably designed to upset, if not confuse, the
  Time stockholders' vote."
- The board was **adequately informed** notwithstanding its refusal to negotiate.
  Time's lengthy pre-June investigation of merger candidates, Paramount among them,
  "mooted any obligation on Time's part to halt its merger process with Warner to
  reconsider Paramount," and the finding is "materially enhanced by the fact that
  twelve of Time's sixteen board members were outside independent directors."
- **Prong two.** "The obvious requisite to determining the reasonableness of a
  defensive action is a clear identification of the nature of the threat," requiring
  "an evaluation of the importance of the corporate objective threatened; alternative
  methods of protecting that objective; impacts of the 'defensive' action, and other
  relevant factors."
- The response was proportionate because it "was not aimed at 'cramming down' on its
  shareholders a management-sponsored alternative, but rather had as its goal the
  **carrying forward of a pre-existing transaction in an altered form**," and because
  it did not preclude Paramount from bidding for the combined Time–Warner or from
  dropping the condition that the Warner agreement be nullified.
- **The time frame belongs to the board.** "The fiduciary duty to manage a corporate
  enterprise includes the **selection of a time frame** for achievement of corporate
  goals. That duty may not be delegated to the stockholders. **Directors are not
  obliged to abandon a deliberately conceived corporate plan for a short-term
  shareholder profit unless there is clearly no basis to sustain the corporate
  strategy.**"
- Incurring heavy debt to finance the acquisition does not by itself make the decision
  unreasonable "so long as the directors could reasonably perceive the debt load not
  to be so injurious to the corporation as to jeopardize its well being."
- Plaintiffs failed to establish a reasonable likelihood of success on the merits.
  **Affirmed.**

**Rejected**

- *The .465 exchange ratio, leaving Warner holders with 62% of the combined company,
  worked a change of control that put Time up for sale.* Control passed to no one.
  Time would be held by a fluid aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders both before
  and after, so no control premium was extinguished and no Revlon duty arose.
- *The directors' own statements that the market might perceive the merger as putting
  Time up for sale show that it did.* Subjective intent of that kind is entirely
  insufficient, and the court declines to extend Revlon to transactions merely because
  they might be construed as putting a company in play.
- *The lock-up, no-shop and dry-up agreements foreclosed the stockholders from any
  control premium and therefore triggered Revlon.* Structural safety devices alone do
  not trigger Revlon; they are reviewed under Unocal.
- *Because Paramount's offer was all-cash and all-shares, the only conceivable threat
  was inadequate value, so a court must decide whether the price was in fact
  inadequate.* This is "a fundamental misconception of our standard of review under
  Unocal," because it would have the court decide which deal was better. Chancery
  decisions to that effect are rejected.
- *Time never duly investigated Paramount's offer and so could not know it was a
  threat.* The years of candidate analysis, Paramount included, supplied the
  information; Time was under no obligation to negotiate, and its failure to do so
  cannot be called uninformed.
- *The response was unreasonable because it precluded stockholders from accepting the
  tender offer or receiving a control premium in the foreseeable future.* This "stems
  from a fundamental misunderstanding of where the power of corporate governance
  lies." Delaware law commits management to the board, and the choice of time frame
  cannot be delegated to stockholders.

## Context

Time is the case that **separates the two standards** and shows what each one costs a
plaintiff. The plaintiffs needed Revlon, because Revlon makes price the only
legitimate objective and would have condemned a restructuring designed to avoid a
$200 all-cash offer. They got Unocal instead, and under Unocal the board wins, because
prong one accepts a threat as diffuse as stockholder misapprehension and prong two
accepts any response that carries forward a pre-existing plan without cramming down an
alternative.

**The trigger holding did not survive intact.** [[Paramount v. QVC]] four years later
rejects the reading that a break-up is required, and sets the modern test: Revlon
attaches on **a change of corporate control or a break-up**, either one. QVC
distinguishes Time on the ground the Chancellor had actually relied on — that Time
"would be owned by a fluid aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders both before and
after the merger." So the surviving rule from Time is not "break-up required" but the
narrower and still-good proposition that a **stock-for-stock merger among dispersed
holders is not a sale of control**, and that structural defences alone do not trigger
Revlon.

**The Unocal holding, by contrast, expanded and stuck.** "In ignorance or a mistaken
belief" is the origin of **substantive coercion**, the threat that stockholders will
disbelieve the board's view of value and tender into an underpriced offer. That idea
does more work in Delaware law than anything else in the opinion, and
[[Air Products v. Airgas]] is its logical end point: Chancellor Chandler upholds a pill
against a non-coercive, all-cash, fully financed, fully informed offer because the
Supreme Court has recognised inadequate price as substantive coercion and because "the
'selection of a time frame for achievement of corporate goals... may not be delegated
to the stockholders.'" Chandler quotes Time's "deliberately conceived corporate plan"
dictum as the sentence that binds him, and says he does "not share the Airgas board's
confidence" but is "constrained" by it.

Note how differently the same facts read under the other doctrines in this chunk.
Under [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum]] the threat in the paradigm case was a coercive
two-tier structure; here it is the possibility that stockholders will make a mistake.
Under [[Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes]] a board may not prefer anything to price once
the company is being sold; here the board prefers a corporate culture to a $200 cash
offer, and wins, because the company was never being sold.

The one thing the opinion passes over is the special committee. Time's board created a
committee of outside directors to oversee the merger, and the committee "concluded,
shortly after its creation, that it was not necessary either to retain independent
consultants, legal or financial, or even to meet." Against
[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] that is a striking fact to leave unremarked, and it is
available to a plaintiff arguing that the informed-decision finding rests on the
board's *pre-June* work rather than on anything it did once Paramount appeared.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Consolidated appeals from the Court of Chancery's denial of a preliminary injunction
  against Time's tender offer for 51% of Warner at $70 cash.
- Paramount sued as a bidder; two groups of Time stockholders sued separately, adding
  the Revlon claim.
- Affirmed.
- The posture matters because the standard is likelihood of success on a preliminary
  record, and because the court is reviewing **two distinct board decisions** — the
  original March merger under the business judgment rule, and the June restructuring
  under Unocal.

### Facts

Time's traditional business was magazine and book publishing, with pay television
through HBO and Cinemax and cable franchises through American Television and
Communications. Its board had sixteen members, twelve of them outside directors.

As early as 1983–84 Time's executive board began considering expansion into
entertainment. A 1987 special committee of executives concluded Time should move into
ownership and creation of video programming, for two reasons: greater control over
the quality and price of the film product flowing through its cable network, and the
increasing globalisation of the economy. Some outside directors, particularly Luce
and Temple, opposed the move as a threat to Time's editorial integrity — the "Time
Culture," an institution built on journalistic independence that management
deliberately refrained from influencing.

In spring 1987 Steve Ross of Warner and Nicholas of Time discussed a jointly owned
cable venture, abandoned mainly for tax reasons. In August 1987 Levin, Time's vice
chairman and chief strategist, wrote Munro recommending a strategic consolidation with
Warner. In June 1988 a comprehensive long-term planning document went to every outside
director, naming Warner as a candidate. At a July 21, 1988 board meeting with all
outside directors present, the board approved a strategic expansion plan in principle
and told management to keep talking to Warner. Its consensus was that a merger was
feasible **only if Time controlled the resulting board**, and it stressed defining the
governance provisions in advance. Some members worried the combination would put Time
in play and discussed further defensive measures. From a field including Warner,
Paramount, Columbia, M.C.A., Fox, MGM, Disney and Orion, the board concluded Warner
was the superior candidate.

Time wanted to buy Warner for cash or cash and securities; its banker Wasserstein
agreed. Warner insisted on a stock swap to preserve its holders' equity. Time
acquiesced on structure but refused to be cast as the acquired company, and talks
collapsed over governance: Time wanted Ross's co-chief-executive role to be temporary
and wanted him to retire in five years, which Ross treated as a vote of no confidence
and Warner treated as inconsistent with a merger of equals.

Talks resumed in January 1989 after a private dinner at which the outside director
Dingman persuaded Ross that the transitional arrangement was not a slight. Ross agreed
to retire in five years with Nicholas succeeding him. Time's board had already
recognised it might need to pay a premium in the exchange ratio to secure the
governance arrangement, and Levin and the outside director Finkelstein were the
primary proponents of paying one to protect the Time Culture. Warner's advisors told
its board that anything over .400 was fair and anything over .450 was "one hell of a
deal." The parties settled at **.465**, leaving Warner holders with roughly 62% of
Time–Warner.

On March 3, 1989 Time's board unanimously approved the stock-for-stock merger, with
all but one director present. It simultaneously adopted an automatic share exchange
(Time taking 9.4% of Warner, Warner 11.1% of Time, either side able to trigger),
purchased "confidence" letters from its banks promising not to finance a third-party
acquisition of Time, and agreed at Warner's insistence to a no-shop. It also created a
special committee of three outside directors — Finkelstein, Kearns and Opel — to
oversee the merger; the committee soon concluded it need not retain consultants or
meet. Time already had a staggered board, a 15%-trigger pill, a fifty-day notice
period and restrictions on calling meetings or acting by consent.

Public reaction was favourable. The vote was scheduled for June 23 and proxy
statements went out May 24. On June 7 Paramount announced an all-cash offer for all
Time shares at $175. Time's stock went from $126 to $170 overnight. Munro's response
letter attacked Davis's personal integrity and called the offer "smoke and mirrors."
Over eight days the board met three times, found the offer inadequate, concluded the
Warner merger was the better course, and declined to open negotiations.

At the same meetings the board recast the transaction. Instead of a stock-for-stock
merger requiring a stockholder vote, Time would make an immediate all-cash offer for
51% of Warner at $70 per share, with the remaining 49% to follow at $70 in cash and
securities — financed by $7–10 billion of debt, eliminating one of the original deal's
principal benefits. The board considered and rejected a Pac-Man defence, a
recapitalisation, acquiring another company, and changes to capitalisation or dividend
policy. On June 23 Paramount raised to $200; on June 26 Time rejected that too,
maintaining that the Warner transaction offered greater long-term value and did not
threaten Time's survival or its culture.

### Issue

- Whether the March 4 Time–Warner agreement put Time up for sale and thereby triggered
  Revlon duties, given the .465 exchange ratio, the resulting 62% Warner ownership,
  the directors' own expressions of concern, and the defensive devices adopted.
- Whether the original merger agreement was entitled to the business judgment rule.
- Whether Time's board had reasonable grounds to believe Paramount's all-cash
  all-shares offer posed a legally cognisable threat, and whether it was adequately
  informed in rejecting it without negotiation.
- Whether restructuring the Warner transaction to eliminate the stockholder vote was a
  proportionate response.

### Holding

- **On Revlon: not triggered.** No substantial evidence that the board made a break-up
  inevitable; no change of control, since Time remained held by a fluid aggregation of
  unaffiliated stockholders; subjective concerns about perception are insufficient;
  and structural safety devices alone do not trigger Revlon.
- **On the original merger: business judgment.** Six years of deliberation and an
  exhaustive survey of candidates support the Chancellor's conclusion.
- **On Unocal prong one: a cognisable threat existed.** Inadequate value is not the
  only threat. Stockholders might tender in ignorance or mistaken belief about the
  strategic benefit of the Warner combination; the offer's conditions introduced
  uncertainty; and the timing appeared designed to upset the vote. The board was
  adequately informed by its years of analysis, and twelve of sixteen directors were
  outside independent directors.
- **On Unocal prong two: proportionate.** The restructuring carried forward a
  pre-existing transaction in altered form rather than cramming down a
  management-sponsored alternative, and left Paramount free to bid for the combined
  company. The choice of time frame belongs to the board and may not be delegated to
  stockholders.
- **Disposition:** affirmed; no reasonable likelihood of success on the merits.

### Rule

- Revlon duties are generally implicated where a corporation initiates an active
  bidding process seeking to sell itself or to effect a clear break-up, or where, in
  response to a bidder, it abandons its long-term strategy for an alternative
  involving a break-up.
- A board reaction that is only a defensive response, and not an abandonment of the
  corporation's continued existence, triggers Unocal but not Revlon.
- The adoption of structural safety devices alone does not trigger Revlon.
- Unocal applies to all director actions taken after receipt of a hostile tender offer
  that are reasonably determined to be defensive.
- Inadequate value is not the only legally cognisable threat a hostile offer may pose.
- Determining the reasonableness of a defensive action requires clear identification
  of the nature of the threat, evaluation of the corporate objective threatened,
  alternative methods of protecting it, and the impact of the defensive action.
- The fiduciary duty to manage includes selecting the time frame for achieving
  corporate goals, and that duty may not be delegated to stockholders.
- Directors need not abandon a deliberately conceived corporate plan for short-term
  shareholder profit unless there is clearly no basis to sustain the strategy.

### Reasoning

*(Horsey, J.)*

- The court makes the Revlon question **logically prior**: "[a] resolution of that
  issue through application of Revlon has a significant bearing upon the resolution of
  the derivative Unocal issue." If price were the only objective, the restructuring
  could not survive; only because Revlon is out can culture and strategy count.
- The Revlon holding is anchored on **inevitability of break-up** rather than on any
  formal test, which is precisely the reasoning QVC later narrows. But the fact the
  Chancellor relied on — a fluid aggregation of unaffiliated stockholders before and
  after — is the one that survives.
- The court refuses to let **subjective intent** create a legal trigger. Directors who
  worry aloud that the market may see a deal as putting the company in play have not
  thereby put it in play, and a contrary rule would penalise candour.
- On prong one, the rejection of the Chancery line is framed as institutional rather
  than doctrinal. Holding that an all-cash offer in a reasonable range cannot be a
  threat would force courts to decide which deal is better, which "is a distortion of
  the Unocal process."
- The informed-decision finding rests almost entirely on **pre-June work**. Because
  Time had already studied Paramount among the candidates, no obligation arose to
  pause and reconsider; the board's refusal to negotiate is treated as a choice, not
  as ignorance.
- Prong two turns on characterising the restructuring as **continuity, not
  substitution**. The same transaction in a different form is not a cram-down, and the
  measure left Paramount able to bid for the combined entity.
- The time-frame passage is the opinion's broadest statement and its most consequential.
  It converts a disagreement about horizon — short-term cash versus long-term strategy
  — from a question about value into a question about **who decides**, and answers it
  in the board's favour.

### Dissent / concurrence

- Unanimous.

The tension the opinion does not resolve is between the restructuring's stated purpose
and its actual effect. The court accepts that the June transaction merely carried
forward the March plan "in an altered form." But the alteration was precisely that it
no longer required a Time stockholder vote — the vote Paramount's offer was about to
influence — and it replaced a debt-free combination with $7–10 billion of borrowing,
eliminating what Time had publicly promoted to the Senate and the President as a
principal benefit. Calling that continuity rather than substitution is the step a
plaintiff would press hardest.

### Cold-call notes

- **Answer the trigger question in two parts.** Time holds that a break-up must be
  inevitable; QVC then holds four years later that **a change of control alone
  suffices**. Give the modern rule — change of control **or** break-up — and say Time
  survives for the narrower proposition that a stock-for-stock merger among dispersed
  holders is not a sale of control.
- **Have the fluid-aggregation phrase ready.** "[A] fluid aggregation of unaffiliated
  stockholders" both before and after is exactly how QVC distinguishes Time, and it is
  the test that decides most trigger problems.
- **Know that structural devices alone do not trigger Revlon.** They get Unocal. That
  sentence is quoted in [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] and is the doctrinal hinge of
  the deal protection line.
- **Name substantive coercion when asked about the threat.** Stockholders tendering
  "in ignorance or a mistaken belief" about the strategic benefit. It originates here
  and it is what Airgas turns on.
- **Be ready to defend the refusal to negotiate.** The answer is that the board had
  studied Paramount for years among its candidates, so no fresh investigation was
  required, and twelve of sixteen directors were outside. The weaker part of the record
  is the special committee that never met.
- **Quote the time-frame dictum precisely.** Directors "are not obliged to abandon a
  deliberately conceived corporate plan for a short-term shareholder profit unless
  there is clearly no basis to sustain the corporate strategy." Chandler calls it
  "powerful dictum" and treats it as binding in Airgas.
- **Expect to be pushed on whether this is entrenchment.** The honest version is that
  the board preferred its own conception of the company to $200 a share in cash, and
  the court's answer is that choosing a horizon is a management decision that cannot be
  delegated to stockholders. Whether that is deference or abdication is the live
  question in the whole chunk.
- **Numbers worth holding.** Sixteen directors, twelve outside; .465 exchange ratio
  against a .450 "hell of a deal" benchmark; 62% Warner ownership; $175 rising to $200
  per share from Paramount; $126 to $170 overnight; $70 for 51% of Warner; $7–10
  billion of debt.
