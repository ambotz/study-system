## Rule

A parent owes a fiduciary duty to its subsidiary, but **that alone does not evoke
intrinsic fairness**. The heightened standard applies only when the fiduciary duty "is
accompanied by **self-dealing** — the situation when a parent is on both sides of a
transaction with its subsidiary."

Self-dealing means the parent "receives something from the subsidiary **to the exclusion
of, and detriment to**, the minority stockholders." Absent that, the business judgment
rule governs, however dominated the board.

## Facts

- Sinclair owned about **97%** of Sinven; the plaintiff held about 3,000 of 120,000
  publicly held shares. Sinclair nominated every Sinven director, and the Chancellor found
  as fact that the directors **were not independent** — almost all were officers,
  directors or employees within the Sinclair complex.
- **Domination and fiduciary duty were conceded.** *Which is precisely why the case
  matters: total control plus a conceded fiduciary duty still did not produce entire
  fairness on two of three claims.*
- **Dividends.** From 1960 through 1966 Sinven paid **$108,000,000**, exceeding its
  earnings by **$38,000,000**, during a period when Sinclair needed large amounts of cash.
  The payments complied with **8 Del. C. § 170**, which the plaintiff conceded.
- **Crucially, the dividends were pro rata.** "[A] proportionate share of this money was
  received by the minority shareholders of Sinven." *This one fact decides the largest
  claim.*
- **Expansion.** Sinclair developed oil fields in Alaska, Canada, Paraguay and elsewhere
  through other subsidiaries, confining Sinven to Venezuela, consistent with a company-wide
  policy of exploiting properties through subsidiaries located in each country. The
  Chancellor found Sinclair "made no real effort to expand Sinven."
- **But the plaintiff could point to no opportunity that came to Sinven.** *No usurpation,
  because nothing was taken.*
- **The contract.** In 1961 Sinclair created International to coordinate foreign
  operations, then caused Sinven to contract to sell all its crude and refined products to
  International at specified prices, with minimum quantities and payment on receipt.
  **International paid as much as 30 days late and bought less than the minimum.**

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Dividends — business judgment, reversed.** Because the dividends were proportionate,
  "Sinclair received nothing from Sinven to the exclusion of its minority stockholders. As
  such, these dividends were not self-dealing." The Chancellor erred in applying intrinsic
  fairness. Motives are "immaterial unless the plaintiff can show that the dividend payments
  resulted from improper motives and **amounted to waste**."
- **The court preserves the possibility**: a dividend by a dominated board can trigger
  intrinsic fairness where it is "in essence self-dealing." The illustration given is a
  subsidiary with Class X stock held by the parent and Class Y by the minority, declaring a
  dividend **on Class X only**.
- **Expansion — business judgment, reversed.** No opportunity came to Sinven, so none was
  usurped. And "with which subsidiaries should these opportunities have been shared?" No
  evidence showed "a unique need or ability of Sinven to develop these opportunities." The
  allocation decision is business judgment, reviewable only on "a showing of gross and
  palpable overreaching."
- **Contract — intrinsic fairness, affirmed.** "Clearly, Sinclair's act of contracting with
  its dominated subsidiary was self-dealing." Sinclair received the products while the
  minority could not share in them; if the contract was breached, Sinclair got them "to the
  detriment of Sinven's minority shareholders."
- "Although a parent need not bind itself by a contract with its dominated subsidiary,
  Sinclair chose to operate in this manner. As Sinclair has received the benefits of this
  contract, so must it comply with the contractual duties."
- Sinclair failed its burden. Late payments "were clearly breaches for which Sinven should
  have sought and received adequate damages," and Sinclair did not prove Sinven "could not
  possibly have produced or someway have obtained the contract minimums."

**Rejected**

- *A parent that dominates its subsidiary and fixes the terms of every dealing should face
  intrinsic fairness across the board.* Fiduciary duty "alone will not evoke the intrinsic
  fairness standard." Self-dealing is the additional element.
- *The dividends were driven by Sinclair's own need for cash, an improper motive.* Motive is
  immaterial absent waste, and the payments complied with § 170. Where value flows
  proportionately, the parent's reason for wanting it is not the question.
- *Sinclair drained Sinven of cash and deliberately excluded it from a company-wide
  expansion programme.* Both findings accepted, and neither amounts to self-dealing, because
  the minority lost nothing that ever belonged to Sinven.

## Context

The foundational Delaware answer to what a **controller** owes, and the case is more
defence-friendly than its reputation suggests. Two of three claims go to business judgment
despite 97% ownership, a concededly non-independent board, and $38 million of dividends over
earnings.

The operative concept is **proportionality**. A controller that takes value *pro rata* has
not self-dealt, however much value moves and whatever its motives. A controller that takes
something the minority cannot share — the products under the International contract — has.
That test is the ancestor of the "**benefit not shared with the corporation's stockholders
generally**" language now in post-SB 21 § 144(e)(3)'s definition of a controlling stockholder
transaction. See [[Interested director and officer transactions - 144(a)]].

[[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]], assigned the same day, is the other half of the inquiry.
Sinclair concerns **what** a conceded controller may do; Tesla concerns **who counts** as a
controller at all. Sinclair had 97% and conceded; Musk had 22.1% and fought.

The § 170 point is worth keeping for session 24: statutory compliance answers the question
whether a dividend was **lawful**, and says nothing about whether it was a **breach of
fiduciary duty**. The two inquiries run in parallel throughout the course.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Derivative action on behalf of Sinven, tried in the Court of Chancery, which ordered
  Sinclair to account on all three claims. Appeal by Sinclair to the Delaware Supreme Court.
- Post-trial, with findings of fact — including the finding that the directors were not
  independent, which the Supreme Court accepts and then holds insufficient.
- Reversed in part, affirmed in part.

### Facts

Sinclair operated primarily as a holding company in oil exploration, production and
marketing. Sinven, incorporated in 1922, was its Venezuelan operating arm and from 1959
worked exclusively there. Sinclair held about 97% of Sinven's stock and nominated its entire
board, which was drawn almost entirely from the Sinclair complex.

Between 1960 and 1966 Sinven paid out $108 million in dividends — $38 million more than it
earned in the period — at a time when Sinclair itself needed cash. The plaintiff conceded the
payments complied with § 170's surplus and net profits requirements, and attacked them
instead as motivated by the parent's needs and as starving Sinven of capital to grow.

Over the same years Sinclair expanded aggressively, buying and developing oil properties in
Alaska, Canada, Paraguay and elsewhere — always through subsidiaries located in the relevant
country, and never through Sinven, which stayed in Venezuela.

Separately, in 1961 Sinclair formed International to consolidate its foreign crude purchases
and had Sinven contract to sell everything it produced to International, at set prices, in set
minimum quantities, with payment due on receipt. International then paid late and bought less
than the minimum, and Sinven — controlled by the same parent — did nothing about it.

### Issue

- Whether every transaction between a dominating parent and its subsidiary is tested by
  intrinsic fairness, or only those involving self-dealing.
- Whether dividends exceeding earnings, paid pro rata by a dominated board at a time of
  parent need, constitute self-dealing.
- Whether confining a subsidiary's operations while expanding through other subsidiaries
  constitutes usurpation of corporate opportunity.
- Whether causing a subsidiary not to enforce a contract against the parent's affiliate is
  self-dealing.

### Holding

- **On the standard: self-dealing is the trigger.** Fiduciary duty alone does not produce
  intrinsic fairness; the parent must be on both sides and receive something to the exclusion
  of and detriment to the minority.
- **On dividends: business judgment.** Pro rata payments give the minority its proportionate
  share, so nothing is received to their exclusion. Motive is immaterial absent waste.
- **On expansion: business judgment.** No opportunity ever came to Sinven, so nothing was
  usurped, and allocating opportunities among subsidiaries is a judgment reviewable only for
  gross and palpable overreaching.
- **On the contract: intrinsic fairness, and Sinclair fails it.** Contracting with a dominated
  subsidiary is self-dealing, and Sinclair did not prove that causing non-enforcement was
  intrinsically fair.
- **Disposition:** reversed as to dividends and expansion; affirmed as to the contract;
  remanded.

### Rule

- A parent owes a fiduciary duty to its subsidiary in parent-subsidiary dealings.
- Fiduciary duty alone does not evoke intrinsic fairness; the standard applies only where the
  duty is accompanied by **self-dealing**.
- Self-dealing occurs where the parent, by virtue of domination, causes the subsidiary to act
  so that the parent receives something from the subsidiary **to the exclusion of, and
  detriment to**, the minority stockholders.
- Intrinsic fairness "involves both a high degree of fairness and a shift in the burden of
  proof" — the parent must prove objective fairness under careful judicial scrutiny.
- A pro rata dividend is not self-dealing; a dividend paid only on the parent's class of stock
  may be.
- Compliance with § 170 answers legality, not fiduciary duty; motive is immaterial absent
  waste.
- A corporate opportunity claim requires that the opportunity **came to** the subsidiary.
- A parent need not contract with its dominated subsidiary, but having taken the benefits of a
  contract it must comply with the duties.

### Reasoning

*(Wolcott, C.J.)*

- The opinion narrows a standard that Chancery had applied broadly, and does so with a single
  distinction: whether value moved **proportionately**. That test is administrable, which is
  much of its appeal — it avoids asking courts to price the parent's motives.
- The Class X / Class Y hypothetical is the court's own illustration and does the doctrinal
  work, showing that the holding is about disproportion rather than about dividends.
- On expansion the court adds a second, independent answer: even assuming error, allocating
  opportunities among many subsidiaries is a judgment no court is equipped to second-guess,
  and no evidence showed Sinven uniquely able to exploit them.
- On the contract, self-dealing is found almost summarily — the products went to Sinclair and
  the minority could not share in them — and the burden then does all the remaining work.
  Sinclair loses not because the contract was unfair but because it could not **prove** that
  non-enforcement was fair.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; unanimous.
- The tension worth two lines: the Chancellor found Sinclair drained Sinven of cash precisely
  so that it could not expand, and pursued a company-wide growth strategy from which Sinven
  was deliberately excluded. Those findings stand, and the minority still loses, because the
  harm is real but not **disproportionate**. That is the cost of choosing an administrable
  test over an inquiry into motive.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect the question: why did the dividends survive and the contract not?** One sentence:
  the dividends were pro rata and the products were not. Proportionality is the whole case.
- **The trap** is arguing Sinclair's motive. The court says motive is immaterial absent waste,
  and the plaintiff conceded § 170 compliance. An answer built on "they needed the cash" has
  restated the losing argument.
- **Have the Class X / Class Y hypothetical ready.** It is the court's own example of a
  dividend that *would* be self-dealing, and it shows the test is disproportion rather than
  form.
- **Note what the corporate opportunity claim needed.** An opportunity that came **to Sinven**.
  Buccola may push on whether that is too formal, given the parent controlled which
  subsidiary received what — which is the strongest attack on the case.
- **Carry it forward.** "[T]o the exclusion of, and detriment to, the minority" becomes, in
  post-SB 21 § 144(e)(3), "a financial or other benefit **not shared with the corporation's
  stockholders generally**." Same idea, statutory form.
- **Numbers:** 97% ownership, 3,000 of 120,000 public shares, $108 million in dividends,
  $38 million over earnings, 1960–66, payments up to 30 days late.
