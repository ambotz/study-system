## Rule

Stockholder ratification of an equity plan's **general parameters** does not ratify
the **discretionary awards** directors later make to themselves under it. Where a
stockholder properly alleges the directors exercised that discretion inequitably,
the ratification defence is unavailable and the directors must prove entire fairness.

## Facts

- Under **8 Del. C. § 141(h)** the board is authorised to fix director compensation —
  but doing so is self-interested, so absent another factor the awards "lie outside
  the business judgment rule's presumptive protection" and entire fairness applies.
- The 2015 Equity Incentive Plan reserved **30,881,296 shares** for roughly 1,800
  officers, employees, non-employee directors and service providers.
- The EIP carried **real limits**: 17,646,455 shares for options or restricted stock
  and 13,234,841 for RSUs or performance shares; no single employee could receive more
  than 25% of either pool; and **non-employee directors in the aggregate were capped
  at 30%** of all option or restricted shares. *These are the "meaningful limits" that
  won dismissal below — and they do not save the awards.*
- **The proxy told stockholders the awards were undetermined**: "[t]he number, types
  and terms of awards to be made pursuant to the [EIP] are subject to the discretion of
  the Committee and have not been determined at this time, and will not be determined
  until subsequent to stockholder approval." *This is the fact that decides the case.*
- **96.25% of voting shares approved the EIP** (79.1% of shares outstanding). *A
  crushing margin, and irrelevant, because the vote did not approve the awards.*
- The board then awarded **itself 7.8 million shares**, fair value **$51,653,997** —
  just under half the options available to directors and nearly 30% of the restricted
  shares, on a five-year vesting schedule.
- Each non-employee director received **more than $2,100,000 in 2015**, against a
  2014 range of **$97,200 to $207,005**. That "eclips[ed] director pay at every Wall
  Street firm," versus a $198,000 median at similarly sized companies and $260,000 at
  much larger ones — and **more than twenty-three times** the $87,556 median award at
  companies after comparable mutual-to-stock conversions.
- CEO Cummings received $20,006,957 in total 2015 compensation, **seven times** his
  2014 package; COO Cama $15,318,257, **nine times** his. Cummings' award ran **3,683%**
  above the post-conversion median for CEOs, Cama's **5,384%** above the median for
  second-highest-paid executives.
- The plaintiffs alleged stockholders were told the EIP would reward **future**
  performance while the board used it to reward **past** efforts already compensated
  in the 2015 packages.

## Court Ruling

**Held**

- **Ratification is available in three situations**: where stockholders approved the
  specific awards; where the plan is **self-executing**, making awards over time on
  fixed criteria with amounts and terms already approved; and where directors exercise
  discretion after approval. "The first two scenarios present no real problems."
- **In the third, ratification cannot foreclose review.** Where a stockholder properly
  alleges directors breached fiduciary duties in exercising discretion under a plan's
  general parameters, the defence is unavailable on a motion to dismiss and the
  directors must prove the awards entirely fair.
- Director action is **"twice-tested"** — first for legal authorisation, second by
  equity. Stockholders "entrust directors with broad legal authority precisely because
  they know that authority must be exercised consistently with equitable principles of
  fiduciary duty."
- "[I]nequitable action does not become permissible simply because it is legally
  possible" under the authority granted.
- Applied: the EIP is a discretionary plan, the plaintiffs pleaded facts supporting a
  reasonable inference of unfair and excessive self-awards, so ratification cannot
  dismiss the complaint. **Reversed and remanded.**

**Rejected**

- *The EIP contained meaningful, specific limits on awards to director beneficiaries,
  so the awards fall within what stockholders approved.* This was the Court of
  Chancery's ground for dismissal and the Supreme Court reverses it. The **"meaningful
  limits"** test drawn from *3COM* and *Seinfeld v. Slager* does not survive as a route
  to dismissal for discretionary awards; specificity of what was submitted, not the
  existence of a ceiling, is what matters.
- *96.25% of voting shares approved the plan.* The size of the vote is beside the
  point when the thing approved was the parameters rather than the awards. Ratification
  "cannot be taken to have approved specific bargains not yet proposed" (*Gottlieb*).
- *Stockholders granted the legal authority, so exercising it cannot be a breach.*
  Legal authority and equitable constraint are separate tests, and the Delaware
  doctrine of ratification "does not embrace a 'blank check' theory" (*Sample v.
  Morgan*).

## Context

Read against [[Bayer v. Beran]] as the two poles of the same session. Bayer shows a
conflicted transaction **surviving** rigorous scrutiny on its merits; Investors
Bancorp shows a conflicted transaction that **cannot even reach** the safe harbour it
claimed. In both, the fiduciary carries the burden once the conflict is established.

The doctrinal line the opinion traces is worth holding as a sequence. *Kerbs* and
*Gottlieb* (1952) allowed ratification for **self-executing** plans with specific
awards disclosed, and refused it for "specific bargains not yet proposed." *3COM* and
*Criden* extended it to discretionary awards within stockholder-approved parameters.
*Sample v. Morgan* refused a "blank check." *Seinfeld v. Slager* split the difference
with a **"meaningful limits"** requirement. Investors Bancorp resolves the line by
returning to specificity — and the practical effect is that a ceiling, however
carefully drafted, no longer buys dismissal.

Against the statutory scheme: § 141(h) grants the **legal authority** to fix director
compensation, and the case is about the **equitable limit** on exercising it. That is
the same charter-versus-equity structure as
[[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]], run the other way — there the
question was whether the corporation had power at all; here the power is conceded and
the constraint is fiduciary.

Note the ratification vocabulary carries forward. "Informed, uncoerced" stockholder
approval by a "disinterested majority" is the formula that appears in
[[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings]] and, now in statutory form, in the post-SB 21
§ 144(a)(2) and § 144(b)(2) — "an informed, uncoerced, affirmative vote of a majority
of the votes cast by the disinterested stockholders." Investors Bancorp is about what
that vote can and cannot reach.

## Class layer

### Posture

- Consolidated stockholder complaints in the Court of Chancery alleging breach of
  fiduciary duty for excessive self-compensation; the defendants moved to dismiss.
- Chancery **dismissed** as to the non-employee directors, holding the EIP contained
  meaningful, specific limits. Supreme Court reviewed **de novo** and reversed.
- **Posture does real work.** Every number in the opinion is an allegation, and the
  holding is only that the plaintiffs pleaded facts supporting "a pleading stage
  reasonable inference." Nothing is found. The consequence of reversal is that the
  directors must now prove entire fairness on remand — which in practice is the
  settlement pressure the case is known for.

### Facts

Investors Bancorp had recently converted from mutual to stock form. In March 2015 the
board proposed an Equity Incentive Plan, framed to stockholders as providing
incentives to "promote [the Company's] growth and performance" and to give the company
flexibility "to continue to attract, motivate and retain highly qualified officers,
employees and directors."

The plan reserved nearly 31 million shares across about 1,800 potential recipients,
with per-category caps and a 30% aggregate ceiling for non-employee directors. The
proxy was explicit that nobody yet knew what the awards would be. Stockholders
approved it overwhelmingly at the June 2015 annual meeting.

The board then granted itself 7.8 million shares with a fair value just over $51.6
million. Non-employee directors went from roughly $100,000–$200,000 in 2014 to more
than $2.1 million each. The CEO's package multiplied sevenfold and the COO's ninefold.
Measured against companies that had made the same mutual-to-stock conversion, the
awards ran into four-figure percentages above median.

### Issue

- Whether stockholder approval of an equity incentive plan's general parameters
  ratifies the specific discretionary awards directors subsequently grant themselves
  under it.
- Whether the presence of "meaningful limits" in the plan is sufficient to sustain a
  ratification defence at the pleading stage.

### Holding

- **On ratification of discretionary awards: not available.** Where stockholders
  approve general parameters and leave allocation to director discretion, and a
  stockholder properly alleges inequitable exercise of that discretion, ratification
  cannot be used to dismiss. The directors must demonstrate entire fairness.
- **On "meaningful limits": insufficient.** A ceiling does not convert unapproved
  awards into approved ones. The test is the **specificity of what was submitted** to
  stockholders, not whether the plan bounded the discretion.
- **On the pleadings: adequately alleged.** The magnitude, the departure from peer
  and prior-year compensation, and the past-versus-future framing support a reasonable
  inference of breach.
- **Disposition:** reversed and remanded.

### Rule

- § 141(h) authorises the board to fix director compensation unless restricted by the
  charter or bylaws.
- Because the board is self-interested when fixing its own compensation, those awards
  fall outside the business judgment rule's presumptive protection, and entire fairness
  applies when properly challenged.
- Stockholder ratification — approval by a fully informed, uncoerced and disinterested
  majority — restores deferential review in three settings: specific awards approved;
  a self-executing plan with fixed criteria and approved amounts and terms; and,
  formerly, discretion exercised within approved parameters.
- Ratification "cannot be taken to have approved specific bargains not yet proposed."
- Delaware's ratification doctrine "does not embrace a 'blank check' theory."
- Director action is twice-tested: for legal authorisation, and by equity.
- Inequitable action does not become permissible because it is legally possible.

### Reasoning

*(Seitz, J.)*

- The opinion frames the problem as a balance between "utility of the ratification
  defence and the need for judicial scrutiny of certain self-interested discretionary
  acts," and resolves it "by focusing on the **specificity** of the acts submitted to
  the stockholders for approval." That word is the holding.
- The candid premise: "Human nature being what it is, self-interested discretionary
  acts by directors should in an appropriate case be subject to review by the Court of
  Chancery."
- The historical survey is doing argumentative work, not decoration. *Kerbs* ratified a
  self-executing option plan; *Gottlieb*, decided the next day, refused ratification
  for future awards whose terms nobody knew. The court treats its own 1952 pair as
  already containing the specificity principle, so *3COM* and *Seinfeld* are the
  deviation being corrected.
- *Sample v. Morgan* supplies the theory in the passage the court quotes at length:
  stockholders entrust broad legal authority "precisely because they know that
  authority must be exercised consistently with equitable principles," relying on "the
  policing of equity."
- The allegations are recited in comparative form throughout — against 2014, against
  peers, against post-conversion medians — which is how magnitude becomes a pleadable
  inference rather than mere dissatisfaction.

### Dissent / concurrence

- None; unanimous.
- The cost of the rule, worth two lines: after this decision no discretionary equity
  plan can be drafted to secure dismissal, however carefully its limits are set, so
  every challenged director award goes to entire fairness and therefore past a motion
  to dismiss. That is a powerful settlement lever on facts far less extreme than
  these. The court's answer is specificity — submit the actual awards — which is
  available but costly, since it requires a stockholder vote on each grant.

### Cold-call notes

- **Expect to be asked how the board should have papered this.** Two clean routes, both
  in the opinion: submit the **specific awards** for stockholder approval, or use a
  **self-executing** plan whose amounts and terms are fixed by criteria approved in
  advance. Discretion is what costs them the defence.
- **The trap** is arguing the 96.25% vote. The margin is irrelevant; what matters is
  what was voted on. The proxy said the awards "have not been determined at this time."
  Quoting that sentence is the whole answer.
- **Do not say "meaningful limits."** The EIP *had* meaningful limits and that is why
  Chancery dismissed. The Supreme Court reversed. Repeating the test that lost is the
  most likely wrong answer in this case.
- **Have "twice-tested" ready**, with its two halves: legal authorisation under
  § 141(h) and the approved plan, then equity. It is the most quotable formulation in
  the case and it generalises well beyond compensation.
- **Be ready on where the burden lands.** Reversal does not mean the directors lose. It
  means they must prove entire fairness on remand — a burden, not a verdict. Compare
  [[Bayer v. Beran]], where the fiduciary carried exactly that burden and won.
- **Numbers worth keeping:** 30,881,296 shares reserved; 30% aggregate cap for
  non-employee directors; 96.25% approval; 7.8 million shares awarded; $51,653,997
  total; $2.1 million per non-employee director against $97,200–$207,005 the prior
  year; Cummings 7x, Cama 9x; 3,683% and 5,384% above post-conversion medians.
