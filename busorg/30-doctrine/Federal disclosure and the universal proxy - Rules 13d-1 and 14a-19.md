## Rule statement

Two federal rules bracket an activist campaign. **Rule 13d-1 governs the buying; Rule
14a-19 governs the asking.** Everything else in either rule is plumbing.

**Rule 13d-1 — cross five per cent with a control purpose and you must announce
yourself.** "Any person who, after acquiring... the beneficial ownership of any equity
security of a class which is specified in paragraph (i)(1)... is directly or indirectly
the beneficial owner of **more than five percent** of the class shall, **within five
business days** after the date of the acquisition, file with the Commission, a statement
containing the information required by **Schedule 13D**."

The entire architecture of the rule turns on **one question**: are you holding with a
purpose or effect of "**changing or influencing the control of the issuer**"?

| | Control purpose | No control purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **What you file** | Schedule 13D — the long form, including your plans for the issuer | Schedule 13G — short form |
| **When** | 5 business days after crossing 5% | Quarterly-ish (45 days after quarter end), or 5 business days after month end if you pass 10% |
| **If you change your mind** | — | You flip to 13D in 5 business days, **and you may not vote or buy more** until the tenth day after you file |

**Rule 14a-19 — run your own slate and everyone's names go on everyone's card.** "No
person may solicit proxies in support of director nominees other than the registrant's
nominees unless such person" gives the company advance notice of its nominees, files a
definitive proxy statement, and "**[s]olicits the holders of shares representing at least
67% of the voting power** of shares entitled to vote on the election of directors."

In exchange, both sides' cards "[s]et forth the names of **all** persons nominated for
election by the registrant and by any person or group of persons that has complied with
this section." That is the **universal proxy**.

## Elements

### Why each rule exists — read this before the mechanics

**Rule 13d-1 is an early-warning system.** It is the operative half of the **Williams
Act**, the 1968 answer to silent accumulations. The trade Congress struck was not that
control contests are bad, but that they should not be **invisible**: before someone
takes control, the board and the market are entitled to know who is buying, with whose
money, and what they intend to do with the company. Section 13(d)(1)(C) is the tell —
the filer must disclose, "if the purpose of the purchases... is to acquire control,"
any plans "to liquidate such issuer, to sell its assets to or merge it with any other
persons, or to make any other major change in its business or corporate structure."

**Rule 14a-19 fixes the two-card problem.** Before it took effect in 2022, a holder
voting by proxy had to choose *one side's card in full*. If a dissident nominated three
candidates for a ten-seat board, a holder who wanted those three plus seven incumbents
could not express that on either card; only a holder attending in person could split a
ticket. The rule makes **proxy voting mirror in-person voting**, and the practical
consequence is that the **short slate** — three seats, not control — became a cheap and
credible campaign. This is the single most important fact about the rule: it lowered the
cost of activism substantially, and it did so in 2022, the year after
[[The Williams Companies Stockholder Litigation]].

### Rule 13d-1 — the five things that carry it

1. **The threshold is "more than five percent" of a registered class** of equity
   securities. Non-voting classes are excluded. § 13(d)(4): the denominator excludes
   treasury shares, and Rule 13d-1(j) lets you rely on the issuer's last periodic report
   for the share count unless you know it is wrong.
2. **"Beneficial ownership" is broader than record ownership** — Rule 13d-3 reaches
   anyone with voting power or investment power, and counts shares obtainable within
   sixty days. This is why a pill that sweeps in options and other synthetic equity is
   said to go *beyond* the federal baseline.
3. **A group is a person.** § 13(d)(3): "When two or more persons act as a partnership,
   limited partnership, syndicate, or other **group** for the purpose of **acquiring,
   holding, or disposing** of securities of an issuer, such syndicate or group shall be
   deemed a 'person'." Note the three verbs — they do not include **voting**. Holders
   who merely arrive at the same view without an agreement are not a group, which is what
   leaves **wolf packs** outside the regime.
4. **Control purpose is the switch, and it is about purpose or *effect*.** Rule
   13d-1(b)(1)(i) and (c)(1) both exempt only a person who holds "**not with the purpose
   nor with the effect of changing or influencing the control of the issuer**, nor in
   connection with or as a participant in any transaction having such purpose or effect."
   The same phrase appears in § 13(d)(5). One phrase, used four times — learn it.
5. **The teeth are in the cooling-off, not the filing.** Rule 13d-1(e)(2): from the moment
   a 13G filer acquires or holds with a control purpose until the tenth day after the 13D
   is filed, that person "shall not (i) Vote or direct the voting of the securities...
   or (ii) Acquire an additional beneficial ownership interest." Rule 13d-1(f)(2) imposes
   the same freeze on anyone crossing **twenty per cent**. A late filer is therefore
   disenfranchised and frozen out of the market for the duration, which matters far more
   in a live contest than the filing itself.

### Rule 13d-1 — who gets the short form, in one line each

- **13d-1(b) — qualified institutions.** Brokers, banks, insurers, registered funds,
  registered advisers, ERISA and public plans, parent holding companies, savings
  associations, church plans, comparable non-U.S. institutions, and groups made up
  entirely of these — but **only** if holding "in the ordinary course of the person's
  business" and without control purpose. This is the box BlackRock, Vanguard and State
  Street sit in.
- **13d-1(c) — passive investors.** Anyone without control purpose who is not a
  qualified institution and holds **under twenty per cent**. Five business days to file,
  same as 13D, but the short form.
- **13d-1(d)** catches holders exempt from 13D by statute, on the quarterly cycle.
- **13d-1(g)** pushes an institution out of (b) the moment it stops holding in the
  ordinary course; **13d-1(h)** lets a filer go back to 13G once the disqualifier is gone.

### Rule 14a-19 — the four obligations, in order

| Step | Content | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Dissident notice** | Names of all nominees, plus a statement of intent to solicit 67% | **60 days** before the anniversary of last year's annual meeting |
| **2. Company notice back** | Names of all the registrant's nominees | **50 days** before that anniversary |
| **3. File a definitive proxy statement** | Under Rule 14a-6(b) | Later of **25 days** before the meeting or **5 days** after the company files its definitive statement |
| **4. Actually solicit** | Holders of at least **67% of the voting power** entitled to vote on the election | Through the campaign |

Both sides must update promptly if their slate or intent changes: (c) for the dissident,
(d) for the registrant.

**The 67% requirement is the price of admission, and it is the point.** Universal proxy
puts a dissident's nominees onto the company's own card, which the company pays to
distribute. Requiring the dissident to solicit two-thirds of the voting power stops a
nominal campaign from free-riding onto that card. It is a real cost — a genuine
solicitation of most of the register — but small next to printing and mailing a rival
card to everyone.

### Rule 14a-19 — what the card must look like

Paragraph (e) is form regulation, and it exists so that the card cannot be used to bury
the other side. Every card must list **all** nominees from both sides (plus any nominee
included through a proxy-access bylaw), **clearly distinguish** the groups, list
alphabetically by last name **within** each group, use the **same font type, style and
size** for everyone, and prominently disclose the maximum number of nominees that may be
voted for and what happens to an over-voted, under-voted or blank card. Paragraph (f)
permits vote-for-a-group-as-a-whole boxes, but only alongside a matching withhold box,
and not where a proxy-access nominee is on the card.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Source of authority | § 13(d) and § 14(a) — both rule-making delegations; the operative law is in the C.F.R., not the statute |
| Who is regulated | 13d-1: any **beneficial owner** crossing 5%. 14a-19: any **soliciting person** running non-registrant nominees, and the registrant too, once someone does |
| Trigger | 13d-1: acquisition taking you over 5% of a registered class. 14a-19: soliciting proxies for nominees other than the registrant's |
| Nature | **Disclosure and process regulation, not a fiduciary standard.** Nobody is weighing reasonableness; the questions are binary — did you file, on time, and did you solicit 67% |
| Enforcement | SEC action; issuer suits for injunctive relief; and in practice the decisive remedy is that a **non-compliant dissident's nominees cannot be voted for** |
| Interaction with state law | These rules set a **floor of disclosure and a form for the card**. They do not decide who wins, and they do not displace Delaware's advance-notice bylaws, § 211 meeting mechanics, or [[Interference with the franchise - Schnell, Blasius and the Coster standard]] |

## Exceptions

- **Rule 14a-19 does not reach consent solicitations** (g)(1), or elections at registered
  investment companies and BDCs (g)(2). The consent carve-out matters in Delaware, where
  [[Stockholder voting machinery - 212, 216, 141(d) and 228]] makes action by written
  consent under § 228(a) available unless the charter removes it — a consent campaign
  therefore avoids the universal card entirely.
- **§ 13(d)(6) exemptions**: acquisitions made through a Securities Act registration
  statement, and creeping acquisitions of **2% or less in any twelve months**.
- **The regime is about registered classes.** Private companies, and non-voting classes,
  are outside it.
- **Deadlines falling on a weekend or holiday** roll to the next business day
  (Instruction 1 to (b)(1) and (d)).

## Leading case

[[The Williams Companies Stockholder Litigation]] — not because it applies these rules,
but because it is the case in which their **gaps** are the asserted justification for a
defensive measure, and the court's treatment of those gaps is the doctrinal payoff.

The Williams board's third asserted threat was the **lightning strike**: "[l]ightning
strikes go undetected under the federal disclosure regime, which requires stockholders to
disclose their ownership position after crossing the 5% threshold but gives stockholders
**ten days** to do so," during which buying may continue, and which "does not capture
'**wolf pack**' activity." So the board adopted a pill with a 5% trigger, a beneficial
ownership definition reaching synthetic equity, and an "acting in concert" provision
reaching parallel conduct — a **private gap-filling regime**, each feature mapped onto one
federal shortfall.

The court assumed that gap filling is a legitimate corporate objective and struck the pill
on **proportionality** instead, because the plan swept far wider than the gaps it claimed
to close. Its stated worry about the alternative is the sentence to remember: recognising
gap filling as a threat would give "all Delaware corporations subject to the federal
disclosure regime... a ready-made basis for adopting a pill" — "an omnipresent
justification."

**The premise has since narrowed, and this is the most valuable thing in this module.**
Section 13(d)(1) says a filer must report "within ten days after such acquisition **or
within such shorter time as the Commission may establish by rule**." The Commission used
that authority: the **2023 amendments** (88 FR 76981) cut the initial Schedule 13D
deadline from ten calendar days to **five business days**, and tightened the 13G cycle as
well. The window Williams described is roughly half what it was. Two of the three gaps —
the delay, and the extension of the 13G timetable — are materially smaller; the
**wolf-pack gap under § 13(d)(3) remains**, because parallel conduct without an agreement
to acquire, hold or dispose still is not a group.

## Best counter-case

There is no litigated counter-case in this course; the counterweight is the **argument for
private gap filling**, which Williams describes respectfully and does not reject.

The case for it: the federal regime was designed for a 1968 world of blockholders and
tender offers. It measures ownership by voting and investment power, so **derivatives**
sit outside it; it defines a group by agreement to acquire, hold or dispose, so **informal
coordination** sits outside it; and even at five business days it leaves a window in which
a determined buyer can move well past five per cent before anyone knows. Kahan and Rock,
among others, have argued that a **narrow** pill tracking exactly those gaps is a
rational private response. Williams expressly declines to decide whether such a pill would
be valid — "[t]his decision need not address whether a true gap-filling pill would be
permissible."

The case against it, which is the one the court found persuasive on these facts: a
justification available to **every** issuer at **all** times is not a threat but a
standing licence, and it would convert a situational defence — these are "nuclear
weapon[s] of corporate governance," handled with "the delicacy they deserve" — into a
default. Add the 2023 amendments and the argument weakens further: the gap that is left
is narrower, so a measure justified by it must be narrower too.

**The live question**, and a good one to be ready for: after the 2023 amendments, could a
board defend a pill that triggers only on undisclosed accumulation past five per cent,
uses the federal beneficial-ownership definition unmodified, and aggregates only holders
with an actual agreement? That is a plan tailored to the one gap that survives.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Reciting "ten days."** The statute still says ten; **the rule has said five business
   days since the 2023 amendments**. Williams was decided in 2021 under the old window, so
   quote its ten-day language as *the law at the time* and then say what changed. Doing
   this is the difference between reproducing the case and understanding it.
2. **Thinking the filing is the sanction.** It is not. The bite is Rule 13d-1(e)(2) and
   (f)(2): **no voting and no buying** from the moment the control purpose attaches until
   ten days after the 13D is filed.
3. **Treating 13G as a category of investor.** It is a category of **intent**. A qualified
   institution that starts holding with a control purpose, or outside the ordinary course,
   flips to 13D — (e) and (g) both say "immediately."
4. **Missing that the trigger is "purpose *or effect*."** A holder can be pushed onto
   Schedule 13D by the effect of what it is doing, not only by what it meant.
5. **Saying a wolf pack must file.** Section 13(d)(3) requires acting as a group "for the
   purpose of acquiring, holding, or disposing" — **voting is not on the list**, and
   parallel conduct without agreement is not a group. This is the gap Williams names and
   the 2023 amendments did not close.
6. **Confusing universal proxy with proxy access.** **Rule 14a-19** puts a dissident's
   nominees on the **card** after the dissident runs and pays for its own campaign.
   **Proxy access** — a bylaw, not a federal mandate — puts a long-term holder's nominees
   into the company's **proxy statement**. Rule 14a-19(e) has to distinguish the two on
   the card precisely because they are different routes.
7. **Forgetting the 67% solicitation floor.** It is an affirmative obligation with a
   statement of intent required up front, not a safe harbour, and failing it means the
   nominees cannot be voted for at all.
8. **Reading 14a-19 as reaching consents.** It does not — (g)(1). In a Delaware company
   that has not opted out of § 228, that is a real alternative route.
9. **Assuming the federal rules decide the contest.** They set disclosure and card
   format. Who may nominate, by when, and on what conditions is still **state law and the
   bylaws** — which is why advance-notice provisions are where the current fights are, and
   why [[Interference with the franchise - Schnell, Blasius and the Coster standard]]
   still does the work.
10. **Ignoring the timing interaction.** A dissident must name its slate **60 days** before
    the anniversary — long before it has seen the company's card. A board that moves the
    meeting by more than thirty days resets the clock to the later of sixty days out or
    the tenth day after announcement, which is a lever worth noticing in any franchise
    problem.
