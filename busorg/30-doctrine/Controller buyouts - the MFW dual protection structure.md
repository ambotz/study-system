## Rule statement

In a controller buyout, business judgment review applies **if and only if** the
transaction is conditioned **ab initio** on both an independent, adequately
empowered special committee that meets its duty of care and the uncoerced,
informed vote of a majority of the minority.

A controller that supplies only **one** protection gets nothing more than
**burden-shifting** within entire fairness — the Kahn v. Lynch rule, undisturbed.

Entire fairness exists in the controller merger context as a **substitute** for
the DGCL's two statutory protections, disinterested board approval and stockholder
approval, because a controller's influence can undermine both. Where the
controller irrevocably and publicly disables itself as to both, the reason for the
substitution disappears and the merger "acquires the shareholder-protective
characteristics of third-party, arm's-length mergers."

## Elements

Business judgment review applies **if and only if**:

1. **Ab initio dual condition.** The controller conditions the procession of the
   transaction on the approval of both a special committee and a majority of the
   minority stockholders, from the outset and before negotiations begin.
2. **Independence.** The special committee is independent.
3. **Empowerment.** The committee is empowered to **freely select its own
   advisors** and **to say no definitively**.
4. **Care.** The committee meets its duty of care in negotiating a fair price.
5. **Informed vote.** The vote of the minority is informed.
6. **No coercion.** There is no coercion of the minority.

### Why ab initio, and why non-waivable

The condition must bind from inception so that the controller "knows that it
cannot bypass the special committee's ability to say no" and "cannot dangle a
majority-of-the-minority vote before the special committee late in the process as
a deal-closer rather than having to make a price move." A condition offered late
is spent as a **closing concession instead of a price concession**.

The vital distinction from Kahn v. Lynch, Southern Peru and Kahn v. Tremont is
that in those cases the controller never gave up its voting power through a
**non-waivable** majority-of-the-minority condition. Surrender of voting power,
not the count of procedural devices, is what the rule rewards.

### The incentive rationale — Strine's teenager

A controller who knew that one protection bought a burden shift, and did not know
whether a second bought anything, faced the incentives of a teenager told she may
go to the movies if she finishes her math **or** her English homework. She does
one assignment. The "or" structure therefore under-produced the protection the
minority needed most, and the "both" rule exists to make the second assignment
worth doing.

The "or" structure satisfies only one of the DGCL's two approval requirements
through impartial decision-makers. The "both" structure replicates the arm's-length
merger process by supplying **two independent approvals** that "serve independent
integrity-enforcing functions."

## Standard of review / burden

| Protections used | Standard | Burden |
|---|---|---|
| Neither | Entire fairness | Controller proves fair dealing and fair price |
| One only | Entire fairness | **Shifts to the plaintiff** |
| Both, ab initio, all six elements met | **Business judgment** | Plaintiff, on business judgment terms |

### Pretrial gates

- **Pleading.** A plaintiff who pleads a reasonably conceivable set of facts
  showing that **any one** element did not exist states a claim and proceeds to
  discovery.
- **Summary judgment.** If triable issues remain after discovery about whether
  either protection was established, or whether an established protection was
  effective, the case goes to trial under **entire fairness**.
- The structure explains why MFW compliance is litigated on the facts far more
  often than the clean six-element rule suggests.

### Why the structure is thought to be a fair substitute

Both the dual protection structure and entire fairness converge on **price**.
Entire fairness comprises fair dealing and fair price, and in a non-fraudulent
transaction "price may be the preponderant consideration." The dual structure
forces two price-related pretrial determinations: a fair price achieved by an
empowered, careful, independent committee, and a fully informed, uncoerced
majority-of-the-minority vote in favour of that price.

## Exceptions

- **SB 21 largely supersedes MFW.** Under 8 Del. C. § 144(b) an ordinary
  controller transaction needs only **one** of three routes: a disinterested
  committee empowered to negotiate and to reject, an informed and uncoerced
  disinterested stockholder vote, or fairness. The two-protection requirement
  survives only for **going private** transactions under § 144(c). See
  [[Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)]].
- **The statutory timing is looser.** Section 144(b)(2) requires the vote
  condition to be in the deal's terms "as in effect at the time it is submitted to
  stockholders" — not ab initio.
- **MFW was extended beyond squeeze-outs.** Southeastern Pennsylvania
  Transportation Authority v. Volgenau applied the framework where a controller
  sells the company to a **third party**, on the reasoning that a seller-controller's
  interest is generally aligned with the minority's.
- **Corwin does not reach a controller deal.**
  [[Cleansing by stockholder vote - Corwin and the fully informed vote]] operates
  only where entire fairness does **not** apply, so MFW and § 144 are the only
  routes back when a controller stands on both sides.
- **Definitional gateway.** If the blockholder is not a controller at all, none of
  this applies. See
  [[Controlling stockholder transactions - the self-dealing trigger]].

## Leading case

[[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide]]. Perelman's MacAndrews & Forbes held roughly 43% of M&F
Worldwide and offered in 2011 to buy the rest, stating in the opening offer letter
that the deal would proceed only with both a special committee's approval and a
non-waivable majority-of-the-minority vote, and that no tender offer would follow
if the committee said no. An independent committee retained its own counsel and
banker, negotiated the price up, and the minority approved. Summary judgment for
the defendants, affirmed.

The appellants' objections and the court's answers are worth carrying. On the
claim that independent directors may be inept or timid: such directors "are likely
to be exceptional," and Delaware's jurisprudence does not embrace so sceptical a
view. On the claim that arbitrageurs approve any premium: that is "an editorial
about the motives of investors" and does not show the vote was involuntary. The
appellants also conceded below that the dual structure "is the optimal one for
minority shareholders," which is the concession the incentive rationale rests on.

## Best counter-case

[[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 2)]]. Musk's board conditioned the SolarCity
acquisition on a majority-of-the-disinterested vote but formed **no special
committee**. Under MFW one protection cannot restore business judgment review, so
entire fairness governed, and Musk had to win a full trial on price.

The distinguishing line: **MFW is all-or-nothing on the structure, and the cost of
missing one element is a trial.** Slights said as much — the defendants "likely
could have avoided this expensive and time-consuming litigation had they just
adopted more objectively evident procedural protections... let this be a parable of
unnecessary peril, despite the outcome."

The same facts under § 144(b) come out differently: the disinterested vote alone
now suffices for an ordinary controller transaction, and there would have been no
trial. That contrast is the cleanest way to show what SB 21 changed.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Reciting the two-prong requirement post-SB 21.** Classify the deal first.
   Going private takes § 144(c) and both protections; anything else takes § 144(b)
   and one of three routes.
2. **Dropping element (iii).** The committee must be able to select its own
   advisors **and to say no definitively**. Negotiating authority alone is not
   enough, and it is the element Tesla 2 turns on.
3. **Confusing burden-shifting with standard-shifting.** One protection shifts the
   burden inside entire fairness. Both change the standard to business judgment.
4. **Ignoring ab initio.** A majority-of-the-minority condition added late buys
   nothing under MFW. Say why: it becomes a deal-closer rather than a constraint on
   price.
5. **Applying Corwin to a controller buyout.** Corwin is expressly limited to
   transactions not subject to entire fairness. Order the analysis: controller
   first, then MFW or § 144; no controller, then Corwin.
6. **Treating the six elements as a balancing test.** The holding is "if and only
   if," and a conceivable pleading that any single element failed defeats dismissal.
7. **Forgetting that an effective protection can still fail.** A triable issue about
   whether an established protection was **effective** sends the case to an entire
   fairness trial, not merely to a burden shift.
