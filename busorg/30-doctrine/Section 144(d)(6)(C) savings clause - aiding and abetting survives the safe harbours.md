## Rule statement

SB 21 built process safe harbours into § 144 that cleanse an interested-director, controller, or controller-buyout transaction and restore business-judgment deference. Subsection (d)(6) is the limit on what that cleansing buys. It provides that "**nothing in subsections (a), (b), or (c) of this section shall**" do three things, and the third is the one assigned here: cleansing does not "**limit or eliminate the right of any person to seek relief on the grounds that a stockholder or other person knowingly aided and abetted a breach of fiduciary duty by one or more of the directors of the corporation**."

The practical holding is that a validly cleansed transaction is **not** immunity. The safe harbour answers whether the directors and the controller are liable; it says nothing about a third party who knowingly participated. An [[Aiding and abetting a fiduciary breach - knowing participation|aiding-and-abetting]] claim against an advisor or a buyer survives a clean § 144 process, and is litigated on its own terms.

Read the operative clause's last six words closely: the claim it preserves is one predicated on a breach "**by one or more of the directors**." The savings clause is written around **director** breaches, not controller breaches — the textual seam the [[Aiding and abetting a fiduciary breach - knowing participation|knowing-participation]] module flags.

## Elements

The clause operates where:

1. A transaction has been (or could be) **cleansed** under § 144(a), (b), or (c) — the clause only qualifies those three safe harbours;
2. the relief sought is against **a stockholder or other person** — a non-fiduciary third party, not the cleansed directors or controller;
3. the theory is **knowing aiding and abetting**, carrying its full common-law scienter burden (see [[Aiding and abetting a fiduciary breach - knowing participation]]); and
4. the predicate breach is one **by one or more directors** of the corporation.

**What falls outside.** A claim that the *directors themselves* breached, once the transaction is cleansed, is foreclosed by the safe harbour — that is what cleansing does. The clause does not reopen director liability; it keeps a separate door open for the third party. A claim predicated solely on a *controller's* breach, with no director breach to abet, sits in the seam the clause's text does not plainly cover.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| What cleansing under (a)/(b)/(c) restores | Business-judgment deference to the transaction as against the fiduciaries |
| What (d)(6)(c) withholds | Any defensive effect of that cleansing against a third-party aiding-and-abetting claim |
| Burden on the preserved claim | Unchanged — plaintiff proves every [[Aiding and abetting a fiduciary breach - knowing participation|Malpiede element]], scienter included |
| Predicate breach required | A director breach; a cleansed transaction still supplies the predicate the same way an exculpated one does |
| What the clause is | A savings clause, not a cause of action — it preserves a claim that lives in the common law |

## Exceptions

- **The sibling savings clauses, § 144(d)(6)(a) and (b).** The same "nothing shall" sentence preserves two further things. **(a)** keeps the right to seek **equitable relief** where a transaction was not approved in compliance with the chapter, the charter or bylaws, or an applicable governmental order — the process-failure escape from the safe harbour. **(b)** keeps **judicial review for injunctive relief** of "provisions or devices designed or intended to deter, delay, or preclude a change of control" — i.e. the safe harbour does not insulate a poison pill or similar device from [[The poison pill - statutory authority and Unocal limits|Unocal]]/[[Takeover defences - Unocal enhanced scrutiny|enhanced-scrutiny]] review. Memorise the trio: equitable relief for process failure, injunctive review of control devices, and aiding-and-abetting against third parties all survive cleansing.
- **The controller's own damages shield is elsewhere.** § 144(d)(5) separately caps a controller's monetary liability (loyalty, bad faith, improper benefit only). (d)(6) is about what the *safe harbours* do not foreclose, not about the controller's liability ceiling — do not conflate the two.

## Leading case

**None construes § 144(d)(6)(c).** The provision is a 2025 SB 21 enactment and has no Delaware decision interpreting it as of the syllabus. The doctrine it preserves has a leading case — [[RBC Capital Markets v. Jervis (2015)|RBC Capital Markets v. Jervis]], where the advisor paid for knowing participation while the exculpated directors did not — and the clause's drafters wrote (d)(6)(c) to confirm that the RBC/[[In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation (2024)|Mindbody]] line is untouched by the new safe harbours. Treat the clause as a legislative codification of that result, not as new liability.

## Best counter-case

[[In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation (2024)|In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation]] marks where the preserved claim still fails. The distinguishing line: **(d)(6)(c) keeps the courthouse door open, but the plaintiff must still prove knowing participation in a director breach — a clean process does not lower that bar, and passive awareness by an arm's-length buyer clears it no more after SB 21 than before.** The clause removes a statutory defence; it does not supply the scienter the common law demands.

## Common trap

1. **Reading the SB 21 safe harbour as immunity.** Cleansing restores deference to the transaction against the fiduciaries; (d)(6) expressly leaves three claims standing, aiding-and-abetting among them.
2. **Thinking cleansing kills the predicate breach.** As with a § 102(b)(7) exculpated care breach, a cleansed transaction still furnishes the director breach an aider-and-abettor is charged with participating in.
3. **Missing the "by one or more of the directors" limit.** The clause is drafted around director breaches; a theory resting only on a controller's breach may not fit its text.
4. **Confusing (d)(6) with (d)(5).** (d)(5) is the controller's damages ceiling; (d)(6) is the list of claims the safe harbours do not foreclose.
5. **Forgetting (d)(6)(b) on the pill.** A control-deterring device is still subject to injunctive Unocal review notwithstanding any cleansing — a tempting thing to overlook in a takeover fact pattern.
