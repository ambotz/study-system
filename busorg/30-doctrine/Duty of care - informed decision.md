## Rule statement

In reviewing a board **decision**, the court presumes the directors acted on an
informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that the action was in
the corporation's best interests. A plaintiff rebuts the informedness prong by
showing the directors failed to inform themselves of all material information
reasonably available to them before acting, and that the failure was **grossly
negligent**.

Rebutting the presumption does not decide the case. It strips the protection,
and the burden shifts to the directors to establish the entire fairness of the
transaction.

## Elements

To rebut the presumption for want of information:

1. **A decision.** The rule presumes a conscious exercise of judgment. A board
   that never acted at all is an oversight problem, not this one.
2. **Material information reasonably available** that the directors did not
   obtain — measured by what was obtainable in the time actually available, not
   by what a perfect process would have produced.
3. **Gross negligence** in failing to obtain it. Not ordinary negligence.
4. In the merger context, § 251(b) independently requires informed and
   deliberate action; the board cannot discharge it by passing the question to
   the stockholders.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Presumption | Directors informed, good faith, honest belief |
| Burden to rebut | Plaintiff |
| Standard for informedness | Gross negligence |
| If rebutted | Burden shifts to directors — entire fairness |
| What is *not* reviewed | The substance or wisdom of the decision |

## Exceptions

- **§ 102(b)(7) exculpation.** A charter provision may eliminate director
  liability for **monetary damages** for a care breach. Virtually every Delaware
  corporation has one. It does **not** reach: duty of loyalty; acts not in good
  faith, intentional misconduct, or knowing violation of law; § 174; or a
  transaction from which the director derived an improper personal benefit. It
  does not touch injunctive relief.
  Consequence: a standalone care-damages claim is dismissed at the pleading
  stage (*Malpiede v. Townson*), so plaintiffs plead loyalty or bad faith
  instead (*In re Cornerstone Therapeutics*).
- **§ 141(e) reliance.** Good-faith reliance on officer reports protects
  directors, but the report must be pertinent to the matter before the board and
  must earn good-faith rather than blind reliance. An oral summary by someone
  who has not read the underlying document is not a report.
- **Reliance on counsel.** May form part of a proper exercise of business
  judgment, but sits outside § 141(e). Correct advice that something is not
  legally required is not advice that what the board did was sufficient.

## Leading case

[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] — $55 derived from an LBO feasibility run, a 20-minute
oral presentation, a two-hour meeting with no notice and no crisis, and an
unasked follow-up to a CFO who said the price was at the bottom of a range.

## Best counter-case

[[Shlensky v. Wrigley]] — the court will not review whether the decision was
*good*. Van Gorkom reviews how it was *reached*.

The line: **the rule protects substance, not process.** When a fact pattern
attacks the wisdom of a choice, it is Shlensky. When it attacks the information
behind the choice, it is Van Gorkom.

## Common trap

1. **Gross vs. ordinary negligence.** Writing "the board was negligent" gets no
   credit. The standard is gross, and saying so is half the point.
2. **Sliding from process into substance.** The instinct is to argue the price
   was too low. The court never says $55 was unfair — it says nobody on that
   board knew whether it was. Argue the information, not the number.
3. **Treating a premium as a valuation.** Market price measures a minority
   share. A premium over it says nothing about the value of the whole company.
4. **Stopping at the care breach.** With a § 102(b)(7) charter provision in the
   facts, a care claim for damages is dead on arrival. The move is to ask
   whether the facts also support loyalty or bad faith — that is what the
   exculpation clause forces, and it is where the exam points will be.
5. **Forgetting the curative question.** Van Gorkom has two halves. Later board
   action can in principle cure an uninformed decision; here it did not, because
   it repeated the same defect.
