## Rule statement

In reviewing a board **decision**, a Delaware court presumes that the directors
acted on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that the
action taken served the corporation's best interests. A plaintiff rebuts the
informedness prong by showing that the directors failed to inform themselves of
all material information reasonably available to them before acting, and that
the failure was **grossly negligent**.

Rebutting the presumption does not decide the case. Rebuttal strips the
protection and shifts the burden to the directors to establish the entire
fairness of the transaction.

## Elements

**1. A board decision.**

The business judgment rule presumes a conscious exercise of judgment, so the rule
attaches only where the board actually decided something. What counts:

- An affirmative act of the board — approving a merger, setting a price,
  declining a bid, adopting a defensive measure.
- A conscious decision **not** to act, where the board considered the question
  and chose inaction. A deliberate refusal to declare a dividend is a decision.
- A decision the board made badly, or on thin information. Poor decisions remain
  decisions; the quality of the process is what the rest of the elements test.

What does not count, and therefore routes elsewhere:

- **Board inattention** — a failure to act because the board never considered the
  question at all. No decision means no presumption to rebut, and the claim
  becomes an oversight claim under [[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]] and the good-faith line of cases rather than a duty of
  care claim under this module.
- **Officer conduct** that the board never reviewed or ratified.

The distinction matters because the two routes carry different standards and
different exculpation consequences. A care claim against a decision is
exculpable under § 102(b)(7); a bad-faith oversight claim is not.

**2. Material information reasonably available that the directors did not
obtain.**

Reasonable availability is measured against the time the directors actually had,
not against the process a perfect board would have run. In [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] the relevant information was obtainable inside the 36 hours remaining,
and the failure to ask for that information was the breach.

**3. Gross negligence in failing to obtain that information.**

Gross negligence, not ordinary negligence. In practice the standard is met by an
accumulation of failures rather than by any single omission.

**4. In the merger context, an independent § 251(b) duty.**

Section 251(b) requires directors to act in an informed and deliberate manner
before submitting a merger agreement to stockholders. Directors cannot discharge
the § 251(b) duty by passing the decision to the stockholders.

| | |
|---|---|
| Presumption | Directors informed, in good faith, honest belief |
| Burden to rebut | Plaintiff |
| Standard for informedness | Gross negligence |
| If rebutted | Burden shifts to directors — entire fairness |
| Never reviewed | The substance or wisdom of the decision |

## Exceptions

- **8 Del. C. § 102(b)(7) exculpation.** A charter provision may eliminate
  director liability for **monetary damages** arising from a breach of fiduciary
  duty. Virtually every Delaware corporation has adopted such a provision.
  Section 102(b)(7) does **not** reach: a breach of the duty of loyalty; acts or
  omissions not in good faith, involving intentional misconduct, or involving a
  knowing violation of law; liability under § 174; or a transaction from which
  the director derived an improper personal benefit. Section 102(b)(7) reaches
  damages only and leaves injunctive relief untouched.
  The practical consequence: a standalone care-damages claim is dismissed at the
  pleading stage ([[Malpiede v. Townson|Malpiede]]), so plaintiffs plead loyalty or bad
  faith instead ([[In re Cornerstone Therapeutics|Cornerstone]]).
- **8 Del. C. § 141(e) reliance.** Good-faith reliance on officer reports
  protects directors, but a report must be pertinent to the matter before the
  board and must earn good-faith rather than blind reliance. An oral summary
  delivered by someone who has not read the underlying document is not a report.
- **Reliance on counsel.** Reliance on counsel may form part of a proper exercise
  of business judgment, but sits outside § 141(e). Correct advice that the law
  does not require a particular step is not advice that what the board did
  instead was sufficient.

## Leading case

[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] — a price derived from leveraged-buyout feasibility
rather than valuation, a twenty-minute oral presentation, a two-hour meeting
called without notice and without any crisis, and an unasked follow-up to a chief
financial officer who placed the price at the bottom of a range.

## Best counter-case

[[Shlensky v. Wrigley]] — a Delaware-adjacent refusal to review whether a
decision was *good*. Van Gorkom reviews how a decision was *reached*.

The operative line: **the business judgment rule protects substance, not
process.** A fact pattern attacking the wisdom of a choice is a Shlensky problem.
A fact pattern attacking the information behind a choice is a Van Gorkom problem.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Gross versus ordinary negligence.** Writing that the board "was negligent"
   earns nothing. The standard is gross negligence, and naming the standard is
   half the available credit.
2. **Sliding from process into substance.** The instinct is to argue the price
   was too low. [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] never holds that $55 was unfair; the
   opinion holds that no director knew whether $55 was fair. Argue the
   information, not the number.
3. **Treating a premium as a valuation.** Market price measures a minority share.
   A premium over market says nothing about the value of the whole company,
   especially where the board already knows the market undervalues the company.
4. **Stopping at the care breach.** Where the facts include a § 102(b)(7) charter
   provision, a care claim for damages is dead on arrival. The next move is to
   ask whether the same facts support loyalty or bad faith, because the
   exculpation provision forces that question and the exam points live there.
5. **Forgetting the curative question.** [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] has two halves.
   Later board action can in principle cure an uninformed decision; the cure
   failed in Van Gorkom because the later action repeated the original defect.
6. **Confusing a bad decision with no decision.** A board that decided badly is
   inside this module. A board that never considered the question at all is an
   oversight problem under [[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]], where § 102(b)(7) offers no protection.
