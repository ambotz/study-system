## Rule statement

A derivative plaintiff must make presuit demand on the board or plead with particularity why
demand is futile. Futility is assessed **director by director**, against the **demand
board** — the board in place when the complaint is filed — on three questions:

1. whether the director **received a material personal benefit** from the alleged misconduct
   that is the subject of the demand;
2. whether the director **would face a substantial likelihood of liability** on any of the
   claims that are the subject of the demand; and
3. whether the director **lacks independence** from someone who received such a benefit or
   who would face such a likelihood of liability.

Demand is excused if half or more of the demand board answer yes to any of the three.

## Elements

**1. Rule 23.1's pleading standard.** The complaint must "state with particularity" any effort
to obtain the action from the entity and the reasons for not obtaining it or not making the
effort, and allege facts supporting a reasonable inference that demand would be futile.

**2. Prong one — material personal benefit.** A benefit from the misconduct itself, not from
being a director generally.

**3. Prong two — substantial likelihood of liability.** **Exculpated claims do not count.**
Because § 102(b)(7) eliminates monetary liability for care breaches, a care claim at a company
with an exculpatory charter provision cannot expose directors to a substantial likelihood of
liability and therefore cannot excuse demand. This is why plaintiffs press loyalty, bad faith
and oversight theories — see
[[Oversight liability - Caremark and good faith]] and
[[Charter exculpation of director liability]].

**4. Prong three — independence.** The assigned cases mark both ends of the line:

- **Enough** ([[Marchand v. Barnhill]]): a director hired by the defendant's father as his
  administrative assistant, promoted to CFO, given a board seat, and honoured by a $450,000
  family-led campaign naming a building after him — "very warm and thick personal ties of
  respect, loyalty, and affection." And independence is judged **against the decision at
  issue**: deciding whether to sue differs in kind from voting against someone.
- **Not enough** ([[United Food (Tri-State) Pension Fund v. Zuckerberg]]): collegiality,
  gratitude, a policy waiver with nothing expected in return, and professional ties to deal
  advisors. Personal friendship alone is insufficient (*Beam*).

**The operative distinction is between a relationship that creates a debt and one that
creates warmth.**

**5. What never suffices.** That the directors "would have to sue themselves." *Aronson* calls
this "bootstrap," and accepting it "would effectively abrogate Rule 23.1 and weaken the
managerial power of directors."

**6. Why Aronson was refined.** When Aronson was decided, raising a reasonable doubt about
business judgment review **implied** care exposure. § 102(b)(7) "and other developments in
corporate law have weakened the connection between rebutting the business judgment standard
and exposing directors to a risk that would sterilize their judgment." Aronson also "proved
difficult to apply" where the board has **turned over** — it asks about the deciding board,
while Rales asks about the demand board. The refined test treats "*Rales* as the general demand
futility test" while drawing on Aronson-like principles at the director level. Prior cases
"properly applying those holdings remain good law."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Burden | Plaintiff, pleading **with particularity** |
| Board assessed | The **demand board**, at filing |
| Method | Director by director |
| Threshold | Half or more of the demand board disqualified |
| Exculpated care claims | Cannot satisfy prong two |
| Friendship alone | Insufficient |
| "They'd have to sue themselves" | Insufficient — bootstrap |

## Exceptions

- **Oversight claims are where this bites hardest.** With no interested transaction, prong one
  is unavailable and everything rests on prong two — which means pleading a substantial
  likelihood of liability on a theory Delaware calls "possibly the most difficult theory in
  corporation law upon which a plaintiff might hope to win a judgment."
- **Demand made is demand conceded.** A stockholder who makes demand concedes the board's
  independence for that purpose. Futility must be pleaded instead, not both.
- **The corporation can still take the case back** after demand is excused, by appointing a
  special litigation committee — see
  [[Special litigation committees - the Zapata two-step]].

## Leading case

[[United Food (Tri-State) Pension Fund v. Zuckerberg]] — the three-part test adopted, on facts
where a nine-member demand board included two directors who were not present for the challenged
vote at all.

## Best counter-case

[[Marchand v. Barnhill]] on prong three, where the same inquiry comes out the other way.

The distinguishing line: **Rankin owed his career and a named building to the defendant's
family; Bowles was collegial and grateful.** A relationship that would make suing feel like
betrayal of a benefactor defeats independence; one that would merely make it awkward does not.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Running Aronson's two prongs.** Superseded as the general frame. Give the three questions.
2. **Assessing the wrong board.** The **demand board** at filing, not the board that approved
   the transaction. Board turnover is one of the two reasons the test changed.
3. **Ignoring exculpation.** A care claim at a company with a § 102(b)(7) provision cannot
   excuse demand under prong two, no matter how bad the conduct looks.
4. **Arguing the directors would have to sue themselves.** Bootstrap, expressly rejected.
5. **Treating friendship as disqualifying.** *Beam* and Zuckerberg both say it is not. Look for
   dependence, debt, or something expected in return.
6. **Forgetting particularity.** Conclusory independence allegations fail even where the
   underlying relationship is real.
7. **Assuming excusal ends the fight.** It gets the plaintiff past Rule 23.1, not past a Zapata
   motion.
