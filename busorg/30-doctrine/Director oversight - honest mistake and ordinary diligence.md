## Rule statement

A director undertakes "diligent attention" to the office and "a faithful and honest
discharge of the duty which it imposes" — **not infallibility**. Two consequences
follow.

First, an **honest error of judgment**, including a misconstruction of the
corporation's own powers, does not charge a director, because a rule holding
trustees liable for good-faith mistake "would put an end to all offices of trust."

Second, directors are **not sureties** for the fidelity of the officers they
appoint. Liability for an employee's fraud requires either appointing someone known
to be unworthy, or an examination process falling below what "prudent men careful of
their own concerns" pursue in similar institutions.

## Elements

**1. No liability for honest error of judgment.**

Applies to mistakes about the scope of corporate power, on questions of doubtful
construction, made without self-dealing. The excuse survives even where loss follows
directly from the error.

**2. No suretyship for appointees.**

Liability attaches on appointment only where directors "select persons to fill
subordinate situations who are known to them to be unworthy of trust or notoriously
of bad character."

**3. The oversight standard: usual practice, diligently followed.**

The question is "whether those investigations were conducted in the usual manner
pursued in other companies by prudent men careful of their own concerns." From
*Manhattan Bank v. Lydig*: "It is not for the court to point out the mode banks are
to pursue to detect frauds; but if they take the usual and uniform method adopted,
not only by this but by other banks, they cannot be subject to a charge of
negligence."

The distinction that carries the weight:

- **What directors could have done** — often a great deal, and often easy.
- **What it was their duty to do** — the usual and uniform method.
- "There is a wide difference between this and what it was their duty to do."

**4. Independent defences that reach the same result.**

- **Stockholder acquiescence.** Stockholders who knew of a course of dealing, took
  its dividends, and re-elected the same directors are *in pari delicto* and cannot
  charge the directors for what grew out of it.
- **Causation.** Funds lost to forgery at the bank would have been equally exposed
  however they came to be there, so an unauthorised investment practice was "after
  all, but a remote" cause.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Standard of conduct | Diligent attention and honest discharge, not infallibility |
| Oversight benchmark | Usual and uniform method of similar institutions |
| Who sets the method | The industry, not the court |
| Appointment liability | Only for a known-unworthy appointee |
| Independent bars | Acquiescence; absence of causation |

## Exceptions

- **Known bad character** at the time of appointment removes the protection.
- **Fraud or wilful disregard of duty** is outside the doctrine entirely; the
  complainant in [[Scott v. Depeyster]] expressly abandoned that theory.
- **Self-dealing** is a loyalty problem and is never excused as honest error — see
  [[Agent duty of loyalty - self-dealing]].

## Leading case

[[Scott v. Depeyster]] — a secretary altered signed checks from five, seven and nine
dollars into thousands, took roughly $179,000 over three years, and passed six
semi-annual director examinations undetected. The directors were not charged,
because their examinations matched industry practice.

## Best counter-case

[[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]], which asks the
same question and answers it on a different axis.

The distinguishing line: **Scott measures oversight against what comparable
institutions actually do; Caremark measures it against whether a reporting system
exists at all and whether the directors consciously disregarded it.** The modern
move relocates oversight from **care** to **good faith**, which matters enormously
after § 102(b)(7), because an exculpatory charter provision covers a care claim and
does not cover a bad-faith one — see
[[Charter exculpation of director liability]].

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Answering with what the directors could have done.** One trip to the bank, or
   one comparison of cancelled checks to the check book, would have exposed the
   fraud, and the court says so. It is still not the test.
2. **Treating industry practice as a safe harbour in the modern law.** It was
   essentially dispositive in 1832. After Caremark and [[Marchand v. Barnhill]],
   conformity to industry practice does not answer whether a board made a good-faith
   effort to implement a reporting system.
3. **Collapsing the three grounds.** [[Scott v. Depeyster]] gives independent
   answers — honest error, acquiescence, no causation — and the causation analysis
   is the most transferable of the three.
4. **Missing that this is a care case, not a loyalty case.** That classification is
   what determines whether an exculpatory charter provision would dispose of it
   today.
