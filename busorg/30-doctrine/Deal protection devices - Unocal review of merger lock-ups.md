## Rule statement

Devices adopted to protect a signed merger are reviewed under **Unocal enhanced
scrutiny**, not the business judgment rule, **even where the merger works no
change of control** and therefore triggers no Revlon duty. Where the devices are
inextricably related, they are scrutinised **collectively as a unitary response**.

The statutory premise is that a merger becomes final only on a stockholder vote,
so Delaware law makes a merger "a shared enterprise and ownership decision."
A device that neutralises the stockholders' half of that decision is not merely
unfair; it is inconsistent with the allocation the statute makes.

## Elements

### The two-stage inquiry

**Stage one — reasonable grounds.** The directors must show they had reasonable
grounds to believe a danger to the corporation and its stockholders existed if the
merger were not consummated, satisfied by a showing of good faith and reasonable
investigation. Proof is "materially enhanced" where a majority of outside
directors or an independent committee approved.

**Stage two — proportionality, in two steps.**

1. **Draconian screen, applied first.** Are the devices preclusive **or**
   coercive? The test is **disjunctive**: either quality alone is draconian and
   impermissible, and the analysis stops.
2. **Range of reasonableness**, reached only if the devices are neither.

| Term | Definition (Unitrin, as quoted in Omnicare) |
|---|---|
| **Coercive** | Aimed at forcing upon stockholders a management-sponsored alternative to a hostile offer |
| **Preclusive** | Deprives stockholders of the right to receive all tender offers, or precludes a bidder from seeking control by fundamentally restricting proxy contests or otherwise |

### What counts as a deal protection device

Any measure or combination of measures intended to protect the consummation of a
merger. Devices may be **economic**, **structural**, or both.

- Termination fees and expense reimbursement
- No-shop and no-talk provisions, and exclusivity agreements
- Force-the-vote provisions requiring submission to stockholders notwithstanding
  a withdrawn board recommendation
- Matching rights and information rights
- Asset lock-up options
- **Stockholder voting agreements**, where they are inextricably intertwined with
  the board's defensive scheme

The voting-agreement point is the doctrinal move to know. A stockholder voting her
own shares is ordinarily beyond fiduciary review, but where the acquiror **demands
the agreements as a non-negotiable condition precedent**, the target is made a
party, and the other devices are worthless without them, the agreements take their
legal character from their function within the board's response.

### The fiduciary out

A merger contract provision purporting to require a board to act, or not act, so
as to limit the exercise of its fiduciary duties is **invalid and unenforceable**.
A sophisticated counterparty that negotiated for such a provision from a board
acting in violation of its duties acquires no vested contract right in it.

Where a cohesive group holding majority voting power is irrevocably committed to
the transaction, the board cannot abdicate to the stockholders, because the
outcome of the vote is already a foregone conclusion. In that configuration an
**effective fiduciary out is required**.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Standard | Unocal enhanced scrutiny |
| Burden | On the **directors**, at both stages |
| Trigger | Adoption of devices protecting a merger — no change of control required |
| Unit of analysis | The **overall response**, scrutinised collectively where the measures are inextricably related |
| Screen | Preclusive **or** coercive → draconian → impermissible, with no proportionality inquiry |
| Independent invalidity | A term that disables the board from discharging its continuing fiduciary duties |
| Typical remedy | Pre-closing injunction; the devices are held unenforceable |

## Exceptions

- **Revlon is not triggered by deal protections alone.** "The adoption of
  structural safety devices alone does not trigger Revlon" — such devices are
  subject to Unocal instead ([[Paramount v. Time]], quoted in Omnicare). Where the
  merger **is** a sale of control, see
  [[Sale of control - Revlon and the duty to maximise price]].
- **Ordinary deal protections survive routinely.** A reasonable termination fee, a
  no-shop with a fiduciary out, and matching rights are standard and proportionate.
  Omnicare condemns a **complete** lock-up, not deal protection as a category.
- **Force-the-vote is statutorily authorised.** Section 251(c) at the time of
  Omnicare, and 8 Del. C. § 146 since 2018, permit a board to submit a merger to
  stockholders notwithstanding a later withdrawal of its recommendation. The
  provision is lawful; the combination in Omnicare was not.
- **Post-closing, a fully informed and uncoerced vote cleanses.** See
  [[Cleansing by stockholder vote - Corwin and the fully informed vote]]. Omnicare
  matters precisely because a predetermined vote is not an uncoerced one.
- **§ 144(d)(6)(b) preserves injunctive review post-SB 21** of devices designed to
  deter, delay or preclude a change of control, so the controller safe harbours do
  not immunise a lock-up.

## Leading case

[[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]]. Three devices operated together: a § 251(c)
force-the-vote provision, irrevocable and specifically enforceable voting
agreements from the two directors who between them held majority voting power, and
the omission of any effective fiduciary out. The combination guaranteed **ab
initio** that the merger would be approved.

The devices were **coercive**, because the 80% public float that favoured
Omnicare's superior bid was forced to accept the Genesis merger as a fait
accompli, and **preclusive**, because they made any competing proposal
"mathematically impossible" and "realistically unattainable" no matter how
superior. Being draconian, they never reached the range-of-reasonableness inquiry.

The proof that the devices had emptied the franchise is that the board **withdrew
its recommendation** and told stockholders to reject the merger, and the devices
operated anyway.

## Best counter-case

[[Paramount v. Time]], and the dissents in Omnicare itself.

Time supplies the doctrinal counterweight: structural safety devices attached to a
strategic stock-for-stock merger get Unocal review and routinely pass it, because
protecting a value-creating transaction from an opportunistic topping bid is a
legitimate objective proportionately served.

The distinguishing line: **each Omnicare device is individually lawful, and the
holding rests entirely on the combination producing a mathematically certain
outcome.** Absent a majority bloc irrevocably committed, the same three terms are
ordinary deal protection.

Veasey, C.J., dissenting with Steele, J., put the practitioner's objection: a
lock-up lets the parties "exchange certainties," certainty itself has value, and
the lock-up here was "indisputably a sine qua non to any deal with Genesis," so a
bright-line rule returns an insolvent company's stockholders to zero rather than
$24 million. He hoped the holding would be "interpreted narrowly and... seen as
sui generis," and the Delaware bar has largely read it that way.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Reaching for Revlon first.** Ask whether there is a change of control only to
   decide whether Revlon adds anything. Deal protections get Unocal review either
   way, which is why Omnicare never decides the Revlon question.
2. **Analysing the devices one at a time.** Where the measures are inextricably
   related they are scrutinised collectively. Each Omnicare device passes in
   isolation and the combination fails.
3. **Running proportionality on a draconian measure.** Preclusive or coercive ends
   the inquiry. Reciting the range of reasonableness after finding preclusion
   inverts the opinion's structure.
4. **Treating voting agreements as private stockholder conduct.** They are part of
   the board's response where the acquiror demanded them as a condition precedent,
   the target is a party, and the other devices depend on them.
5. **Stating that a fiduciary out is always required.** The holding is conditioned
   on a cohesive majority bloc irrevocably committed. Say that condition out loud
   before stating the requirement.
6. **Misreading coercion as pressure on voters.** The NCS minority was not
   compelled to vote yes; the outcome was fixed whatever they did. Coercion here
   means the emptying of the franchise.
7. **Citing § 251(c) as current law for force-the-vote.** Cite it as the opinion
   does, then note that the authority now sits in § 146.
