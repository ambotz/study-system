## Rule statement

§ 141(h) authorises the board "to fix the compensation of directors" unless restricted
by the charter or bylaws. Exercising that authority is **self-interested**, so the
awards "lie outside the business judgment rule's presumptive protection" and, when
properly challenged, entire fairness applies.

Stockholder ratification can restore deference — but **only for what the stockholders
actually approved**. Approval of a plan's general parameters does not approve the
discretionary awards made under it.

## Elements

**1. Legal authority to act.** § 141(h), plus whatever the stockholder-approved plan
confers. Authority is rarely disputed; it is the first half of what
[[In re Investors Bancorp]] calls the **"twice-tested"** inquiry.

**2. The self-interest that strips the presumption.** A board fixing its own
compensation is "deciding how much they should reward themselves for board service."
Absent another factor, entire fairness.

**3. Ratification — available in three settings, effective in two.**

| Setting | Ratification available? |
|---|---|
| Stockholders approved the **specific awards** | **Yes** — they knew what they were approving |
| **Self-executing** plan: awards over time on fixed criteria, amounts and terms already approved | **Yes** |
| **Discretionary** plan: stockholders approve general parameters, directors later allocate | **No**, where inequitable exercise is properly alleged |

**4. What ratification requires when it does apply.** Approval by a **fully informed,
uncoerced, and disinterested majority** of stockholders. The same formula now appears
in statutory form in post-SB 21 § 144(a)(2) — see
[[Interested director and officer transactions - 144(a)]].

**5. The equitable second test.** Legal authority does not exhaust the inquiry.
Stockholders "entrust directors with broad legal authority precisely because they know
that authority must be exercised consistently with equitable principles of fiduciary
duty," relying on "the policing of equity." Therefore **"inequitable action does not
become permissible simply because it is legally possible."**

**What does *not* save a discretionary plan**

- **"Meaningful limits."** Caps and per-category ceilings were exactly what won
  dismissal in the Court of Chancery in [[In re Investors Bancorp]], and the Supreme
  Court reversed. The test is the **specificity of what was submitted**, not whether
  the discretion was bounded.
- **A large approval margin.** 96.25% of voting shares approved the Investors Bancorp
  plan. Irrelevant, because the vote reached parameters and not awards.
- **Retention of the benefit.** Compare [[Bayer v. Beran]], where acceptance and
  retention of benefits with full knowledge ratified informal board action. That works
  where the stockholders knew what the action was; it does not manufacture approval of
  "specific bargains not yet proposed."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Default for self-compensation | Entire fairness; burden on the directors |
| Effective ratification | Restores business judgment review |
| Ratification of a discretionary award | Unavailable once inequitable exercise is properly alleged |
| Consequence of unavailability | Directors must prove the awards entirely fair — a burden, not a verdict |
| Posture where this usually bites | Motion to dismiss; the defence is what would have produced dismissal |

## Exceptions

- **Ratification cures voidable acts, not everything.** From *Kerbs*: stockholder
  ratification of voidable director acts "is effective for all purposes unless the
  action of the directors constituted a **gift of corporate assets** to themselves or
  was **ultra vires, illegal, or fraudulent**."
- **Disclosure conditions the defence.** Also from *Kerbs*: effectiveness "depends upon
  the type of notice sent to the stockholder and the explanation to them of the plan
  itself."
- **Charter or bylaw restriction.** § 141(h)'s grant is a default and can be
  constrained by the certificate or bylaws — the same instrument hierarchy as
  [[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]].

## Leading case

[[In re Investors Bancorp]] — a plan capping non-employee directors at 30% of
available shares, approved by 96.25% of voting shares, under which the board then
awarded itself 7.8 million shares worth $51.6 million, taking each non-employee
director from roughly $100,000–$200,000 to more than $2.1 million.

## Best counter-case

[[Bayer v. Beran]], where a conflicted transaction survived.

The distinguishing line: **the fiduciary in Bayer defended the transaction on its
merits and met the burden; the fiduciaries in Investors Bancorp tried to avoid the
burden with a vote that had not reached their awards.** A fact pattern where the
conflicted party can show market terms, arm's-length process and full corporate
benefit is a Bayer problem and can be won. A fact pattern where the defence rests on a
prior approval is a ratification problem, and the question is only ever what that
approval actually covered.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Reciting "meaningful limits" as the test.** It is the test that **lost**. The plan
   had meaningful limits; Chancery dismissed on them; the Supreme Court reversed.
2. **Leaning on the size of the vote.** 96.25% proves nothing about awards the proxy
   said "have not been determined at this time."
3. **Confusing legal authority with equitable permission.** § 141(h) and the plan gave
   the directors every right to make the awards. That is the first test only.
4. **Reading reversal as liability.** The holding is that the plaintiffs pleaded enough
   and the defence is unavailable at the pleading stage. The directors may still prove
   entire fairness.
5. **Missing the self-executing escape.** A plan with fixed criteria and approved
   amounts and terms keeps the defence. So does submitting the specific awards. Being
   able to name both routes is the most likely follow-up question.
6. **Forgetting which stockholders count.** Ratification requires a fully informed,
   uncoerced, **disinterested** majority — and post-SB 21 § 144(a)(2) makes the
   disinterested-and-votes-cast measure statutory.
