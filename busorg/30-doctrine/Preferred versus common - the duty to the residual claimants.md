## Rule statement

Directors "must strive in good faith and on an informed basis to **maximize the value of the
corporation for the benefit of its residual claimants**, the ultimate beneficiaries of the firm's
value, **not for the benefit of its contractual claimants**."

Consequently: "it is the duty of directors to pursue the best interests of the corporation and its
**common stockholders**, **if that can be done faithfully with the contractual promises owed to the
preferred**," and "generally it will be the duty of the board, where **discretionary judgment** is
to be exercised, to prefer the interests of the common stock... to the interests created by the
special rights, preferences, etc. of preferred stock."

**The principle is not about preferred stock.** "This principle is not unique to preferred stock;
it applies equally to **other holders of contract rights** against the corporation."

## Elements

### The two-track analysis

| The preferred holder's claim | How it is enforced |
|---|---|
| Rights **stated in the certificate of designations** — liquidation preference, cumulative dividend, conversion ratio, consent rights | **Contract.** The board must honour them faithfully |
| Anything requiring the board's **discretionary judgment** — whether to sell, when to sell, how to allocate consideration | **Fiduciary.** Owed to the **common** as residual claimants |

Run every preferred/common problem through that split first. The preferred get their document; they
do not get the board's discretion.

### Why the interests diverge — the economics

"[T]he fact that preferred and common 'may have incentives to pursue different exit strategies is
not obvious'," since both are equity. The divergence comes from the **special rights**.

- "Because of the preferred shareholders' liquidation preferences, they sometimes **gain less from
  increases in firm value than they lose from decreases** in firm value. This effect may cause a
  board dominated by preferred shareholders to choose **lower-risk, lower-value** investment
  strategies over higher-risk, higher-value investment strategies."
- "[P]referred dominated boards may favor immediate '**liquidity events**' (such as dissolution or
  sale of the business) **even if operating the firm as a stand-alone going concern would generate
  more value** for shareholders."
- "[B]ecause VCs in... sales often exit as preferred shareholders with liquidation preferences that
  must be paid in full before common shareholders receive any payout, common shareholders may receive
  little (if any) payout. At the same time, **the sale eliminates any 'option value' (upside
  potential) of the common stock**."

### The middle case

The distorting effects "are **most likely to arise when, as is often the case, the firm is neither a
complete failure nor a stunning success**."

- **Stunning success** — an IPO or a rich sale — everybody wins, no conflict.
- **Complete failure** — liquidation is the obvious call, the preference absorbs what is left,
  nobody disputes it.
- **In between** — a sale at a mediocre price guarantees the preferred a return and wipes out the
  common's option. This is where the litigation lives.

### The VC business model reinforces it

"Before venture capitalists invest, they **plan for exit**." Exit lets them reallocate funds, lets
their own investors evaluate them, and disciplines entrepreneurs. "The timing and form of exit are
critical because VCs seek very high rates of return, usually a **ten-fold return of capital over a
five-year period**," and they "will sometimes **liquidate an otherwise viable firm**, if its
expected returns are not what they (or their investors) expected."

Three exits, ranked: an IPO is "the gold standard"; a trade sale is "a second-best solution"; a
write-off is "the least attractive."

### The dual fiduciary problem

From Weinberger: "[t]here is no dilution of [fiduciary] obligation where one holds **dual or
multiple directorships**," and "[t]here is **no 'safe harbor'** for such divided loyalties."

"If the interests of the beneficiaries to whom the dual fiduciary owes duties are **aligned**, then
there is no conflict... But if the interests of the beneficiaries **diverge**, the fiduciary faces an
inherent conflict of interest."

### Interest and independence on a VC-backed board

- **Interest**: a personal financial benefit "not equally shared by the stockholders," "of a
  sufficiently material importance, **in the context of the director's economic circumstances**, as
  to have made it improbable that the director could perform her fiduciary duties... without being
  influenced by her overriding personal interest."
- **Post-transaction board membership "standing alone, would not be sufficient."**
- **"Independent" directors are often not.** Scholars argue they "are often not truly independent of
  the VCs," being "chosen by the VCs, who tend to have much larger professional networks," and able
  to "expect to be recommended for other board seats or even invited to join the VC fund as a
  'venture partner'." But a plaintiff "could not rely on **general characterizations of the VC
  ecosystem**" and must prove it case by case — which Trados does, finding a "sense of
  '**owingness**'."

### Management incentive plans — where to look

A plan rewarding management on a sale is ordinary in VC portfolio companies. Look for the features
that realign management **away from** the common:

- A **cutback feature** reducing the MIP payout by any consideration received as an equity holder —
  it "ensured that management would focus **exclusively** on proceeds received through the MIP."
- **No allocation rule for incremental dollars above the preference**, so the common fund the plan
  disproportionately.
- **Who designed, presented and approved it** — in Trados, its three largest recipients.
- **Mid-process adjustments to secure votes** — Hummel's share raised from 12% to 14% when he
  wavered, after which he was "obviously a lock."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Default | Business judgment rule |
| Trigger for entire fairness | No **disinterested and independent board majority** approved the decision, determined **director by director** |
| Initial burden | On the **plaintiff**, to rebut an element of the business judgment rule |
| Burden after that | Shifts to **defendants**, to prove entire fairness |
| Entire fairness | Fair dealing **and** fair price, examined "as a whole since the question is one of entire fairness"; "perfection is not possible, or expected" |
| Test of fairness | Whether the stockholder "shall receive the **substantial equivalent in value of what he had before**" |
| Unfair process alone | **Not a separate breach** where price is fair — "an unfair process **can** infect the price," but need not |
| Cleansing devices | Special committee aligned with the common; independent valuation; condition on a **majority-of-the-disinterested-common** vote |

Note what the cleansing devices do. Failing to use them is not itself a breach; it "resulted in
their being forced to **prove at trial** that the Merger was entirely fair."

## Exceptions

- **Preferred holders may vote their shares in their own interest.** "Stockholders in Delaware
  corporations have a right to control and vote their shares in their own interest... limited only by
  any fiduciary duty owed to other stockholders." The preferred voting for the deal is not evidence of
  unfair dealing.
- **Failure to condition on a disinterested common vote is not itself unfair** — "it simply deprives
  the defendants of otherwise helpful **affirmative evidence** of fairness."
- **The rule reverses on insolvency.** Once creditors become the primary residual claimants, directors
  loyal to the equity face the mirror conflict — the "**reverse-Trados theory**" of
  [[Quadrant Structured Products v. Vertin]]. And the limit is the same in both directions: the theory
  reaches transactions conferring **direct and specific benefits**, not decisions affecting the firm as
  a whole. See [[Fiduciary duties on insolvency - Gheewalla standing and the Quadrant line]].
- **Contractual workarounds are available at the stockholder level.** A drag-along with a covenant not
  to sue is not facially invalid, within limits. See
  [[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]].
- **Or choose a different entity.** An LLC operating agreement can waive fiduciary duties outright, and
  then the implied covenant is all that remains. See
  [[LLCs - freedom of contract, default duties and the implied covenant]] and [[Miller v. HCP & Co]].

## Leading case

[[In re Trados]]. Six of seven directors were interested or conflicted — two by MIP payments and
post-deal employment, three as dual fiduciaries for preferred-holding VC funds, one through a "sense
of 'owingness'" to Sequoia plus a material payment — so entire fairness applied.

**Fair dealing weighed decisively against the defendants.** There was "no contemporaneous evidence
suggesting that the directors set out to deal with the common stockholders in a procedurally fair
manner." The sale was initiated by directors who wanted an exit; the MIP "**converted the management
team from holders of equity interests aligned with the common stock to claimants whose return profile
and incentives closely resembled those of the preferred**"; the board never discussed the common;
there was no special committee, no fairness opinion, and no disinterested common vote. "**Conflict
blindness and its lesser cousin, conflict denial, have long afflicted the financially
sophisticated.**"

**And the defendants won**, because the common stock had no value. On $60 million the preference was
$57.9 million, the MIP took $7.8 million, and the common received nothing — but Trados "did not have a
realistic chance of generating a sufficient return to **escape the gravitational pull of the large
liquidation preference and cumulative dividend**." Receiving nothing was "the substantial equivalent
in value of what they had before."

**The arithmetic to carry**: without the MIP the common would have had $2.1 million; with it, the
common funded **100%** of their ex-MIP proceeds and the preferred **10%**; break-even for the common
was **$66.5 million**; and even at $70 million the common keep 25% of what they would otherwise
receive while "[t]he preferred stockholders would not lose a dime."

## Best counter-case

[[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]] is the contractual answer to Trados, and the pair defines the
space.

The distinguishing line: **Trados denies preferred holders the board's discretion; Rich lets them buy
a substitute for it at the stockholder level.** Loyalty "cannot be eliminated without destroying [the]
fiduciary character" of the relationship, but it can be **oriented** by a purpose clause, and
fiduciaries can be **authorised in advance** to take specific actions that would otherwise breach.
And "through a private agreement, stockholders can agree to more constraints on their ability to
exercise stockholder-level rights than corporate planners can impose through the charter or bylaws."

What the covenant could **not** do is the part that decides the motion. "Delaware law generally
prohibits contractual provisions that purport to exculpate a party for tort liability resulting from
**intentional or reckless harm**," and while corporate law extends exculpation to recklessness treated
as gross negligence, nothing reaches intent. So the covenant was valid, reasonable under the Manti
factors, and still did not get the defendants dismissed.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Saying the board owes duties to the preferred.** It owes them **contract**. The fiduciary duty
   runs to the residual claimants, and where discretion is exercised the board prefers the common —
   subject to honouring the preferred's stated rights.
2. **Dropping the qualifier.** The duty is to prefer the common "**if that can be done faithfully with
   the contractual promises owed to the preferred**." A board cannot breach the certificate of
   designations to help the common.
3. **Assuming a conflict from the capital structure.** Preferred and common are both equity, and in a
   stunning success or a total failure their interests align. Locate the **middle case** before
   asserting divergence.
4. **Treating dual-fiduciary status as automatic disqualification.** It creates a conflict only where
   the beneficiaries' interests actually diverge, and that must be proved on the facts.
5. **Relying on the VC-ecosystem critique instead of evidence.** Trados accepts the scholarship and
   insists the plaintiff prove owingness for **this** director.
6. **Confusing standard of review with breach.** A conflicted board majority makes entire fairness the
   lens. It is not a finding that anyone breached — the Trados defendants won.
7. **Forgetting that price can carry the case.** Entire fairness is unitary. Unfair process "can infect
   the price," but where the common were worthless, receiving nothing is the substantial equivalent of
   what they had.
8. **Calling the preferred's vote unfair dealing.** Stockholders may vote their own interest, limited
   only by duties owed to other stockholders.
9. **Missing the MIP's cutback feature.** The plan is ordinary; the cutback is what stripped management
   of any reason to fight for a price above the preference.
