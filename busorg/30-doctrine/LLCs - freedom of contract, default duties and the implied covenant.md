## Rule statement

"[I]t is the policy of [the LLC Act] to give the **maximum effect to the principle of freedom of
contract** and to the enforceability of limited liability company agreements" (§ 18-1101(b)).

Three consequences, in order:

1. **The operating agreement is the constitution.** The Act "provide[s] members with broad discretion
   in drafting the Agreement and... furnish[es] **default provisions when the members' agreement is
   silent**."
2. **Default fiduciary duties exist**, since the 2013 amendment to § 18-1104 — "the rules of law and
   equity, **including the rules of law and equity relating to fiduciary duties** and the law merchant,
   shall govern" — and **may be waived**, but only **explicitly**.
3. **The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing cannot be waived** (§ 18-1101(c)) — and where
   the agreement specifies the scope of a discretion, there is **no gap** for the covenant to fill, so
   the mandatory duty often has nothing to do.

## Elements

### The mandatory / default line

"[O]nly where the agreement is **inconsistent with mandatory statutory provisions** will the members'
agreement be invalidated. Such statutory provisions are likely to be those intended to **protect third
parties**, not necessarily the contracting members."

**That is the test.** Ask whom the provision protects. Provisions structuring the members' own
relations are defaults; provisions safeguarding outsiders bind.

What the members may contract around, on the authority of [[Elf Atochem North America v. Jaffari]]:

- The **Delaware forum**, including for **derivative** claims otherwise permitted by § 18-1001.
- The **Chancery jurisdiction** conferred by §§ 18-110(a) and 18-111. "[F]or the purpose of designating
  a more convenient forum, we find no reason why the members cannot alter the default jurisdictional
  provisions of the statute."
- **Fiduciary duties** themselves, under § 18-1101(c).

What they may not:

- The **implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing**. An LLC agreement "may not eliminate" it, and
  "the implied covenant **inheres in every contract** governed by Delaware law."

### The LLC is bound by an agreement it never signed

Section 18-101(7) defines a limited liability company agreement as "**any agreement, written or oral, of
the member or members** as to the affairs of a limited liability company and the conduct of its
business." So the members' execution binds the entity.

The reason given is conceptual and worth quoting: "**It is the members who are the real parties in
interest. The LLC is simply their joint business vehicle.**"

### Default fiduciary duties — how the question was settled

- Until 2013 the question was open. Chancery held for years that the defaults matched corporate duties;
  the Supreme Court had never said.
- **Gatz Properties (2012)** rebuked the Chancellor for reaching it sua sponte, declaring the
  "statutory pronouncements must be regarded as **dictum without any precedential value**," and
  reminding trial judges that writing opinions "is **not a license to use those opinions as a platform
  from which to propagate their individual world views** on issues not presented."
- **The General Assembly answered in 2013** by adding the bolded words to § 18-1104, codifying the
  Chancellor's view.
- **Members remain free to contract out**, and "[t]he issue remains open in several other
  jurisdictions." See [[Note on LLC default fiduciary duties]].

### Waiver must be explicit

"[U]nder our law an LLC itself implies default fiduciary duties; **to eliminate such duties, the members
must explicitly waive them**."

### The implied covenant — the analysis, in order

**Step one: is there a gap?**

- "[T]he implied covenant applies **only if the contract is silent** as to the subject at issue."
- "If the contract directly addresses the matter at hand, '[e]xisting contract terms control... such
  that implied good faith cannot be used to **circumvent the parties' bargain**.'"

**Step two, only if silent: what would the parties have agreed to?**

- "[W]hether it is clear from what was expressly agreed upon that the parties who negotiated the express
  terms of the contract would have agreed to proscribe the act later complained of... **had they thought
  to negotiate with respect to that matter**."
- Not from the court's "own notions of justice or fairness," but from what the parties "would have agreed
  to **had they considered the issue in their original bargaining positions at the time of
  contracting**."
- The covenant "operates only in that **narrow band of cases** where the contract as a whole speaks
  sufficiently to suggest an obligation and point to a result, but does not speak directly enough to
  provide an explicit answer."

**Definitions that narrow it further:**

- Applying the covenant is "a '**cautious enterprise**'," and the doctrine is "**rarely invoked
  successfully**."
- It applies only where a party "acted **arbitrarily or unreasonably**, thereby frustrating the fruits of
  the bargain that the asserting party reasonably expected."
- Implied terms must address gaps "that the asserting party pleads **neither party anticipated**."
- **Good faith** means "faithfulness to the **scope, purpose, and terms** of the parties' contract."
- **Fair dealing** "does not imply equitable behavior. The term 'fair' is **something of a misnomer**";
  it means action consonant "with the terms of the parties' agreement and its purpose."
- It does not "establish a **free-floating requirement** that a party act in some morally commendable
  sense."
- "The Court will **not rewrite a contract** simply because a party now wishes it had gotten a better
  deal."

**The elements are contractual**: "a specific implied contractual obligation, a breach of that
obligation by the defendant, and resulting damage to the plaintiff."

### The specified-discretion rule

- General: "[w]hen a contract confers discretion on one party, the implied covenant requires that the
  discretion be used **reasonably and in good faith**."
- **The limit that matters**: "if the **scope of discretion is specified**, there is no gap in the
  contract as to the scope of the discretion, and there is no reason for the Court to look to the
  implied covenant to determine how discretion should be exercised."
- Sole-discretion clauses ordinarily invite the covenant because "the party entitled to exercise that
  discretion may **abuse it for self-interested reasons**" — but where the parties themselves addressed
  that risk, they "**recognized (and filled) the gap**."

### Waiver counsels extra restraint

"When an LP [or LLC] agreement **eliminates fiduciary duties as part of a detailed contractual
governance scheme**, Delaware courts should be **all the more hesitant** to resort to the implied
covenant," because such an agreement "implies an agreement that **losses should remain where they
fall**."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Governing instrument | The **operating agreement**; the Act supplies defaults where it is silent |
| Default duties | Corporate-style fiduciary duties, § 18-1104 as amended in 2013 |
| Waiver | Permitted, but must be **explicit** |
| Non-waivable | The implied covenant, § 18-1101(c) |
| Elements of an implied covenant claim | Specific implied obligation, breach, damage — **contract elements** |
| First question | Is the contract **silent** on the subject? If not, the claim ends |
| Measuring expectations | **At the time of contracting** |
| Where duties are waived | Courts are "all the more hesitant"; losses lie where they fall |
| What is not available | Entire fairness, Revlon, Unocal, Corwin — the entire corporate apparatus, once duties are waived |

## Exceptions

- **The corporate form is the alternative, and the difference is stark.** "[I]f the parties had chosen to
  employ the **corporate form** here, with its common-law fiduciary duties, this matter would be subject
  to **entire fairness review**." Compare [[In re Trados]] — nearly identical economics, opposite result.
- **Corporations cannot do this at the entity level.** Stockholders may not use the charter or bylaws to
  rearrange the board's § 141(a) authority — [[Gorman v. Salamone]],
  [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co]],
  [[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]]. Gorman's footnote is the bridge: "a Delaware
  corporation is a **board-centric entity**. Other governance structures can be imposed on other
  entities, if that is what the stakeholders desire."
- **Corporations can do something similar at the stockholder level.**
  [[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]] upholds a covenant not to sue in a stockholder-level
  agreement, while noting that this "does not turn corporations into LLCs," because "the fundamental
  differences... operate at the **basal level of their statutes and constitutive documents**."
- **Intentional wrongdoing.** Rich holds that Delaware "generally prohibits contractual provisions that
  purport to exculpate a party for tort liability resulting from **intentional or reckless harm**,"
  extended in corporate law to recklessness but never to intent. The LLC Act's analogous limit is
  § 18-1101(c) — the implied covenant — and § 18-1101(e), which bars limiting liability for a **bad
  faith violation of the implied covenant**.
- **Mandatory provisions protecting third parties** bind regardless of the agreement.
- **Elf Atochem contracts away the forum, not the claim.** Elf could still litigate everything — in
  California, by arbitration or in court.

## Leading case

[[Elf Atochem North America v. Jaffari]]. A two-member joint venture whose LLC agreement required
arbitration in San Francisco and selected California courts, and which the LLC itself never signed. Elf
sued in Chancery, individually and derivatively, alleging that Jaffari breached fiduciary duties,
withdrew funds for personal use, pushed the LLC toward insolvency and interfered with business
opportunities.

Dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, and affirmed. The LLC is bound because § 18-101(7)
defines the agreement as one "of the member or members" and the members signed it. The derivative label
is irrelevant because the clauses "do not distinguish between direct and derivative claims." Even counts
pleaded under a separate Distributorship Agreement with no forum clause are swept in, because "all of
plaintiff's claims revolve around Jaffari's conduct (or misconduct) as Malek's manager." And the
statutory grants of Chancery jurisdiction are defaults members may alter.

The casebook's framing is the one to remember: a corporation "must have a board of directors, the
directors must be human individuals..., the board must meet regularly, and the fiduciary duties of the
directors are rather difficult to alter." With an LLC, "[d]on't want a board? Fine. Don't want to meet
every quarter? No problem."

## Best counter-case

[[Miller v. HCP & Co]] shows what the freedom costs the minority, and is the better case for the exam
because it runs the implied covenant analysis end to end.

The distinguishing line: **Elf Atochem lets members contract away the forum; Miller shows that once they
also contract away fiduciary duties, the only surviving protection usually has no work to do.**

The operating agreement waived all fiduciary duties (§§ 2.05, 3.09) and gave an HCP-appointed board
majority "**sole discretion**" over "the manner in which... an Approved Sale shall occur," subject only
to the sale being to an unaffiliated third party. The waterfall gave HCP the bulk of the first $30
million and 0% of the next dollars, so the board had every reason to stop negotiating there. The
plaintiffs alleged no open process, undisclosed calls with the buyer, litigation scare tactics, and an
admission by an HCP manager that a more open process should have been used.

All assumed true, and all irrelevant. "[T]he **incentive complained of is obvious on the face of the
OA**," so it was a term rather than an unanticipated gap. Section 8.06(a) specified the scope of
discretion, so there was no gap as to scope. And the parties had themselves filled the self-dealing gap
that sole-discretion clauses usually leave, by barring insider sales. Dismissed on the pleadings.

The justification is ex ante: the agreement "was drafted to attract capital investment, by allowing an
exit on terms favorable to the investors," so implying an auction requirement "would deprive the
Defendants of a **negotiated-for benefit**."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Saying LLC managers owe no default fiduciary duties.** They do, since the 2013 amendment to
   § 18-1104. What is true is that the duties are **waivable**, and the waiver must be **explicit**.
2. **Saying the implied covenant is a general fairness backstop.** "Fair" is "something of a misnomer,"
   good faith means faithfulness to the contract's scope purpose and terms, and the doctrine is "rarely
   invoked successfully."
3. **Skipping the gap question.** It is step one and usually dispositive. If the contract addresses the
   subject, existing terms control and the analysis ends.
4. **Assuming "sole discretion" invites the covenant.** It does where the grant is unqualified; it does
   not where the **scope is specified**. Look for the limiting clause.
5. **Measuring expectations at the wrong time.** As of **contracting**, not as of the transaction. A
   risk visible in the document when signed is a term, not a gap.
6. **Forgetting that the LLC is bound by an unsigned agreement.** Section 18-101(7) defines it as an
   agreement of the members, who are "the real parties in interest."
7. **Thinking the derivative label preserves a Delaware forum.** It does not, where the clause covers any
   action.
8. **Reaching for corporate doctrine.** Entire fairness, Revlon, Unocal and Corwin have no purchase once
   duties are waived. That is precisely what Miller's entity-choice sentence is pointing out.
9. **Forgetting the mandatory/default test.** Provisions protecting **third parties** bind; provisions
   structuring the members' own relations do not.
