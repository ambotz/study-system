## Rule statement

Actual authority is created by a principal's manifestation to an agent that, as
**reasonably understood by the agent**, expresses the principal's assent that the
agent act on the principal's behalf (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 3.01).

The test is objective and runs on the manifestation. What the principal privately
intended to accomplish is a separate question from what the principal authorised,
and only the second determines whether the agent's act binds the principal.

## Elements

**1. A manifestation by the principal to the agent.**

Words, conduct, or — critically — review of a specific document circulated for
approval followed by assent or silence. Circulating a draft, receiving it back
without objection, and letting the agent proceed is a manifestation.

**2. Reasonable understanding by the agent.**

Measured from the agent's position, not the principal's. An agent who reasonably
reads the approved documents as authorising an act has actual authority to do it.

**3. Assent to the agent acting on the principal's behalf.**

What does **not** defeat authority:

- The principal's contrary intention as to the transaction's purpose.
- The principal's failure to understand the document's effect.
- That the authorised act was catastrophic and nobody wanted it.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Question asked | What did the principal authorise the agent to do |
| Question **not** asked | What did the principal intend to accomplish |
| Perspective | The agent's reasonable understanding |
| Effect of a mistake in the approved document | None — the agent performing the approved document has not exceeded authority |

## Exceptions

- **Apparent authority** operates on manifestations to the *third party* rather
  than to the agent, and can bind a principal who gave the agent no actual
  authority at all. The two are independent routes to the same result.
- **Ratification** can supply authority after the fact, but requires knowledge of
  the material facts — see [[Agent duty of loyalty - self-dealing]], where an
  uninformed principal's ratification and waiver both failed.
- The agent's own **duties** are a separate question from the agent's **power**.
  An agent may bind the principal while breaching every duty owed.

## Leading case

[[In re Motors Liquidation]] — JPMorgan reviewed and assented to a UCC-3
termination statement that released a $1.5 billion security interest it never
intended to touch. Because JPMorgan and its counsel manifested assent to the
document, the filing was authorised and effective.

## Best counter-case

No true counter-case is assigned. The nearest contrast is
[[Detroit Lions v. Argovitz]], which concerns an agent's **duties** rather than an
agent's **power**.

The distinguishing line: **Motors Liquidation asks what the agent could do;
Argovitz asks what the agent owed while doing it.** A fact pattern about whether
the principal is bound is an authority question; a fact pattern about whether the
agent must answer for the outcome is a loyalty question. Both can be live at once.

## Common trap

1. **Arguing nobody meant it.** The court assumes throughout that nobody intended
   the result. Intention is the question the opinion sets aside in its first move,
   so an answer resting on it has restated the losing argument.
2. **Confusing scope with duty.** An agent can have full authority to do something
   and still be liable to the principal for doing it. Separate the two.
3. **Missing that silence is a manifestation.** Review without objection to a
   circulated document is the fact pattern that decides
   [[In re Motors Liquidation]], and it is the version most likely to reappear.
4. **Forgetting apparent authority.** Where a fact pattern removes the principal's
   manifestation to the agent, check whether there was a manifestation to the third
   party instead.
