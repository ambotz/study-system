## Rule statement

Where the business judgment rule applies, a court will not review the
**substance** of the board's decision. Whether the decision was wise, profitable,
or consistent with industry practice is not a question courts answer. The
directors' judgment carries a presumption that the judgment was formed in good
faith and designed to promote the corporation's best interests, and that judgment
is accepted as final.

A court intervenes only where the directors' conduct at least **borders on fraud,
illegality, or conflict of interest**. Those three triggers are the only doors
into the boardroom, and the rest of the course is largely about how each door
opens.

## Elements

The plaintiff must plead conduct crossing, or at least approaching, one of three
lines. Nothing else gets a court to the merits.

**1. Fraud.**

Deception of the corporation or the stockholders. The narrowest of the three and
the least litigated, because a plaintiff with real fraud evidence rarely needs the
business judgment rule discussion at all.

**2. Illegality.**

A decision to violate the law is outside the protection regardless of whether the
violation was profitable. Illegality also supplies the bridge to bad faith and
oversight liability, where a conscious disregard of legal obligations becomes a
loyalty problem rather than a care problem — see
[[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]].

**3. Conflict of interest.**

The trigger that does the real work. The line runs between two things that look
similar and are not:

- **Personal gain** — the director or controller stands to receive something the
  other stockholders do not. **Protection is lost**, and the transaction moves toward
  entire fairness review. See [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]] and
  [[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]].
- **Personal preference** — the director acts on idiosyncratic conviction,
  aesthetic judgment, or civic concern, taking nothing from the decision.
  **Protection holds**. Philip Wrigley's belief that baseball belongs in daylight
  produced no side payment, so it was preference and not conflict, even though he
  held 80% of the stock and the corporation lost money for five years.

Control alone is not conflict. Control **plus a transaction the controller sits on
both sides of** is conflict.

**What falls outside this module entirely**

- A challenge to the board's **process** rather than its substance. That challenge
  routes to [[Duty of care - informed decision]], which is how protection gets
  lost from the inside rather than through one of the three doors.
- A board that never decided anything. Inattention is an oversight claim under
  [[In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation|Caremark]].

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Presumption | Judgment formed in good faith, to promote the corporation's best interests |
| Burden | Plaintiff, at the pleading stage |
| Threshold to plead | Conduct that at least borders on fraud, illegality, or conflict of interest |
| If the threshold is not met | Dismissal; the merits are never reached |
| Never reviewed | Whether the decision was correct, profitable, or conventional |

## Exceptions

- **Legitimate indirect and long-run interests do not defeat the rule.** A director
  may weigh considerations that look non-corporate on their face where those
  considerations connect to the corporation's position over time — patron
  willingness to attend a park in a deteriorating neighborhood, and the long-run
  value of the corporation's real property, both qualified in
  [[Shlensky v. Wrigley]]. The effect is to make the conflict trigger harder to
  reach, because a personal-sounding motive can usually be reframed as a long-run
  corporate one.
- **Departure from industry practice is not a trigger.** Directors are elected to
  exercise judgment and cannot be required to adopt another board's. "Mere failure
  to 'follow the crowd'" is not dereliction, and this holds even where the
  corporation is losing money.
- **Corporate purpose is not policed through this rule.** 8 Del. C. § 122 permits
  donations for public welfare, charitable, scientific, and educational purposes,
  and Delaware's public benefit corporation statute supplies an express
  dual-purpose form. Neither is needed to sustain a decision that can be framed as
  serving long-run corporate interest.

## Leading case

[[Shlensky v. Wrigley]] — a derivative complaint dismissed on the pleadings even
with every allegation about Wrigley's personal motives taken as true, because the
complaint alleged no fraud, no illegality, and no gain flowing to Wrigley
personally.

## Best counter-case

[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] — the same rule, reaching the opposite result, because the
attack landed on process rather than substance.

The distinguishing line: **a fact pattern attacking the wisdom of a choice is a
Shlensky problem and loses; a fact pattern attacking the information behind a
choice is a Van Gorkom problem and can win.** Posture reinforces the point —
Shlensky failed at the pleading stage with facts assumed true, while Van Gorkom
produced a money judgment after trial.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Arguing the merits.** Any answer explaining why the board's decision was
   commercially wrong has already conceded the case. The court in
   [[Shlensky v. Wrigley]] says twice that the correctness of the decision is
   beyond its jurisdiction and ability. Argue what a court may examine, not what
   the board should have done.
2. **Treating control as conflict.** Wrigley held 80% and won. Control becomes a
   conflict when the controller is on both sides of a transaction or receives
   something the minority does not, and the later controlling-stockholder cases
   are built on that distinction rather than on control itself.
3. **Confusing preference with gain.** A director acting on personal conviction
   keeps the protection. A director extracting personal benefit loses it. The exam
   version of this question supplies a motive that sounds personal and asks
   whether protection survives; the answer turns on whether anything flowed to the
   director.
4. **Collapsing scope into application.** Two separate questions live here:
   whether the rule applies, and how much protection the rule gives once it
   applies. This module answers the second. [[Duty of care - informed decision]]
   answers the first. A fact pattern usually raises one, and answering the wrong
   one earns nothing.
5. **Forgetting posture.** Where the rule is raised on a motion to dismiss, the
   allegations are taken as true and the plaintiff still loses if the three
   triggers are unpleaded. Noting that the deference survives assumed-true
   allegations is the strongest form of the point.
