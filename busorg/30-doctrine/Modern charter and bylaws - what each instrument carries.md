## Rule statement

Under the modern enabling statute the charter no longer **grants** corporate power;
it **varies defaults**. Purpose is general — a corporation may be formed "to engage
in any lawful act or activity" (§ 102(a)(3)) — so the interesting content of a
charter is everything it elects out of.

The division of labour between the two instruments:

- **Certificate of incorporation** — the only instrument that can displace board
  authority under § 141(a), and the only home for exculpation under § 102(b)(7).
- **Bylaws** — "any provision, not inconsistent with law or with the certificate of
  incorporation, relating to the business of the corporation, the conduct of its
  affairs, and its rights or powers or the rights or powers of its stockholders,
  directors, officers or employees" (§ 109(b)). Process, not substance.

## Elements

**1. What the charter must or may carry.**

- **Purpose** — § 102(a)(3). A general-purpose clause is now standard, which is the
  whole distance travelled from [[Early corporate charters - enumerated powers]].
- **Capital structure**, including **blank-check preferred**: authority for the
  board to fix by resolution the designations, powers, preferences and rights of
  unissued series. This is the mechanism Laster points to in
  [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]] — a single golden share
  whose certificate of designations becomes part of the charter by law.
- **Variations on § 141(a)**, which is the only place they are permitted.
- **§ 102(b)(7) exculpation**, which cannot live in the bylaws.
- **Supermajority amendment thresholds**, entrenching the provisions above.

**2. What the bylaws carry.** Under § 109(a) the power to adopt, amend or repeal
bylaws sits with the stockholders once the corporation has been paid for stock,
**but** a corporation may by its certificate confer that power on directors — and
doing so "shall not divest the stockholders of the power." Hence the standard
pattern: both hold the power, with a supermajority imposed on the stockholders.

Bylaws in practice carry meeting mechanics, quorum, advance-notice and proxy-access
procedure, officer roles, indemnification, and forum selection.

**3. The § 109(b) ceiling.** Bylaws may not be inconsistent with law or with the
charter, and under [[Gorman v. Salamone]] may not reach substance. A bylaw is tested
by context and purpose, not by how it is drafted.

## Worked example — NVIDIA

Read the charter as a list of elections, not as prose.

| Charter article | What it does | DGCL hook |
|---|---|---|
| III | "any lawful act or activity" | § 102(a)(3) |
| IV | 4,000,000,000 common, 2,000,000 preferred; board may fix each series by Preferred Stock Designation | blank-check preferred; the Moelis cure |
| V.A.1 | management "vested in its Board"; board fixes its own size exclusively | § 141(a), (b) |
| V.A.2 | classified board **whose classification terminated at the 2014 annual meeting** — vestigial text | — |
| V.A.3 | removal **with** cause by majority; **without** cause by 66⅔% | raises the § 141(k) default |
| V.A.4 | vacancies filled by the board only, not stockholders | § 223 |
| V.A.5 | cumulative voting **if** Cal. Corp. Code § 2115 applies | quasi-California statute; NVIDIA sits in Santa Clara |
| V.B.1 | stockholders may amend bylaws only by 66⅔%; board also empowered | § 109(a) |
| V.B.3 | **no stockholder action by written consent** after the IPO | opts out of the § 228(a) default |
| VI.A | exculpation, reciting the four carve-outs verbatim | § 102(b)(7) |
| VII.B | 66⅔% to amend Articles V, VI, VII | entrenchment |

**The bylaws {skim} — what to notice, not what they say.** Twenty-two pages, almost
all of it machinery. The provisions that do work for this course:

- **Section 5 — advance notice, and § 5(h) proxy access**, with an "Exclusive
  Method" clause making these the only routes to get a nominee into the company's
  proxy materials. This is the private-ordering counterpart to the federal proxy
  rules in session 22.
- **Section 10 — anyone soliciting proxies must use a card of a colour other than
  white, which is reserved to the Board.** A one-sentence provision, and a real
  weapon in a universal-proxy contest.
- **Section 48 — amendments**, mirroring the charter's 66⅔% stockholder threshold
  while leaving the board free to act by simple majority. The asymmetry is the point.
- **Section 49 — Delaware forum** for derivative actions, fiduciary duty claims,
  DGCL claims, and internal-affairs claims. Compare the federal-forum provisions
  upheld in *Salzberg v. Sciabacucchi*, which appear later in the casebook.
- **Drafting artifact worth noticing.** Charter Art. V.B.1 is expressly "[s]ubject
  to paragraph (h) of Section 43 of the Bylaws," but Section 43 of the current
  bylaws is *Declaration of Dividends*; the amendment limitation now sits in Section
  48, subject to Section 46(h). The instruments have been renumbered out of sync
  since the 2021 restatement. A live reminder that charter and bylaws are amended on
  different clocks by different actors.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Charter vs. bylaw conflict | Charter wins — § 109(b) |
| Bylaw vs. law | Law wins — § 109(b) |
| Who may amend bylaws | Stockholders by default; directors too if the charter so provides; conferring it on directors does not divest stockholders — § 109(a) |
| Test for a bylaw's validity | Process versus substance, by context and purpose |
| Where board authority may be varied | Certificate of incorporation only |

## Exceptions

- **§ 122(18)**, added after [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]]:
  "Notwithstanding § 141(a) of this title," a corporation may make contracts with
  current or prospective stockholders in that capacity, for minimum consideration
  determined by the board, including to induce them to take or refrain from action —
  "provided that no provision of such contract shall be enforceable against the
  corporation to the extent such contract provision is contrary to the certificate
  of incorporation" or would be unlawful if included in it. Remedies are those of
  contract law. See [[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]].
- **Quasi-California.** Cal. Corp. Code § 2115 can impose California governance rules
  on a Delaware corporation with sufficient California contacts, which is why
  NVIDIA's charter carries a conditional cumulative-voting article.

## Leading case

[[Gorman v. Salamone]] for the bylaw ceiling;
[[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]] for the charter's privileged
position. The NVIDIA documents are the specimen, not authority.

## Best counter-case

[[Scott v. Depeyster]] and the 1815 National Insurance charter.

The distinguishing line: **in 1815 the charter enumerated what the corporation could
do; today it enumerates what the corporation declines to do by default.** Anything in
a modern charter is there because someone chose to displace a statutory default —
which is why reading a charter means asking, at each article, "what would happen
without this?"

## Common trap

1. **Reading a charter for what it says rather than what it varies.** Every article
   is an election. The exam version gives you a charter provision and asks what the
   default would have been.
2. **Putting exculpation in the bylaws.** § 102(b)(7) requires the certificate.
3. **Assuming the board cannot amend bylaws.** Under § 109(a) stockholders hold the
   power by default, but the charter may confer it on directors as well — and
   almost always does.
4. **Missing the asymmetry.** NVIDIA's board amends bylaws by simple majority while
   stockholders need 66⅔%. That gap, not the existence of the power, is the
   governance fact.
5. **Treating vestigial text as operative.** NVIDIA's charter still describes a
   classified board whose classification terminated in 2014.
6. **Forgetting that § 122(18) does not repeal § 141(a).** A contract under it is
   still unenforceable to the extent it contradicts the certificate.
