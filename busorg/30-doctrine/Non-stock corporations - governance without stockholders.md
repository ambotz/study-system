## Rule statement

A non-stock corporation has **no shares and, in the assigned example, no members**. Remove the
stockholder and the whole enforcement apparatus of the course loses its subject: no vote, no
derivative suit, no appraisal, no residual claimant whose value the board must maximise.

What remains is a **self-perpetuating board** and a **purpose clause**. The purpose clause does the
work the residual claimant does in a stock corporation — it **orients** the fiduciary relationship,
so that fiduciaries "must pursue the corporate purpose selflessly for the benefit of the
corporation... but they are **limited to pursuing the corporation's purpose. They cannot pick
another path simply because they prefer it**."

## Elements

### What disappears when the shares do

| Doctrine | Why it has no subject |
|---|---|
| The **franchise** — [[Interference with the franchise - Schnell, Blasius and the Coster standard]] | "The shareholder franchise is the ideological underpinning upon which the legitimacy of directorial power rests." With no electorate, legitimacy must come from elsewhere |
| **Derivative litigation** — [[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test]] | No stockholder with standing to sue on the corporation's behalf |
| **Appraisal and the merger machinery** | No shares to value or convert |
| **The residual claimant rule** — [[Preferred versus common - the duty to the residual claimants]] | No residual claim on which to maximise value |
| **Corwin cleansing, MFW, § 144** | Every safe harbour is keyed to disinterested **stockholder** approval |
| **Takeover doctrine** — [[Takeover defences - Unocal enhanced scrutiny]] | Nothing to acquire; there is no market for control |

### What remains

- **§ 141(a) or its analogue, at full strength.** The University's Articles: "The management of the
  corporation shall be vested in a Board of Trustees... **The acts of the Board of Trustees shall be
  the acts of the corporation for all purposes**." No counterparty exists who could even attempt the
  constraining bylaw at issue in [[Gorman v. Salamone]] or the stockholder agreement in
  [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co]].
- **The purpose clause**, in the **enumerated powers** form that Delaware stock corporations abandoned
  — the same structure as the
  [[An Act to Incorporate the National Insurance Company|1815 National Insurance charter]] and
  [[Early corporate charters - enumerated powers]]. Modern stock charters say "any lawful act or
  activity"; a purpose-limited charter now survives chiefly in the non-stock world.
- **Fiduciary duties of care and loyalty**, owed to the corporation — but enforced, if at all, by the
  state attorney general, donors enforcing gift restrictions, accreditors and tax authorities rather
  than by any private plaintiff with standing.
- **Charter provisions as permanent commitments.** The University's 1890 anti-sectarian covenant
  survives verbatim through the 2010 restatement: "**No religious test or particular religious
  professions shall ever be held as a requisite** for elections to said Board or for admission to said
  University... or for election to any professorship."

### The self-perpetuating board

From the assigned documents:

- **Trustees "elected by the Board of Trustees."** The board chooses its own successors.
- Forty-seven trustees at the 2010 restatement, capped by the Bylaws at **fifty-five**.
- **Five-year staggered terms** across five groups "as equal as is feasible" — the same classification
  logic as 8 Del. C. § 141(d), but adopted by a board answerable only to itself, and serving continuity
  rather than takeover defence.
- **Removal with or without cause by a majority of the Trustees.** In a stock corporation removal is a
  stockholder power under § 141(k); here the board removes its own.
- **Vacancies filled by the Board at any meeting**, and mandatory retirement after the Annual Meeting
  following a trustee's seventy-fifth birthday, waivable for one-year terms.
- **Honorary Trustees and Trustees Emeriti** are expressly not members of the Board, do not vote, hold
  no elective office and are not counted for quorum — influence without governance rights.

### Voting mechanics, and where they differ from the DGCL

- **Quorum is one-third of the Board** — the floor 8 Del. C. § 216 sets for *stockholder* quorums, here
  applied to directors, and far below the majority a Delaware board needs under § 141(b).
- The act of a **majority of those present** is the act of the Board, **except** four matters requiring
  the affirmative vote of a **majority of all Trustees**: amending the Articles; electing Trustees and
  elected officers; final action on the annual budget; and designating committees to exercise Board
  authority in management.
- **Notice**: five days for regular meetings, two for special. Special meetings may be called by the
  Chair, a Vice Chair, **any three Trustees**, or the President.
- **Three regular meetings a year**, the last being the Annual Meeting — which is why the **Executive
  Committee**, empowered "to exercise the authority of the Board of Trustees in the management of the
  University," matters so much. Compare the delegation limits in 8 Del. C. § 141(c).

### Amendment — the single most important contrast

| | Stock corporation (Delaware) | The assigned non-stock corporation |
|---|---|---|
| **Bylaws** | Stockholders always; the board if the charter so provides (§ 109) | Board, at a regular meeting, on five days' notice, on Executive Committee recommendation |
| **Charter** | Board approval **plus a stockholder vote** (§ 242) | **Board alone**, by majority of all Trustees, on five days' notice |

In a stock corporation the charter is the one instrument the board cannot change by itself, which is
why so much of this course turns on what must live in the charter rather than the bylaws — see
[[Modern charter and bylaws - what each instrument carries]]. Here that asymmetry vanishes. The board
amends both, and the only remaining difference is procedural.

### Financial mechanics

**8 Del. C. § 154**: "for purposes of this section and §§ 160 and 170..., **the capital of any nonstock
corporation shall be deemed to be zero**." With capital at zero, net assets are entirely surplus — the
legal capital constraint in [[Dividends and legal capital - 170 and 154]] simply does not bind. The
operative constraints are the non-distribution constraint of not-for-profit status, donor restrictions,
and tax law.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Governing law | The state's **not-for-profit corporation act** — for the assigned documents, Illinois |
| Management | Vested in the board; its acts are the acts of the corporation |
| Board accountability | **Internal only** — the board elects, removes and replaces itself |
| Duties | Care and loyalty, owed to the corporation, **oriented by the purpose clause** |
| Who enforces | The state **attorney general**; donors on gift restrictions; accreditors; tax authorities. Not private plaintiffs with standing |
| Charter amendment | Board alone, in the assigned documents |
| Capital | **Deemed zero**, § 154 |
| Indemnification | "[T]o the fullest extent authorized by" the governing not-for-profit act — the analogue of § 145 |

## Exceptions

- **Most non-stock corporations have members**, and members function roughly as stockholders do —
  8 Del. C. § 228(b) lets members act by written consent, and the DGCL's non-stock provisions generally
  parallel the stock ones. The University's "**The corporation shall have no members**" is the sharper
  case, not the typical one.
- **Attorney general enforcement is real**, and is the principal external check on a self-perpetuating
  board.
- **Donor restrictions are enforceable** as gift terms, and a corporation acting as trustee for
  restricted funds — as the University's Article 4 expressly contemplates — takes on trust obligations
  alongside its corporate ones.
- **Tax law constrains distribution.** Not-for-profit status bars private inurement and excess benefit
  transactions, which polices the self-dealing that entire fairness would police in a stock corporation.
- **The purpose clause is a real limit**, and the cleanest corporate-law statement of why is in
  [[New Enterprise Associates 14 v. Rich]]: a purpose clause **orients** a fiduciary relationship, and
  fiduciaries "cannot pick another path simply because they prefer it."

## Leading case

There is no case for this session. The reading is the **documents**:
[[University of Chicago Articles and Bylaws]] — Restated Articles filed with the Illinois Secretary of
State on September 7, 2010, and Bylaws last amended May 26, 2022.

Three provisions carry the session. "**The corporation shall have no members.**" "The management of the
corporation shall be vested in a Board of Trustees... The acts of the Board of Trustees shall be the
acts of the corporation for all purposes." And "**Trustees shall be... elected by the Board of
Trustees**."

The fourth thing to notice is the page-long Article 4 purpose clause — higher education "to persons of
both sexes on equal terms," schools and a university "in which may be taught all branches of higher
learning," power to prescribe courses, employ faculty, fix tuition, receive and invest gifts, act as
trustee for donors, and grant degrees — which is the closest thing to an accountability mechanism the
documents contain.

## Best counter-case

The **stock corporation** is the counter-case, and the best paired comparison is the
[[NVIDIA Certificate of Incorporation]] and [[NVIDIA Bylaws]] from earlier in the term. Same
two-instrument architecture; entirely different allocation of power.

The distinguishing line: **in a stock corporation the board's power is licensed by a franchise it
cannot control and constrained by an instrument it cannot amend alone; in this non-stock corporation
neither is true.**

Run the comparison across four axes and the point is unmistakable:

| | NVIDIA | The University |
|---|---|---|
| Who elects directors | Stockholders | The Board |
| Who amends the charter | Board **and** stockholders, § 242 | The Board alone |
| Who may sue for breach | Stockholders, directly or derivatively | Effectively, the attorney general |
| Purpose | Any lawful act or activity | A page of enumerated objects |

The closing question the session poses is the one worth having an answer to: **if there are no residual
claimants, for whose benefit do the trustees manage?** [[In re Trados]] gives the corporate answer —
maximise value for the residual claimants, "not for the benefit of its contractual claimants" — and it
has no referent here. The documents' answer is the purpose clause, enforced by everyone except a
private plaintiff.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Assuming a non-stock corporation has members.** Many do and function roughly like stock
   corporations; the assigned one expressly does not, and that is the whole point of the reading.
2. **Importing stockholder doctrine.** Blasius, Corwin, MFW, Revlon, Unocal, § 144 and Rule 23.1 are all
   keyed to stockholders. Say what is missing before saying what applies.
3. **Assuming the § 242 rule.** Charter amendment requiring a stockholder vote is a **stock corporation**
   rule. Here the board amends the charter alone, which removes the entire charter/bylaw asymmetry the
   course is built on.
4. **Saying trustees owe no fiduciary duties.** They owe care and loyalty to the corporation. The
   difficulty is **enforcement**, not existence.
5. **Overlooking the purpose clause.** In a non-stock corporation it is the substitute for the residual
   claimant, and the Rich orientation language is the cleanest way to say why.
6. **Forgetting § 154.** The capital of a nonstock corporation is deemed zero, so the legal capital
   constraint on distributions does not bind.
7. **Missing the quorum and supermajority structure.** One-third quorum for the Board, with four matters
   requiring a majority of **all** trustees — including charter amendments and the designation of
   committees with Board authority.
8. **Underrating the Executive Committee.** A board meeting three times a year delegates the authority of
   the Board in management to a standing committee. That is where the governance actually happens.
