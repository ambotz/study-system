## Rule statement

**Directors' fiduciary duties always run to the corporation.** Insolvency changes **who may enforce
them**, not what they are.

- **Direct** fiduciary claims by creditors: **never**, whether the corporation is insolvent or in the
  zone of insolvency.
- **Derivative** standing for creditors: **on actual insolvency only**, because creditors then "take
  the place of the shareholders as the **residual beneficiaries** of any increase in value."
- **What a creditor can win** once standing exists: entire fairness review of **specific transfers of
  value to the controller**, and nothing as to **firm-wide business strategy**, which stays in the
  business judgment rule.

"[T]he general rule is that **directors do not owe creditors duties beyond the relevant contractual
terms**."

## Elements

### The grid

| | Zone of insolvency | Actual insolvency |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** creditor fiduciary claim | No | No |
| **Derivative** creditor standing | No — the company is solvent, stockholders enforce | **Yes** |
| Whose interests directors serve | Unchanged: the corporation and its **shareholders** | The corporation, for its **residual claimants**, which now includes creditors |

"When a solvent corporation is navigating in the zone of insolvency, **the focus for Delaware
directors does not change**."

### Pleading insolvency — two tests

- **Balance sheet**: "liabilities in excess of a reasonable market value of assets held," or "a
  deficiency of assets below liabilities **with no reasonable prospect that the business can be
  successfully continued** in the face thereof."
- **Cash flow**: "an inability to **meet maturing obligations as they fall due** in the ordinary course
  of business."

"In a mature company, the existence of 'a great disparity between assets and liabilities... at least
raises an issue of material fact as to whether the company was insolvent' sufficient to survive a
motion to dismiss."

**The zone of insolvency is deliberately never defined.** Gheewalla declines to define it below and on
appeal, because the holding makes definition unnecessary.

### Why no direct claim — the four reasons

1. **Creditors are already protected**, by "contractual agreements, fraud and fraudulent conveyance
   law, implied covenants of good faith and fair dealing, bankruptcy law, general commercial law and
   other sources of creditor rights." Recognising fiduciary duties here means "using the law of
   fiduciary duty to **fill gaps that do not exist**."
2. **Directors need clarity.** The court aims to provide "**clear signal beacons and brightly lined
   channel markers**," and duties that shift somewhere inside an undefined zone supply the opposite.
3. **Distressed companies need decisive management.** "[A]n otherwise solvent corporation operating in
   the zone of insolvency is one **in most need of effective and proactive leadership**—as well as the
   ability to negotiate in good faith with its creditors."
4. **A direct duty would create an impossible conflict** between "those directors' duty to maximize the
   value of the insolvent corporation for the benefit of **all those having an interest in it**, and
   the newly recognized direct fiduciary duty to **individual creditors**." Directors "must retain the
   freedom to engage in **vigorous, good faith negotiations** with individual creditors."

### Why insolvency does not convert derivative claims into direct ones

"The fact that the corporation has become insolvent **does not turn [derivative] claims into direct
creditor claims, it simply provides creditors with standing** to assert those claims. At all times,
claims of this kind **belong to the corporation itself** because even if the improper acts occur when
the firm is insolvent, they operate to injure the firm in the first instance by reducing its value,
injuring creditors only **indirectly**."

The narrow "marked degree of animus" direct claim floated in Production Resources and Big Lots does not
exist: "**We think not.**"

### The Quadrant sorting rule — what the derivative claim can reach

| Challenged decision | Standard | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific transfers** to the controller — interest on notes it holds, above-market fees to its affiliate | **Entire fairness**, burden on defendants | The transfer confers a **direct and specific benefit** and moves value from all residual claimants to the party in control |
| **Firm-wide business decisions** — more risk, keep operating, liquidate | **Business judgment rule** | Affects "the value of the entity as a whole" and confers no specific benefit |

"[W]hen directors make decisions that appear **rationally designed to increase the value of the firm as
a whole**, Delaware courts do not speculate about whether those decisions might benefit some residual
claimants more than others."

### The solvent/insolvent asymmetry

"A transfer of value from a **solvent** subsidiary to the holder of 100% of the equity **cannot give
rise to a fiduciary wrong**. Before the transfer, the 100% stockholder owned the value indirectly and
beneficially. After the transfer, the 100% stockholder owned the value directly."

But on insolvency "a transfer of value to the sole stockholder does not inure to the ratable benefit of
all of the residual claimants. The payment now transfers value previously owned beneficially and
indirectly by all of the residual claimants **to the party in control** of the corporation."

**Inaction counts.** "A conscious decision not to take action is just as much of a decision as a
decision to act" — "'inaction' and 'action' may be substantive equivalents, different only in form."

### The trust fund doctrine is not the law

- "Current Delaware law does not require the Board to **shut down** [the] business and manage towards a
  near-term dissolution for the benefit of creditors."
- "**The directors continue to have the task of attempting to maximize the economic value of the
  firm.**"
- "Even when a firm is insolvent, its directors may, in the appropriate exercise of their business
  judgment, take action that might, if it does not pan out, result in the firm being **painted in a
  deeper hue of red**... By doing so, the directors **do not become a guarantor of success**."
- **Liquidation is equally protected.** "[T]he **efficient liquidation** of an insolvent firm might well
  be the method by which the firm's value is enhanced," and "the business judgment rule would protect a
  board's decision to pursue an efficient liquidation."
- There is no "**magic dividing line** that should signal the end to some, most, or all risk-taking on
  behalf of stockholders or even on behalf of creditors, who are **not homogenous** and whose interests
  may not be served by a board that refuses to undertake any further business activities that involve
  risk."

### The "reverse-Trados" theory, and its limit

[[In re Trados]]: **solvent** company, directors loyal to **preferred** holders whose contract rights
diverge from the **common** residual. Quadrant: **insolvent** company, directors loyal to the **equity**
whose interests diverge from the **creditors** who are now primary residual claimants. Same structure,
mirrored — and "[i]n a reverse of the situation in Trados, **the duty of loyalty to the common
stockholders creates the conflict**."

"The fault in this reasoning lies **not in the theory, but in its application** to business decisions
that generally affect the value of the entity as a whole." The theory works for direct and specific
benefits; it does not convert every strategic choice into an entire fairness case. Holding otherwise
"would be inconsistent with the explanation the Delaware Supreme Court gave in Gheewalla."

### The equal treatment safe harbour

From [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]]: a distribution in which the controller "received nothing... to the
exclusion of its minority stockholders" is not self-dealing and draws business judgment review;
differential treatment is. "[E]qual treatment... operates as a **presumptive safe harbor**... even when
those fiduciaries allegedly have divergent economic interests" — but it "is **not an absolute safe
harbor**," because bad faith can still produce a loyalty breach where "the fiduciary intentionally acts
with a purpose other than that of advancing the best interests of the corporation."

"Greed is not the only human emotion that can pull one from the path of propriety; so might hatred,
lust, envy, revenge,... shame or pride." But "[i]t is not enough... simply to argue **in the abstract**
that a particular director has a conflict of interest or is acting in bad faith because she is
affiliated with a particular type of institution."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Standard of conduct | Maximise the value of the corporation for its **residual claimants** — stockholders when solvent, creditors joining them on insolvency |
| Default standard of review | Business judgment rule |
| Entire fairness trigger | Actual conflicts such that the deciding directors were not a disinterested and independent majority — in practice, a **specific transfer to the controller** |
| Burden, entire fairness | On the **defendants** |
| Standing | Creditors, **derivatively**, on pleading insolvency by the balance sheet or cash flow test |
| **§ 102(b)(7)** | **Applies.** "When creditors assert derivative claims..., they are seeking to impose **personal liability on directors**, so Section 102(b)(7) potentially applies" |
| Defeating exculpation | Plead that directors "**depend on an interested controller for their income or employment**" |
| Rebutting BJR the traditional way | Facts supporting an inference "that **no rational person** would take on additional risk" — a hard road |

The § 102(b)(7) point means creditor derivative suits, like stockholder ones, must be pleaded as
**loyalty or bad faith**. The same facts that establish non-independence for the standard of review
also strip the exculpation defence, which is why the pleading effort concentrates there.

## Exceptions

- **Non-fiduciary direct claims survive untouched.** Creditors "may nonetheless protect their interest
  by bringing derivative claims... or **any other direct nonfiduciary claim**... that may be available."
  Contract, tort and fraudulent transfer are all available — Quadrant pairs derivative fiduciary claims
  with **direct fraudulent transfer** claims against the controller and its affiliate.
- **The legal capital rules operate independently.** Section 170 constrains the source of funds for a
  distribution regardless of any fiduciary analysis. See
  [[Dividends and legal capital - 170 and 154]].
- **Contract is the primary protection** and is what Gheewalla's whole rationale rests on — covenants,
  liens, security instruments and negotiated agreements.
- **Bad faith defeats the equal treatment safe harbour**, but only on specific allegations and evidence,
  not on abstract assertions of conflict.
- **Solvency restores the ordinary rule.** A transfer to a 100% stockholder of a solvent subsidiary is
  no wrong at all, and "in a parent and wholly-owned subsidiary context, the directors of the subsidiary
  are obligated only to manage the affairs of the subsidiary in the best interests of the parent and its
  shareholders."

## Leading case

[[NACEPF v. Gheewalla]]. A creditor holding FCC spectrum licences sued three Clearwire directors who
served "at the behest of Goldman Sachs" and allegedly controlled the company because Goldman was its
only source of funding. Crucially, NACEPF **waived any derivative theory** and asserted only direct
claims.

Held: no direct claim in the zone of insolvency, and none in actual insolvency either. Derivative
standing exists on insolvency because "[t]he corporation's insolvency 'makes the creditors the principal
constituency injured by any fiduciary breaches that diminish the firm's value'," so "equitable
considerations give creditors standing to pursue derivative claims."

The case is a lesson in pleading as much as in doctrine. A derivative complaint on the same facts —
directors favouring the controller's agenda at an insolvent company — is precisely what
[[Quadrant Structured Products v. Vertin]] allows.

## Best counter-case

[[Quadrant Structured Products v. Vertin]] is where the derivative claim actually works, and where its
boundary is drawn.

The distinguishing line: **Gheewalla tells you who may sue; Quadrant tells you what they can win, and
the answer turns entirely on whether the decision conferred a direct and specific benefit on the
controller.**

Three decisions, one pleading standard, two results. **Not deferring interest** on Junior Notes held by
EBF — which owned 100% of the equity and would receive nothing on those notes in a liquidation —
survives under entire fairness. **Above-market service and licence fees** paid to an EBF affiliate
survive on the same analysis. **Amending the Operating Guidelines to permit riskier investments** is
dismissed, because it "affected the value of the entity as a whole."

The authorities marshalled for that limit all did the same thing. **Shandler** dismissed a claim that a
controller-affiliated board caused an insolvent company "to take a reckless and value-destroying gamble"
for the controller's benefit, holding the board "was entitled to exercise a good faith business judgment
to continue to operate the business." **Production Resources** treated continued operation as an
exculpated **care** claim while letting challenges to **specific transfers** proceed as non-exculpated
derivative claims. **Trenwick** "spoke consistently in terms of the business judgment rule" despite
pleaded dual-fiduciary status.

Insolvency was pleaded here on the balance sheet test — $100 million of equity capital, six times that
in long-term debt, another 500x leverage writing swaps, $48 million and then $320 million paid to unwind
two swaps, and $600 million of bond debt against $426 million of assets, a figure the defendants' own
brief put at $747 million of liabilities.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Saying directors owe duties to creditors on insolvency.** They owe duties to the **corporation**
   throughout. Creditors gain **standing**, not a duty of their own.
2. **Allowing a direct claim.** Never — not in the zone, not on actual insolvency, and not on the
   "marked degree of animus" theory Gheewalla killed with "We think not."
3. **Treating the zone of insolvency as a doctrinal trigger.** It changes nothing, which is why Delaware
   has never defined it.
4. **Forgetting the two insolvency tests.** Balance sheet or cash flow. Quadrant used the balance sheet
   test.
5. **Missing the sorting rule.** Specific transfers to the controller → entire fairness. Firm-wide
   strategy → business judgment. Ask what the decision conferred, on whom, and whether it was specific.
6. **Asserting the trust fund doctrine.** Directors of an insolvent firm need not wind down; they may
   keep taking risk, may operate at a loss, and may liquidate — all protected.
7. **Treating dual-fiduciary status as enough.** It creates a conflict as to specific benefits, not as to
   decisions affecting the firm as a whole — the limit on the reverse-Trados theory.
8. **Overlooking inaction.** Failing to defer a payment that flows to the controller is reviewed like an
   affirmative transfer.
9. **Forgetting § 102(b)(7).** It reaches creditor derivative claims, so the complaint must plead loyalty
   or bad faith, usually through dependence on the controller for income or employment.
10. **Ignoring the non-fiduciary routes.** Fraudulent transfer, contract and tort claims are expressly
    preserved and are often the better cause of action.
