## Rule statement

Two questions, in order, and conflating them is the commonest error:

1. **Is there a controller?** At common law, a stockholder owning more than 50% of voting
   power, or owning less but who "exercises control over the business affairs of the
   corporation."
2. **Is the transaction self-dealing?** Fiduciary duty alone "will not evoke the intrinsic
   fairness standard." The standard applies only where the duty is accompanied by
   self-dealing — where the controller "receives something from the subsidiary **to the
   exclusion of, and detriment to**, the minority stockholders."

A controller can dominate completely and still get business judgment review, provided value
moves **proportionately**.

## Elements

### Question 1 — controller status

**Route A: more than 50% of voting power.** Straightforward, and usually conceded.
[[Sinclair Oil v. Levien|Sinclair]] held 97%.

**Route B: less than 50%, plus actual control.** The test is "actual domination and control
over ... [the] directors," with power "so potent that independent directors ... [could not]
freely exercise their judgment." Two ways to plead it:

- **Generally** — domination of the corporation or of a majority of the board at large.
- **Transaction-specifically** — domination of the board or the deciding committee with
  respect to the challenged transaction. *This is the narrower and more commonly available
  theory, and the one [[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]] accepts.*

**There is no percentage threshold**, and no "linear, sliding scale approach whereby a
larger share percentage makes it substantially more likely" that control is found. The
reported range: 17.3% held reasonably conceivably controlling (*Zhongpin*); 23.1% held not
controlling (*Larkin*); 33.7% described as an "aggressive" case (*Crimson Exploration*).

Factors that carried Tesla 1 past the pleading threshold, none sufficient alone:

- influence over the stockholder vote, magnified by **supermajority bylaw thresholds**;
- influence as visionary, CEO and Chairman, where the company's own filings concede
  dependence and non-independence;
- a demonstrated willingness to **remove senior management**;
- an **interested board majority** and dense personal and financial ties;
- the controller's and the company's **own acknowledgements** of control.

### Question 2 — the self-dealing trigger

Self-dealing requires the controller to be **on both sides** and to receive something the
minority cannot share.

| Transaction | Self-dealing? | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Pro rata dividend, even exceeding earnings, even to meet the parent's cash needs | **No** — the minority receives its proportionate share | Business judgment |
| Dividend on the parent's class of stock only | **Yes** (the court's own hypothetical) | Entire fairness |
| Confining the subsidiary while expanding through other subsidiaries | **No**, absent an opportunity that **came to** the subsidiary | Business judgment |
| Contracting with the dominated subsidiary, then causing it not to enforce | **Yes** — the parent takes the products; the minority cannot share | Entire fairness |

**Motive is immaterial absent waste.** Compliance with § 170 answers whether a dividend was
**lawful**; it says nothing about fiduciary duty, and the two inquiries run in parallel.

### The statutory overlay, post-SB 21

SB 21 replaces the open-ended inquiry above with definitions:

- **§ 144(e)(2) — controlling stockholder.** A person (with affiliates and associates) who
  (a) owns or controls a **majority** in voting power; (b) has the right "by contract or
  otherwise, to cause the election of nominees" constituting a board majority; or (c) has
  power "functionally equivalent" to majority ownership by virtue of owning **at least
  one-third** in voting power **and** "power to exercise managerial authority over the
  business and affairs."
- **§ 144(e)(3) — controlling stockholder transaction.** A transaction between the
  corporation and a controller or control group, **or** one "from which a controlling
  stockholder or a control group receives a financial or other benefit **not shared with the
  corporation's stockholders generally**." *That last clause is Sinclair's proportionality
  test, in statutory form.*
- **§ 144(e)(1) — control group.** Two or more non-controllers who, "by virtue of an
  agreement, arrangement, or understanding," constitute a controlling stockholder.

**The one-third floor is the change to watch.** Musk at 22.1% clears none of the three routes
on their face, so the statute would likely take Tesla 1 out of controller analysis entirely.
That is the obvious hypothetical, and it is why the syllabus returns to this material in
session 10.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| No controller, or controller without self-dealing | Business judgment |
| Controller **plus** self-dealing | Entire fairness — "a high degree of fairness **and** a shift in the burden of proof" |
| Who bears it | The controller, to prove objective fairness under careful judicial scrutiny |
| Pleading standard for controller status | **Reasonably conceivable**; "intensely factual" and hard to resolve on the pleadings |
| Cleansing by stockholder vote | Unavailable for a conflicted-controller transaction under the common law |

## Exceptions

- **Proportionality.** Value taken pro rata is not self-dealing however large, however
  motivated, and however dominated the board.
- **No opportunity, no usurpation.** A corporate opportunity claim requires that the
  opportunity **came to** the subsidiary; allocating opportunities among subsidiaries is
  business judgment absent "gross and palpable overreaching."
- **The safe harbours in § 144(b) and (c)** — new in SB 21 — supply statutory routes out for
  controller transactions, and are taken up with [[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 2)]] in session
  10. They will get their own module.
- **A parent need not contract with its subsidiary at all**, but "[a]s Sinclair has received
  the benefits of this contract, so must it comply with the contractual duties."

## Leading case

[[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]] for the trigger — 97% ownership, a concededly non-independent
board, $108 million of dividends exceeding earnings by $38 million, and **two of three claims
still reviewed under business judgment** because the money moved pro rata.

[[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 1)]] for controller status — 22.1%, "a close call," decided on
accumulation rather than any single factor.

## Best counter-case

The two against each other, since they pull in opposite directions on how much control
matters.

The distinguishing line: **Sinclair shows that total control without disproportion is not
enough to trigger the standard; Tesla shows that a small block with enough other levers can
be enough to establish the status.** Status and trigger are separate questions, and a fact
pattern can fail on either.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Collapsing the two questions.** Controller status and self-dealing are separate. Sinclair
   had status and lost the trigger on two claims; a fact pattern can supply a 60% holder and
   still get business judgment if the transaction is proportionate.
2. **Arguing from the percentage alone.** 17.3% has been a controller and 23.1% has not.
   Citing that spread is the answer; picking a number is not.
3. **Arguing the controller's motive.** Immaterial absent waste. Sinclair took $38 million
   over earnings because it needed cash, and won that claim.
4. **Treating § 170 compliance as a defence to fiduciary duty.** It answers legality only.
5. **Forgetting the pleading posture.** Tesla 1 finds nothing; it holds control "reasonably
   conceivable." The consequence is entire fairness plus discovery, which is where these cases
   settle — so surviving dismissal is most of the game.
6. **Missing the vote-exclusion problem.** Tesla excluded three people and left in four
   interested directors and every other SolarCity-owning holder. Post-SB 21 § 144(e)(5) now
   defines a **disinterested stockholder** for exactly this purpose.
7. **Applying the common-law test to a post-March 2025 transaction.** § 144(e)(2) supplies a
   definition with a one-third floor. Establish which regime governs before running the
   analysis.
