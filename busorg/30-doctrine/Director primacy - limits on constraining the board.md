## Rule statement

8 Del. C. § 141(a): "[t]he business and affairs of every corporation organized under
this chapter shall be managed by or under the direction of a board of directors,
**except as may be otherwise provided in this chapter or in its certificate of
incorporation**." (Quoted from [[Gorman v. Salamone]] and
[[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]].)

That exception clause is the entire doctrine. An arrangement that removes board
authority is valid if it sits in the **charter** and invalid if it sits anywhere
else. Director primacy holds regardless of who holds the shares — it "remains the
centerpiece of Delaware law, even when a controlling stockholder is present."

## Elements

**1. Identify the instrument.** This is the first and usually the dispositive move.

| Instrument | May it displace board authority? |
|---|---|
| **Certificate of incorporation** | **Yes** — § 141(a) says so expressly |
| **Bylaws** | Process and procedure only, never substance |
| **Stockholder agreement** | No — internal governance cannot be done by contract |
| **External commercial contract** | Not reached by § 141(a) at all |

**2. For a bylaw: is it process or substance?**

- Bylaws may "define the process and procedures by which those decisions are made."
- Bylaws may not "mandate how the board should decide specific substantive business
  decisions."
- The classification turns on **context and purpose**, not drafting: "even facially
  procedural bylaws can unduly intrude upon board authority."
- A bylaw cannot compel board action "in circumstances that a proper application of
  fiduciary principles could preclude."
- Removal of officers **without cause** is substance, because choosing who conducts
  daily business is "a primary way by which a corporate board manages a company."

**3. For a contract: is the arrangement internal or external?**

External commercial contracts constrain boards lawfully all the time — an exclusive
supply agreement, or a credit agreement making a dividend an event of default. The
line is drawn by **comparison to prototypes**, not by a bright-line test. Markers of
an internal arrangement, all present in Moelis:

- the only parties are the company and the stockholder's own entities;
- signed on the eve of the public offering, after a reorganisation;
- resembles governance rights normally carried by **preferred stock**;
- drafted to bind **the Board** rather than the Company;
- untethered to any underlying commercial transaction;
- indefinite, and not terminable by the Board.

**4. If internal, apply the *Abercrombie* test.** Restrictions violate § 141(a) where
they "have the effect of removing from directors in a very substantial way their
duty to use their own best judgment on management matters," or "tend to limit in a
substantial way the freedom of director decisions on matters of management policy."

**5. Distinguish compelling a board decision from enabling a stockholder act.** This
is the fine-grained line Moelis actually draws, and the most likely place for a
close question:

- **Invalid** — Pre-Approval Requirements (board cannot act without consent);
  Recommendation Requirement (board must endorse his candidates); Vacancy
  Requirement (board must refill with his designees); Size Requirement (board cannot
  expand); Committee Composition Provision (also violates § 141(c)).
- **Valid on a facial challenge** — Designation Right (he identifies candidates);
  Nomination Requirement (he could nominate at a meeting anyway); Efforts
  Requirement (proxy inclusion and disclosure). As-applied challenges survive.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Nature of the question | **Power**, not duty — no fiduciary breach need be pleaded |
| Consequence | The provision is void, facially |
| Test for internal arrangements | *Abercrombie* |
| Effect of a controlling stockholder | None — primacy holds anyway |
| "It only bites when they disagree" | Not a defence: "when Moelis and the Board agree, the provisions are not operating at all" |
| Pre-approval versus veto | Equivalent: "the power to review is the power to decide" |

## Exceptions

- **The charter.** § 141(a)'s own exception clause. Everything invalid in
  [[Gorman v. Salamone]] and [[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]]
  would have been valid in the certificate of incorporation.
- **Blank-check preferred.** A board may issue preferred stock — even "a single
  golden share" — carrying voting and director-appointment rights. The certificate
  of designations becomes part of the charter **as a matter of law**, at which point
  the rights comply with § 141(a).
- **The doctrine of independent legal significance.** That the DGCL forbids one
  route to a result and permits another is deliberate. "Although some might find it
  bizarre that the DGCL would prohibit one means of accomplishing a goal while
  allowing another, that is what the doctrine of independent legal significance
  contemplates."
- **8 Del. C. § 122(18)** — assigned immediately after Moelis, and the legislature's
  response to it. **The text of § 122(18) is not in this vault**, so this module does
  not state its terms. Obtain the provision and complete this entry before relying
  on it.

## Leading case

[[West Palm Beach Firefighters v. Moelis & Co|Moelis]] — eighteen categories of
pre-approval plus six board-composition provisions in a stockholder agreement signed
the day before the IPO, held facially invalid in substantial part. "With the
Pre-Approval Requirements in place, the Board is not really a board."

Paired with [[Gorman v. Salamone]] for the bylaw branch: a stockholder-adopted bylaw
permitting direct removal of officers without cause is invalid.

## Best counter-case

No assigned case upholds an internal governance arrangement outside the charter. The
real counter-position is the **soritical argument** the Company made in Moelis: every
commercial contract constrains a board to some degree, so there is nowhere principled
to draw the line, and § 141(a) must therefore invalidate all contracts or none.

The distinguishing line: **courts distinguish by prototype, not by definition.** "The
soritical paradox bedevils logicians, but not mere humans... Cases regularly turn on
whether the facts are more like one precedent or another. That's how legal reasoning
works." A supply agreement is a housecat; the Moelis Stockholder Agreement is a lion.

## Common trap

1. **Treating it as a fiduciary duty question.** No breach is alleged in either case.
   These are **power** cases, which is why facial challenges succeed with no showing
   of harm and why the provisions are void rather than voidable.
2. **Arguing the stockholder had the votes.** Gorman held a majority and lost. Moelis
   held **less than** a majority and still controlled the board by contract, which is
   what made his arrangement worse rather than better.
3. **Stopping at invalidity.** The expected follow-up is always how to do it lawfully:
   the charter, or a golden share of preferred whose certificate of designations
   becomes part of the charter.
4. **Taking a bylaw's self-description at face value.** "This bylaw only prescribes
   procedure" was Gorman's argument. Classification turns on context, purpose, intent
   and effect.
5. **Forgetting § 141(c).** The Committee Composition Provision violated it
   independently of § 141(a), and committee composition questions carry their own
   subsection.
6. **Missing the legislative pattern.** Court invalidates a market practice, General
   Assembly amends the DGCL. [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] → § 102(b)(7); Moelis →
   § 122(18). Both pairs are assigned, which makes the pattern a likely theme rather
   than a coincidence.
