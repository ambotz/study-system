## Rule statement

Stockholders may bind themselves to each other about how they will vote. A **voting trust**
under § 218(a) transfers the stock to a trustee and must meet the statute's requirements; a
**pooling agreement** under § 218(c) leaves ownership alone and is simply a contract.

The limit is the one [[Ringling Brothers v. Ringling]] states: a stockholder may vote for any
reason, "for personal profit, or determined by whims or caprice, **so long as he violates no
duty owed his fellow shareholders**."

**Vote buying** crosses that line. It is **illegal per se** where "the object or purpose is to
defraud or in some way disenfranchise the other stockholders," and otherwise is "a **voidable
transaction subject to a test for intrinsic fairness**."

## Elements

### Voting trust — § 218(a)

- One or more stockholders **deposit stock with or transfer stock to** a trustee, "for the
  purpose of **vesting** in such person... the right to vote thereon for any period of time
  determined by such agreement."
- A **copy of the agreement is delivered to the registered office** or principal place of
  business, "open to the inspection of any stockholder... or any beneficiary of the trust
  daily during business hours."
- **New certificates or uncertificated stock issue to the trustee**, stating that they are
  issued pursuant to the agreement, and the fact is also stated in the **stock ledger**.
- The trustee votes the stock during the specified period, in person or by proxy, and "shall
  incur no responsibility as stockholder, trustee or otherwise, **except for their own
  individual malfeasance**."
- With two or more trustees and no agreed method, the right and manner of voting is
  determined by a **majority of the trustees**; if equally divided, the vote of the stock "shall
  be divided equally among the trustees."
- **(b)** Amendments must be in writing and a copy delivered to the registered office or
  principal place of business.

### Pooling agreement — § 218(c)

"An agreement between **2 or more stockholders**, if **in writing and signed** by the parties
thereto, may provide that in exercising any voting rights, the shares held by them **shall be
voted as provided by the agreement, or as the parties may agree, or as determined in
accordance with a procedure agreed upon by them**."

Three permitted forms, in ascending flexibility: a fixed instruction, whatever the parties
later agree, or a **procedure** — which is the Ringling arbitrator, codified.

**§ 218(d)** is the savings clause: "This section shall not be deemed to invalidate any voting
or other agreement among stockholders or any **irrevocable proxy** which is not otherwise
illegal."

### Voting trust versus pooling agreement

| | Voting trust, § 218(a) | Pooling agreement, § 218(c) |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership of the shares | **Transferred** to the trustee | Stays with each holder |
| Who votes | The **trustee** | **Each holder**, individually |
| Formalities | Copy filed and open to inspection; new certificates; noted in the stock ledger | **Writing, signed** — nothing more |
| Duration | As the agreement provides | As the agreement provides |
| Failure to comply | The arrangement fails as a trust | No statutory consequence |

The distinction Ringling drew before § 218(c) existed: the voting trust statute "does not
purport to deal with agreements whereby shareholders attempt to bind each other as to how they
shall vote their shares."

### The enforcement gap, and how to close it

A pooling agreement creates **promises**, not powers. Absent an express grant, no party may
vote another's shares and no arbitrator may enforce his own decision — "[t]he power to enforce
a decision does not seem a necessary or usual incident of such a function," and a convenient
implied power "would not justify implying a delegation of the power in the absence of some
indication that the parties bargained for that means."

The fix is an **irrevocable proxy under § 212(e)**: irrevocable "if it states that it is
irrevocable and if, and only as long as, it is coupled with an interest sufficient in law to
support an irrevocable power," and the interest may be "**in the stock itself or an interest in
the corporation generally**."

Without it, the remedy is **rejection of the breaching party's votes** in an election review —
"[t]he Court of Chancery may, in a review of an election, reject votes of a registered
shareholder where his voting of them is found to be in violation of rights of another person."
Blunt, and it binds only the parties: in Ringling the non-party's votes stood untouched.

### Vote buying — the Schreiber test

| | |
|---|---|
| **Per se illegal** | Where "the object or purpose is to defraud or in some way disenfranchise the other stockholders" |
| **Otherwise** | "[A] voidable transaction subject to a test for **intrinsic fairness**" |
| **Threshold pleading** | Facts supporting a reasonable inference that "in exchange for '**consideration personal to the stockholder**,' a stockholder has agreed to vote, **or has in fact voted**, his shares as directed by another" |
| **Not required** | A **contractually binding** obligation to vote a particular way |
| **Not required** | That a **majority** of outstanding shares was obligated to vote in favour |

**The distinction that reconciles everything is who is buying.** "Shareholders are free to do
whatever they want with their votes, including selling them to the highest bidder. **Management,
on the other hand, may not use corporate assets** to buy votes in a hotly contested proxy
contest about an extraordinary transaction that would significantly transform the corporation,
unless it can be demonstrated... that management's vote-buying activity does not have a
deleterious effect on the corporate franchise."

The older rule — that vote buying was per se illegal as a matter of public policy, because "each
stockholder should be entitled to rely upon the independent judgment of his fellow stockholders"
— is "obsolete because it is both impractical and impossible of application to modern
corporations," and § 218(c) codifies the permissibility of voting agreements. What survives is
per se illegality **for deleterious purposes**.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Validity of a pooling agreement | Presumptively valid; a deadlock-breaking arbitration provision is reasonable |
| Limit on a stockholder's vote | Only the duty owed to fellow shareholders |
| Vote buying by a **stockholder** | Permitted — the vote is the holder's own asset to sell |
| Vote buying by **management with corporate assets** | Voidable, tested for **intrinsic fairness**; per se illegal if the purpose is to defraud or disenfranchise |
| Burden at the pleading stage | Plaintiff pleads consideration personal to the stockholder plus voting as directed |
| Burden at trial | "[S]ignificant burden" on the plaintiff to prove coercion and to negate independent business reasons |
| What saves a management arrangement | A **ratifying vote of disinterested stockholders** — present in Schreiber, absent in Hewlett |

## Exceptions

- **Stockholders cannot contract for the board's power.** A pooling agreement allocates the
  holders' own voting rights; a bylaw purporting to let stockholders remove officers directly is
  invalid, because it "would unduly interfere with directors' management prerogatives"
  ([[Gorman v. Salamone]]). See also
  [[Director primacy - limits on constraining the board]] and § 122(18).
- **Irrevocable voting agreements can become deal protection devices.** Where an acquiror demands
  them as a condition precedent and the target is a party, they are scrutinised as part of the
  board's defensive response — [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] and
  [[Deal protection devices - Unocal review of merger lock-ups]].
- **Management arrangements with salutary purposes survive.** "It is certainly possible for
  management to enter into vote-buying arrangements with salutary purposes," and Schreiber itself
  upheld one.
- **A controller's informal assent is not a vote.** Espinoza v. Zuckerberg — the formality is what
  carries the legal effect.
- **The three-year proxy limit.** A proxy may not be voted after three years from its date unless
  it provides for a longer period, § 212(b). Long-term arrangements need drafting attention.

## Leading case

[[Ringling Brothers v. Ringling]]. Two of three stockholders, each holding 315 of 1,000 shares
with cumulative voting for seven seats, agreed to consult, confer and act jointly, with deadlocks
submitted to an arbitrator whose decision "shall be binding upon the parties." Acting together
they could elect five directors; acting alone, two each.

The agreement was **valid** — not a voting trust, not an evasion, and a reasonable
deadlock-breaking measure. But it was **unenforceable in the way the plaintiff needed**, because
nothing in it empowered the arbitrator to vote the shares or either party to vote the other's.
"[E]ach party promised the other to exercise **her own** voting rights in accordance with the
arbitrator's decision."

So Mrs. Haley breached by casting 1,103 and 1,102 for herself and her husband instead of the
directed 882/882/441, and partial compliance was no defence: the direction was "part of a single
plan or course of action... calculated to utilize an advantage of joint action," and voting for
two of the three candidates "frustrates that plan" rather than partly performing it. The remedy
was rejection of her votes, a six-member board, and one seat left vacant.

## Best counter-case

[[Hewlett v. Hewlett-Packard]] marks where the freedom ends.

The distinguishing line: **Ringling is stockholders binding their own votes to each other;
Hewlett is management spending the stockholders' money to change how the vote comes out.**

Deutsche Bank's proxy committee independently decided to vote 25 million shares against the
HP–Compaq merger and did so on March 15 — the same day HP closed a credit facility adding
Deutsche Bank as co-arranger. Three days later the bank feared for its future HP business; on the
morning of the vote a telephone conference was held, allegedly at management's demand; and
Deutsche Bank switched as many as 17 million votes. Fiorina delayed the meeting's opening and
closed the polls promptly on word of the switch.

Chancellor Chandler denied dismissal. The merger was "one of the limited types of transactions a
corporate board **cannot unilaterally cause its corporation to consummate**," and management
"purportedly used the shareholders' own money... to buy votes in opposition to HP shareholders who
did not favor the merger," which "made it proportionally more difficult for shareholders opposing
the merger to defeat the transaction." No ratifying vote of independent stockholders was used.

HP's objection that only "future business" was promised gets a one-sentence answer: "[t]hat future
business with Deutsche Bank would necessarily have to be paid for with corporate funds."

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Confusing a voting trust with a pooling agreement.** A trust transfers the stock and must
   meet § 218(a)'s filing, certificate and ledger requirements; a pool needs only a signed writing
   under § 218(c).
2. **Assuming validity means enforceability.** Ringling's agreement was valid and still could not
   deliver the fifth director, because nobody held the power to cast the breaching party's shares.
3. **Forgetting the irrevocable proxy.** Section 212(e) is the drafting answer, and the interest may
   be in the corporation generally rather than in the stock.
4. **Stating the vote-buying rule as flat prohibition.** Per se illegal only for deleterious
   purposes; otherwise voidable and tested for intrinsic fairness — and § 218(c) affirmatively
   validates voting agreements.
5. **Missing the who-is-buying distinction.** A stockholder sells his own asset; management spends
   everyone's. That single line reconciles Ringling, § 218(c) and Hewlett.
6. **Requiring a contract, or a majority.** Hewlett rejects both. What matters is consideration
   personal to the stockholder and shares actually voted as directed, and less than a majority can
   decide an election.
7. **Overlooking the cleansing device.** A ratifying vote of disinterested stockholders is what
   saved the arrangement in Schreiber and what was missing in Hewlett.
8. **Reciting the liberality passage without its limit.** "[S]o long as he violates no duty owed his
   fellow shareholders" is the clause that does the work.
