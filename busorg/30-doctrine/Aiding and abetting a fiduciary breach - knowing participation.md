## Rule statement

A non-fiduciary is liable for aiding and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty where
it **knowingly participates** in the breach. Knowing participation has two halves,
and a plaintiff must prove both.

- **Knowledge (scienter)** — the third party must know that the fiduciary's conduct
  is a breach **and** that its own conduct is legally improper.
- **Participation** — the third party must provide **substantial assistance**, which
  means active conduct rather than passive awareness.

The predicate breach may be an **exculpated** one. Section 102(b)(7) removes the
directors' monetary liability for a care breach; it does not erase the breach, and
it does not reach third parties.

## Elements

### The four Malpiede elements

1. The existence of a **fiduciary relationship**;
2. a **breach** of the fiduciary's duty;
3. **knowing participation** in that breach by the defendant; and
4. **damages proximately caused** by the breach.

Element three is where every case is decided.

### Knowledge — two distinct requirements

| | Source | What must be shown |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge of the primary breach | Malpiede | The third party "act[ed] with the knowledge that the conduct advocated or assisted constitutes such a breach" |
| Knowledge of its own impropriety | RBC | "[A]ctual or constructive knowledge that their conduct was legally improper" |

The mental state is "knowingly, intentionally, or with reckless indifference," an
"illicit state of mind." **It is the aider and abettor that must act with
scienter**, and whether it did is a **factual** determination.

The second requirement is the one that decides close cases. In
[[In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation]] the buyer's internal scrubbing
showed it knew its **sale-process** conduct "was not above suspicion" — and said
nothing about whether it knew its **proxy silence** was improper, which was the
conduct actually charged. Match the knowledge to the conduct pleaded.

### Participation — substantial assistance under Restatement § 876(b)

Section 876(b) imposes liability on one who "knows that the other's conduct
constitutes a breach of duty and gives **substantial assistance or encouragement**
to the other so to conduct himself."

The comment d factors, as restated in Dole and adopted by the Delaware Supreme
Court in Mindbody:

- **The nature of the act** participated in or encouraged, including its severity,
  the clarity of the violation, the extent of the consequences, and the secondary
  actor's knowledge of these aspects;
- **The amount, kind and duration of assistance** given, including how directly
  involved the secondary actor was in the primary actor's conduct;
- **The nature of the relationship** between the secondary and primary actors; and
- **The secondary actor's state of mind.**

**Active conduct is required.** Delaware corporate-governance cases have found
liability "only where there has been overt participation such as active attempts to
create or exploit conflicts of interest in the board or an overt conspiracy or
agreement between the buyer and the board." "[P]assive awareness... does not
constitute 'substantial assistance.'" A failure to act supports liability only
where the third party owed the plaintiff an **independent duty**.

### Who the defendant is changes the difficulty

| Defendant | Posture |
|---|---|
| **Arm's-length acquiror** | Participation "should be the most difficult to prove." Delaware protects arm's-length bargaining, and "a bidder's attempts to reduce the sale price through arm's-length negotiations cannot give rise to liability" |
| **Financial advisor to the board** | The advisor has an informational advantage over its own conflicts and "is under an obligation not to act in a manner that is contrary to the interests of the board of directors, thereby undermining the very advice that it knows the directors will be relying upon" |

Two routes to liability survive against **any** third party, including a buyer:

- it "attempts to **create or exploit conflicts of interest** in the board"; or
- "the bidder and the board **conspire in or agree to** the fiduciary breach."

### The RBC route — fraud on the board

"[I]f the third party knows that the board is breaching its duty of care and
participates in the breach by **misleading the board or creating the informational
vacuum**, then the third party can be liable for aiding and abetting." Affirmed as
a **narrow** holding, and expressly not extended to "any failure on the part of a
financial advisor to prevent directors from breaching their duty of care."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Burden | On the plaintiff, on every element |
| Mental state | Scienter — never mere negligence, and never gross negligence |
| Difficulty | Scienter "makes an aiding and abetting claim among the most difficult to prove" |
| Nature of the scienter finding | Factual; reviewed for clear error |
| Nature of the participation ruling | Legal conclusion drawn from the facts; reviewed **de novo** |
| Effect of a § 102(b)(7) charter | Shields the directors from damages; leaves the breach intact as a predicate; does not reach the third party |
| Contribution from exculpated directors | Unavailable — the third party bears the burden of showing the directors would not have been exculpated and would have shared a common liability |

The de novo point is not a technicality. In Mindbody the Supreme Court left every
fact-finding undisturbed and reversed anyway, because participation is a conclusion
about what those facts add up to.

## Exceptions

- **No independent fiduciary duty arises from contract.** A merger agreement
  covenant obliging the buyer to notify the target of material omissions gives the
  target a **breach of contract** claim. It does not create a duty of disclosure
  running to the target's stockholders, and it does not convert silence into
  participation. The contrary rule "would collapse the arms'-length distance between
  the third-party buyer and the target" and would force the buyer to "second-guess
  the materiality determinations and legal judgment of the target's board."
- **Arm's-length price negotiation is protected.** Pushing the price down is not
  assistance in a breach.
- **Gross negligence by an advisor is not enough.** That is the protection advisors
  enjoy which directors do not, and it is the court's answer to the charge that
  exculpation shifts liability unfairly.
- **§ 144(d)(6)(c) preserves the claim post-SB 21.** Nothing in the controller safe
  harbours limits "the right of any person to seek relief on the grounds that a
  stockholder or other person knowingly aided and abetted a breach of fiduciary duty
  **by one or more of the directors** of the corporation." Read the last clause
  closely — the savings clause is written around director breaches. See
  [[Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)]].
- **Advisors are not gatekeepers.** The Supreme Court in RBC expressly declined to
  adopt the trial court's gatekeeper language, even as dictum: an advisor's role "is
  primarily contractual in nature, is typically negotiated between sophisticated
  parties, and can vary."

## Leading case

[[RBC Capital Markets v. Jervis]]. RBC ran Rural/Metro's sale process while
planning to use that engagement to win buy-side financing mandates on a competitor's
simultaneous sale, favouring the bidders who were pursuing both. It never disclosed
that interest, conducted back-channel communications with a bidder, modified its
valuation analysis and delivered the modified version on the day of the board vote,
and pressed for financing from the winning bidder while leading the price
negotiations.

The directors breached the duty of care and paid nothing, being exculpated and
lacking scienter. RBC owed no fiduciary duty at all and paid, because it knew
exactly what it was doing — "[t]he manifest intentionality of RBC's conduct" shown
by the bankers' own internal communications.

The holding on the shield is the durable part: "[t]he literal language of Section
102(b)(7) only covers directors; it does not extend to aiders and abettors."
Delaware's legislature did not intend to create "a perverse incentive system wherein
trusted advisors to directors could, for their own selfish motives, intentionally
mislead a board only to hide behind their victim's liability shield."

## Best counter-case

[[In re Mindbody, Inc., Stockholder Litigation]], which reversed a finding of
liability on facts that look superficially similar and is the better teaching case
for where the line sits.

The distinguishing line: **RBC created the informational vacuum; Vista walked past
one that did not exist.** "In that case, RBC knew all of the relevant information
and the board knew none of it." In Mindbody, "Stollmeyer knew everything that Vista
knew," so Vista "did not create an informational vacuum, or purposely mislead
Stollmeyer, or proximately cause his disclosure breach" — it "passively stood by."

Two further contrasts do independent work. **Role**: RBC was the board's own
advisor, Vista an arm's-length acquiror, and participation is hardest to prove
against the latter. **Fit between knowledge and charge**: RBC's scienter went to the
very conduct charged, while Vista's went to its sale-process behaviour rather than
to the proxy silence pleaded against it.

The pleading lesson is the loudest one. Plaintiffs waived the claim that Vista aided
and abetted the **Revlon** breach, and that is the claim the facts fit — Vista took a
price tip and a process tip, sprinted on a head start it knew it had been handed,
and scrubbed its own file about it, which is the classic "exploit conflicts of
interest in the board" theory. A choice of theory, not a change in doctrine,
produced the reversal.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Asking only whether the third party knew of the breach.** Scienter has two
   halves, and the second — knowledge that its **own** conduct was legally improper
   — is what Mindbody turns on.
2. **Pleading knowledge of the wrong conduct.** Match the scienter evidence to the
   breach charged. Awareness that one's negotiating behaviour was questionable does
   not establish knowledge that one's silence about a proxy was improper.
3. **Treating silence as participation.** Substantial assistance means active
   conduct. Passive awareness is not enough absent an independent duty to the
   plaintiff.
4. **Turning a merger agreement covenant into a fiduciary duty.** It creates a
   contract claim for the counterparty, not a disclosure duty to stockholders.
5. **Assuming an exculpated breach cannot be a predicate.** Section 102(b)(7)
   removes the remedy against directors, not the breach, so element two is satisfied
   by a care breach nobody pays for.
6. **Extending § 102(b)(7) to the advisor, or seeking contribution from exculpated
   directors.** The statute covers directors by its terms, and the third party bears
   the burden on common liability.
7. **Calling the advisor a gatekeeper.** The trial court in RBC did; the Supreme
   Court refused the label while affirming the judgment.
8. **Applying a negligence standard.** Gross negligence by an advisor is never
   enough. Only scienter is, which is why these claims are among the hardest to
   prove.
9. **Forgetting that the buyer's status matters.** Against an arm's-length acquiror
   participation is the most difficult element in Delaware law, and the plaintiff
   needs the create-or-exploit-conflicts route or a conspiracy.
