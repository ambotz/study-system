## Rule statement

A disclosure-only class settlement will be approved only where the supplemental
disclosures address a **plainly material** misrepresentation or omission and the
release is **narrowly circumscribed** to disclosure and sale-process claims that
have been sufficiently investigated. Supplemental information that merely adds
detail to an already fair summary is not consideration for a release.

Disclosure claims in deal litigation "optimally should be adjudicated outside of
the context of a proposed settlement... in an adversarial process."

## Elements

### The court's task under Rule 23

- The Court of Chancery must **independently** examine the fairness of a class
  settlement, because the settlement binds absent class members. "Approval of a
  class action settlement requires more than a cursory scrutiny."
- The court evaluates the claim, the available defences, the obstacles to
  successful prosecution, and "the reasonableness of the 'give' and the 'get'" —
  what the class surrenders against what the class receives.
- Delaware's general preference for voluntary settlement is displaced by the
  fiduciary character of a class action. Unanimity among the parties at the
  fairness hearing is a symptom of the problem, not evidence of fairness.

### Materiality — the Delaware standard, borrowed from the federal securities laws

Information is material if there is a **substantial likelihood that a reasonable
stockholder would consider it important in deciding how to vote**, such that it
would significantly alter the **total mix** of information made available.

**Plainly material** is the higher settlement-approval threshold: it should not be
a close call that the information is material.

### The fair summary rule

Where a board relies on a financial advisor's advice in recommending stockholder
action, stockholders are entitled to "a fair summary of the substantive work
performed by the investment bankers."

- A fair summary is **a summary**. It need not contain everything underlying the
  opinion or in the advisor's report to the board.
- It need not supply enough data for stockholders to run their own valuation.
- "The essence of a fair summary is not a cornucopia of financial data, but rather
  an accurate description of the advisor's **methodology and key assumptions**."
- Disclosures supplying extraneous detail do not contribute to a fair summary and
  add no value.

### The two adversarial channels

Both work by restoring an opponent to the proceeding.

| Channel | Who bears what | Why the adversary survives |
|---|---|---|
| **Preliminary injunction motion** | Plaintiff must show a reasonable likelihood of proving the omission or misrepresentation is material | Defendants oppose on the merits; no release is on the table |
| **Mootness fee application** | Plaintiff applies for fees after defendants voluntarily supplement and moot the claim | Defendants are incentivised to oppose a fee they view as excessive |

Where a settlement is pursued anyway and the information is not plainly material,
the court may appoint an **amicus curiae** to supply the missing adversary, as
Professor Griffith was permitted to do in Trulia.

### Surviving fiduciary claims

Claims challenging the sale process that remain in the case are tested at the
pleading stage with **special care**, because "the risk of strike suits means that
too much turns on the mere survival of the complaint," and because thirty years of
Unocal and Revlon jurisprudence now equips courts to sort them.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Procedural vehicle | Court of Chancery Rule 23 settlement approval |
| Who decides | The court, exercising **independent judgment**, not the parties |
| Structural problem | The hearing is **non-adversarial**; both sides of the caption want approval |
| Test | Reasonableness of the give and the get |
| Threshold for approval | Plainly material disclosure **and** a narrowly circumscribed release |
| Consequence of failure | Approval denied; the claims and the release both survive unresolved |

## Exceptions

- **Meritorious deal litigation is unaffected.** Trulia expressly notes that such
  litigation "has generated meaningful economic benefits for stockholders" where
  a sale process was corrupted by conflicts. [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare]] is the
  same docket working properly.
- **Monetary settlements are not disclosure settlements.** The give-and-get
  analysis is about a release purchased with information of no value.
- **Forum shopping has an answer.** A Delaware corporation may enact a **forum
  selection bylaw** to keep the litigation at home.
- **The claims did not disappear; they relocated.** After Trulia the practice
  migrated to mootness fee applications and to federal court under § 14(a), which
  is the second channel the opinion itself identified as acceptable.

## Leading case

[[In re Trulia]]. Four supplemental disclosures, all concerning J.P. Morgan's
financial analysis — synergy figures, comparable transaction multiples, public
trading multiples, and implied terminal EBITDA multiples. The proxy already ran
224 pages and summarised the banker's opinion in ten single-spaced pages.

The record was thin enough to show what the process had become: no motions
decided, not even a motion to expedite; fewer than 3,000 pages produced; three
depositions, one of them confirmatory. The injunction brief pleaded price, process
and preclusive terms but argued only disclosure, and the release would have
extinguished everything. The only money changing hands was a $375,000 fee that
defendants agreed not to oppose. Approval denied.

The incentive diagnosis is the durable part. Plaintiffs' leverage is the threat of
an injunction; defendants buy closing certainty and deal insurance; supplemental
disclosure is a costless currency; many defendants self-expedite by volunteering
core documents, bypassing "the only gating mechanism... the Court has to screen out
frivolous cases"; and once the memorandum of understanding is signed, "the
litigation takes on an entirely different, non-adversarial character."

## Best counter-case

**Rural/Metro**, described within Trulia itself, is the case that proves the stakes
run the other way as well. The Court of Chancery called it a "very close call" to
reject a disclosure settlement whose release would have extinguished claims that
later produced over $100 million for stockholders, most of it from a post-trial
judgment after new counsel took over. The same litigation is assigned in session 16
as [[RBC Capital Markets v. Jervis]].

The distinguishing line: **the harm from a disclosure settlement is not only that
the class gets nothing, but that the release extinguishes claims nobody
investigated.** A settlement whose "get" is worthless and whose "give" is unbounded
fails on both halves of the test, and Rural/Metro shows the second half is where
the real money is.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Reciting materiality without the "plainly" qualifier.** The settlement
   threshold is deliberately higher than materiality itself, because the court is
   purchasing a class-wide release with it.
2. **Claiming stockholders are entitled to the banker's full work product.** A
   fair summary is methodology and key assumptions, expressly not enough data for
   an independent valuation.
3. **Reading Trulia as hostility to deal litigation.** The target is the
   settlement mechanism, not the claims. The opinion reroutes disclosure claims to
   two adversarial channels rather than extinguishing them.
4. **Forgetting that the fairness hearing has no adversary.** Every structural move
   in the opinion — amicus appointment, the injunction channel, the mootness fee
   channel — is an attempt to supply one.
5. **Treating the release as an afterthought.** The narrowness of the release is an
   independent condition of approval, and Rural/Metro is the reason.
6. **Missing the connection to cleansing.** The same materiality standard decides
   whether a vote was "fully informed" for
   [[Cleansing by stockholder vote - Corwin and the fully informed vote]] and
   whether the minority vote was informed under
   [[Controller buyouts - the MFW dual protection structure]]. One analysis, three
   uses.
