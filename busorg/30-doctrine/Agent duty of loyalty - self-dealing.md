## Rule statement

An agent must place the principal's interests above the agent's own. Once it is
shown that the agent held an interest in the transaction **antagonistic** to the
principal's, **fraud is presumed**, and the burden shifts to the agent to prove
that the principal had full knowledge of the agent's interest and of every
material fact known to the agent, and freely consented with that knowledge.

The transaction is then voidable at the principal's election. The fairness of the
bargain is not a defence.

## Elements

**1. An agency relationship.**

**2. An interest in the transaction adverse to the principal's.**

Ownership, compensation, or exposure on the other side of the deal. In
[[Detroit Lions v. Argovitz|Argovitz]] the agent held 29% of the counterparty, drew
a $275,000 salary from it, took 5% of its cash flow, and carried personal liability
on 29% of its letter of credit.

**3. Failure to carry the shifted burden.** The agent must show **both**:

- the principal knew the agent was interested; **and**
- the principal knew **every material fact known to the agent** that might affect
  the principal's rights or interests or influence the action taken.

Disclosing the bare existence of a conflict is not enough. The agent "must inform
the principal of all facts that come to his knowledge that are or may be material
or which might affect his principal's rights or interests or influence the action
he takes."

**What does not satisfy the burden**

- Disclosure by someone other than the agent, of the conflict's existence only.
- The principal's instruction to forgo a step, where the instruction rests on
  information the agent himself distorted.
- A waiver signed without independent advice and before the principal learned the
  withheld facts.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Agent's adverse interest, shown |
| Consequence of the trigger | Fraud presumed |
| Burden after the trigger | Agent |
| What the agent must prove | Full disclosure of every material fact **and** free consent |
| Remedy if the burden fails | Rescission, at the principal's election |
| Not examined | Whether the deal was fair or the price adequate |

## Exceptions

- **Consent after full disclosure.** Business law does not forbid a fiduciary from
  acting under a conflict; it demands a higher standard of conduct and more
  process. A conflict fully disclosed, to a principal advised independently and
  free to refuse, is curable.
- **Ratification and waiver**, but only with knowledge of the facts ratified or
  waived. Both failed in Argovitz for want of that knowledge.

## Leading case

[[Detroit Lions v. Argovitz]] — an agent negotiated his client's contract with a
football club he partly owned, never called the competing bidder for a final offer,
and never sought for his client the contract terms an independent agent had
extracted from the same club for a lesser player.

## Best counter-case

No assigned case comes out the other way on these facts. The instructive contrast
is the corporate line that develops the same architecture with **cleansing
mechanisms** attached — 8 Del. C. § 144, [[Sinclair Oil v. Levien]], and
[[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide]].

The distinguishing line: **Argovitz offers a fiduciary only one way out —
disclosure plus free consent — while Delaware corporate law adds disinterested
director approval, informed stockholder approval, and the post-SB 21 statutory safe
harbours.** A fact pattern that supplies a cleansing device is a corporate problem;
one that supplies only silence is this problem.

## Common trap

1. **Arguing the deal was good for the principal.** *Burleson*, quoted in Argovitz:
   the question "does not relate to the mala fides of the agent nor to whether or
   not a greater sum might have been procured." The inquiry stops once the adverse
   interest exists.
2. **Treating disclosure of the conflict as disclosure.** The standard is every
   material fact, not the headline. Naming the gap between "he knew I had a
   franchise" and "he knew my salary, my equity, my cash-flow interest, and my
   letter of credit" is where the points are.
3. **Accepting the principal's consent at face value.** Consent given on information
   the agent shaped is not free consent.
4. **Forgetting the burden shifts.** Writing that the plaintiff must prove unfair
   dealing inverts the rule. Once the adverse interest is shown, the fiduciary
   carries the burden.
5. **Missing the second breach.** In Argovitz the failure to seek terms the agent
   had given another client was an independent breach, separate from the conflict
   itself.
