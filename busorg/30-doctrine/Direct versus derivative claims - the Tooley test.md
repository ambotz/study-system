## Rule statement

Classification rests on two questions and nothing else:

1. **Who suffered the alleged harm** — the corporation, or the suing stockholder
   individually?
2. **Who would receive the benefit** of the recovery or other remedy?

The stockholder's claimed direct injury "must be **independent** of any alleged injury to
the corporation," and the stockholder must show the duty breached was owed to the
stockholder and that he or she "can prevail **without showing an injury to the
corporation**."

**"Special injury" is disavowed.**

## Elements

**1. Prong one — whose harm.** Operationalised as: "[l]ooking at the body of the complaint
and considering the nature of the wrong alleged and the relief requested, has the plaintiff
demonstrated that he or she can prevail without showing an injury to the corporation?"

**2. Prong two — whose recovery.** It "should logically follow." Derivative recovery goes
to the corporation; direct recovery flows to the stockholders.

**3. What the test is not.**

- **Not about how many stockholders were harmed.** A wrong shared by every stockholder can
  still be direct. That is what abolishing "special injury" accomplishes.
- **Not about whether the defendant owed fiduciary duties.** Both kinds of claim can rest on
  fiduciary breach.

**4. The consequences that make classification matter.** If derivative, Rule 23.1 requires
the stockholder to:

- **retain ownership** of the shares throughout the litigation;
- **make presuit demand** on the board, or plead futility — see
  [[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test]]; and
- obtain **court approval of any settlement**.

And recovery "flows only to the corporation." A direct plaintiff faces none of this.

**5. The merger pattern.** A merger extinguishes derivative standing, because continuous
ownership fails. Where the merger was itself the vehicle for extinguishing a claim against
the approving directors, [[In re Riverstone National]] reclassifies: the lost claim is a
corporate asset and its loss is corporate harm, but **the failure to price it into the merger
consideration is a direct harm to the former stockholders**. To plead it a plaintiff must
allege the claim existed, would have **survived a motion to dismiss**, was threatened and
known to the board, and was **material** to the directors.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Test | Two questions: whose harm, whose recovery |
| Required showing for a direct claim | Injury independent of corporate injury, **and** duty owed to the stockholder |
| Abolished | "Special injury" |
| If derivative | Rule 23.1 — continuous ownership, demand or futility, court-approved settlement |
| Recovery | Corporation (derivative) or stockholders (direct) |
| Why it matters | Classification "may be outcome-determinative" |

## Exceptions

- **Extinguishment by merger** converts what is left into a direct fairness claim on the
  Riverstone conditions.
- **Contractual stockholder rights** — voting, or rights existing independently of any
  corporate right — remain the clearest direct claims, even though the "special injury"
  label describing them is gone.

## Leading case

[[Tooley v. Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette]] — two questions, "special injury" disavowed, and
the standard must be "clear, simple and consistently articulated" precisely because
classification decides cases.

## Best counter-case

[[In re Riverstone National]] as the hard application rather than a conflict.

The distinguishing line: **ask what the plaintiff must prove to win.** If proving the case
requires showing the corporation was hurt, it is derivative. In Riverstone the plaintiffs had
to show the **price they personally received** was too low — a claim they could win without
proving corporate injury, because the corporation no longer existed independently of the deal.

## Common trap

1. **Asking whether this stockholder was hurt differently from the others.** That is "special
   injury," and Tooley abolished it.
2. **Stopping at prong one.** Both questions must be answered, and the remedy question
   sometimes exposes a mischaracterisation the harm question missed.
3. **Forgetting Rule 23.1's other two requirements.** Demand gets all the attention, but
   continuous ownership and court approval of settlement are equally part of the derivative
   package — and continuous ownership is what the merger cases turn on.
4. **Treating a merger as ending the matter.** It ends the derivative claim. Whether anything
   survives depends on Riverstone.
5. **Conflating classification with merit.** A direct claim can be weak and a derivative claim
   strong. Tooley decides only who may sue and who collects.
