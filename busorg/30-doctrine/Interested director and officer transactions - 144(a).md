## Rule statement

**The common-law default**, stated in [[Bayer v. Beran]]: the business judgment rule
"yields to the rule of undivided loyalty." A conflicted transaction loses the
presumption, is examined with "the most scrupulous care," and the burden falls on the
fiduciary "not only to prove the good faith of the transaction but also to show its
inherent fairness."

**§ 144 is the statutory overlay** supplying routes out of that default. What the
routes *do* is the single biggest thing SB 21 changed.

| | Pre-SB 21 | Post-SB 21 |
|---|---|---|
| What compliance buys | The transaction is not "**void or voidable** solely for this reason" | The transaction "**may not be the subject of equitable relief, or give rise to an award of damages**, against a director or officer" |
| Nature | An anti-voidability provision | A genuine safe harbour against liability and injunction |

That is a change in kind. The old statute told you the contract stood; it did not
tell you the directors were safe. The new one does.

## Elements

### The three routes — and what changed in each

**Route 1 — disinterested director approval. § 144(a)(1).**

| | Pre | Post |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure to | "the board of directors or the committee" | "**all members** of the board of directors or a committee" |
| Quality of approval | "in good faith" | "in good faith **and without gross negligence**" |
| Who approves | "a majority of the disinterested directors, even though the disinterested directors be less than a quorum" | same, but "**then serving**" |
| If the board is mostly interested | *Unresolved — the central fight* | **Proviso**: the act "shall be approved (or recommended for approval) by a committee of the board of directors that consists of 2 or more directors, each of whom the board of directors has determined to be a disinterested director" |

**The pre-SB 21 fight the proviso settles.** The casebook poses it directly. Delaware
**common law** gave a safe harbour only where a **majority of the board** was
disinterested; it was not enough for all disinterested directors to approve if they
were fewer than half the board. Some cases — *Benihana of Tokyo* among them — read
§ 144(a)(1) as an **independent** statutory safe harbour that worked even where most
of the board was interested, since the text says "even though the disinterested
directors be less than a quorum." Most courts rejected that reading and left only the
common-law route.

Work the casebook's own hypothetical and the stakes are obvious:

- **A.** Directors A and B interested; C, D and E disinterested. Majority of the board
  is disinterested → safe harbour under either reading.
- **B.** Directors A, B and C interested; D and E disinterested. Majority is
  **interested** → safe harbour on the statutory reading, none on the common-law
  reading.

Post-SB 21 answers scenario B: the route stays open, but only through a **committee of
two or more determined-disinterested directors**. Delaware chose the statutory reading
and then supplied the procedural protection the common law was worried about.

**Route 2 — stockholder approval. § 144(a)(2).**

| Pre | Post |
|---|---|
| "specifically approved in good faith by **vote of the stockholders**" | "approved or ratified by an **informed, uncoerced, affirmative vote of a majority of the votes cast by the disinterested stockholders**" |

Four changes in one sentence: the vote must be **informed**, must be **uncoerced**,
counts only **disinterested** stockholders, and is measured by **votes cast** rather
than shares outstanding. The interested party's own block no longer carries the vote.

**Route 3 — fairness. § 144(a)(3).**

| Pre | Post |
|---|---|
| "**fair as to the corporation** as of the time it is authorized, approved or ratified" | "fair as to the corporation **and the corporation's stockholders**" |

The timing language drops out and stockholders are added as a distinct object of
fairness.

### Who counts as disinterested — now defined by statute

Pre-SB 21 these were case-law questions. Post-SB 21 they are definitions:

- **Disinterested director**, § 144(e)(4) — "not a party to the act or transaction and
  does not have a material interest in the act or transaction or a material
  relationship with a person that has a material interest."
- **Material interest**, § 144(e)(7) — a benefit "other than one which would devolve on
  the corporation or the stockholders generally" that "would reasonably be expected to
  impair the objectivity of the director's judgment."
- **Material relationship**, § 144(e)(8) — "a **familial**, financial, professional,
  employment, or other relationship" meeting the same impairment test. *This is
  [[Bayer v. Beran]] codified: the president's wife is a material relationship.*
- **Disinterested stockholder**, § 144(e)(5) — no material interest in the transaction
  and no material relationship with the controller or other interested person.

### Two thumbs on the scale for the defence, § 144(d)(2)–(3)

- A director of a **listed** company whom the board has determined satisfies the
  exchange's independence criteria is **presumed disinterested**, and that presumption
  "shall be heightened and may only be rebutted by **substantial and particularized
  facts**" of a material interest or relationship.
- Being **designated, nominated or voted in** by a person with a material interest
  "shall not, of itself, be evidence that a director is not a disinterested director."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Default, no safe harbour | Entire fairness; burden on the fiduciary ([[Bayer v. Beran]]) |
| Pre-SB 21 compliance | Transaction not void or voidable; fiduciary review **not** foreclosed |
| Post-SB 21 compliance | No damages and no equitable relief against directors or officers |
| Scope, post | "act or transaction," including subsidiaries, and including mere involvement "in the initiation, negotiation, or approval" |
| Quorum | Interested directors still count — § 144(d)(1), formerly § 144(b) |

## Exceptions

- **Controlling stockholder transactions are carved out of § 144(a)** and routed to
  § 144(b) and (c), which are new in SB 21. Those subsections are assigned separately
  with [[In re Tesla Motors (Tesla 2)]] and get their own module.
- **§ 144(d)(6) savings clauses.** Nothing in (a), (b) or (c) limits: the right to seek
  equitable relief that a transaction "was not authorized or approved in compliance
  with the procedures set forth in this chapter," with the charter or bylaws, or with a
  governmental order; **judicial review for injunctive relief of anti-takeover
  devices** "designed or intended to deter, delay, or preclude a change of control";
  or claims that a person "knowingly **aided and abetted** a breach of fiduciary duty."
- **§ 144(d)(5)** limits a controlling stockholder's monetary liability to breaches of
  loyalty, acts not in good faith or involving intentional misconduct or knowing
  violation of law, and transactions producing an improper personal benefit — a
  controller-level analogue of
  [[Charter exculpation of director liability|§ 102(b)(7)]], but statutory rather than
  charter-adopted.
- **§ 144(d)(7)** deems shares irrevocably accepted in a § 251(h) tender offer voted in
  favour, and disinterested shares not tendered voted against, for the (a)(2), (b)(2)
  and (c)(1) counts.

## Leading case

[[Bayer v. Beran]] for the common-law default and what surviving rigorous scrutiny
looks like — market compensation, arm's-length negotiation, no undue prominence,
legitimate corporate purpose, full benefit received.

## Best counter-case

[[In re Investors Bancorp]], assigned the same day, as the transaction that never
reaches a safe harbour at all.

The distinguishing line: **a safe harbour protects what the approving body actually
approved.** Bayer's directors defended the transaction on its merits and won.
Investors Bancorp's directors pointed to a 96.25% stockholder vote that had approved
parameters rather than awards, and the defence was unavailable. Under post-SB 21
§ 144(a)(2) the same problem appears in statutory clothing — a vote ratifies "the act
or transaction," not a category of future acts.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Saying § 144 compliance means the business judgment rule applies — pre-SB 21.**
   It does not. The old statute says only that the transaction is not void or voidable
   "solely for this reason." Which version of the statute governs the fact pattern is
   the first thing to establish, and the answer changes the remedy, not just the
   analysis.
2. **Missing the committee proviso.** Post-SB 21, where a majority of the board is
   interested, a majority of the disinterested directors is **not enough** — it must
   go to a committee of two or more determined-disinterested directors. This is the
   single most likely fine-grained question on the amendment.
3. **Counting the wrong stockholders.** Post-SB 21 the vote is a majority of **votes
   cast** by **disinterested** stockholders. Counting outstanding shares, or counting
   the interested holder's block, gets the wrong answer on facts designed to turn on it.
4. **Treating "disinterested" as intuitive.** There are statutory definitions now, and
   they have two independent branches — material **interest** and material
   **relationship**. A director with no financial stake can still be interested through
   a familial or professional relationship.
5. **Forgetting the listed-company presumption.** On facts involving a public company
   and an exchange-independent director, the defence starts ahead, and the presumption
   is rebuttable only by "substantial and particularized facts."
6. **Routing a controller deal through § 144(a).** Subsection (a) expressly excepts
   controlling stockholder transactions. Identify whether a controller is on the other
   side before choosing the subsection.
7. **Assuming the safe harbour ends the case.** § 144(d)(6) preserves procedural
   challenges, injunctive review of anti-takeover devices, and aiding-and-abetting
   claims against third parties.
