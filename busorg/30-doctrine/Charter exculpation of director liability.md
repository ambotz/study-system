## Rule statement

A Delaware certificate of incorporation may contain a provision eliminating or
limiting a director's personal liability to the corporation or its stockholders
for **monetary damages** for breach of fiduciary duty as a director, subject to
four carve-outs. Virtually every Delaware corporation has adopted such a
provision.

The practical effect is that a duty of care claim seeking damages is no longer
viable standing alone. Section 102(b)(7) was the Delaware General Assembly's
direct answer to [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]], adopted quickly after that decision
alarmed directors and their insurers.

## Elements

**1. A provision in the certificate of incorporation.**

The charter, not the bylaws, and not a board resolution. A corporation without the
provision has no exculpation, so the first move on any fact pattern is to check
whether the charter contains one.

**2. Eliminating or limiting liability of a director.**

The statute extends by its terms to "such other person or persons, if any, who,
pursuant to a provision of the certificate of incorporation in accordance with
§ 141(a) of this title, exercise or perform any of the powers or duties otherwise
conferred or imposed upon the board of directors."

**3. For monetary damages only.**

Section 102(b)(7) addresses director liability for monetary damages and therefore
has **no effect on injunctive relief**. A care-based challenge seeking to enjoin a
transaction is unaffected by the provision.

**4. Not reaching any of four carve-outs.** Liability survives for:

- Any breach of the director's **duty of loyalty** to the corporation or its
  stockholders.
- Acts or omissions **not in good faith**, or which involve intentional misconduct
  or a knowing violation of law.
- Liability under **§ 174** (unlawful dividends and unlawful stock purchases or
  redemptions).
- Any transaction from which the director **derived an improper personal benefit**.

**5. Prospective only.**

No such provision eliminates or limits liability for any act or omission occurring
before the provision became effective.

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| What the provision does | Eliminates monetary liability, rather than insuring against it |
| Effect on a standalone care-damages claim | Dismissed at the pleading stage |
| Burden once the provision is in play | Plaintiff, to plead around it |
| What the plaintiff must plead | Self-interest adverse to stockholders, action advancing an interested party's interest, or bad faith |
| Untouched | Injunctive relief; loyalty; bad faith; § 174; improper personal benefit |

On a motion to dismiss against an exculpated director, a plaintiff survives only
by "pleading facts supporting a rational inference that the director harbored
self-interest adverse to the stockholders' interest, acted to advance the
self-interest of an interested party from whom they could not be presumed to act
independently, or acted in bad faith"
([[In re Cornerstone Therapeutics|Cornerstone]]). Where no such facts are pleaded
against the independent directors, dismissal as to those directors is required.

## Exceptions

- **Injunctive relief is outside the provision entirely.** Section 102(b)(7)
  speaks only to monetary damages, so a plaintiff seeking to stop a transaction
  rather than to be paid is unaffected.
- **Insurance is a different mechanism.** Exculpation eliminates the liability;
  D&O insurance pays a liability that exists. The two interact economically —
  universal adoption of exculpation provisions lowered premiums, which the
  corporation usually pays — but they are not substitutes doctrinally.
- **Repleading as loyalty or bad faith is the standard response.** Plaintiffs'
  counsel frame conduct as a loyalty breach rather than a care breach precisely
  because the carve-outs define what survives. Expect the exam fact pattern to
  make that repleading available or unavailable on purpose.

## Leading case

No case construes § 102(b)(7) in the assigned reading for this session. The
provision is assigned as statutory text alongside [[Smith v. Van Gorkom]], the
decision that prompted it.

The two cases named in the casebook note are [[Malpiede v. Townson]], for the
proposition that a standalone care-damages claim is dismissed immediately where
the corporation has adopted the provision, and
[[In re Cornerstone Therapeutics|Cornerstone]], for what a plaintiff must plead to
survive against an exculpated director.

## Best counter-case

[[Smith v. Van Gorkom]] — the world before the provision, and the reason the
provision exists.

The distinguishing line: **§ 102(b)(7) changes the remedy, not the standard.** Van
Gorkom's process holding survives intact and still governs how boards must run a
sale process and still supports injunctive relief. What does not survive is the
money judgment against the outside directors. On any fact pattern, separate the
question of whether the directors breached from the question of whether the
plaintiff can collect.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Treating the provision as a defence to the breach.** Section 102(b)(7) does
   not make uninformed conduct lawful. The breach still occurred; the damages
   remedy is gone. Answers that conclude "no breach" rather than "no monetary
   liability" lose the distinction the statute is built on.
2. **Forgetting injunctive relief.** The provision reaches damages only. Where the
   facts involve a pending transaction rather than a completed one, exculpation
   may be irrelevant.
3. **Assuming the provision exists.** Adoption is near-universal in practice but
   the provision is optional and lives in the charter. A fact pattern that omits
   it has omitted it deliberately.
4. **Stopping at exculpation.** Once the care claim is exculpated, the question
   becomes whether the same facts support loyalty, bad faith, or an improper
   personal benefit. That is where the analysis is expected to go, and the
   carve-outs are a checklist for going there.
5. **Confusing exculpation with indemnification and insurance.** Exculpation
   eliminates liability, indemnification reimburses a director who incurs it, and
   insurance funds the reimbursement. Three different mechanisms answering three
   different questions.
6. **Missing the bylaws-versus-charter point.** The provision must be in the
   certificate of incorporation. A bylaw purporting to do the same thing does not.
