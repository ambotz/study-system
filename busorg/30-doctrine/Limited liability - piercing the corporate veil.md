## Rule statement

Incorporation exists to let proprietors escape personal liability, and the
privilege holds unless the plaintiff pleads that the shareholder was **conducting
the business in his individual capacity**. Courts disregard the corporate form
whenever necessary to prevent fraud or achieve equity, guided by general rules of
**agency**: whoever uses control of a corporation to further his own rather than
the corporation's business answers for the corporation's acts on respondeat
superior principles, in tort as well as contract.

Undercapitalisation and fragmentation of a business across many thinly financed
corporations do not by themselves state a claim against the shareholder.

## Elements

**1. Identify whose business was really being conducted.** Because the doctrine
runs on agency, the pleading must name the principal — and the answer determines
the defendant:

- **Enterprise entity** — the corporation is a fragment of a larger *corporate*
  combine that actually conducts the business. Only the **larger corporate entity**
  is reached. The shareholder is not.
- **Dummy corporation** — the corporation is a facade for *individual*
  stockholders carrying on the business in their personal capacities for purely
  personal rather than corporate ends. The **stockholder** is personally liable.

Pleading facts that fit the first while asking for the remedy available under the
second is the error that decides [[Walkovzky v. Carlton]].

**2. Particularised allegations of personal conduct.** The language the court
supplies: the shareholder and his associates were "actually doing business in their
individual capacities, shuttling their personal funds in and out of the corporations
without regard to formality and to suit their immediate convenience." That is a
"perversion of the privilege to do business in a corporate form."

**3. The modern Delaware overlay.** The plaintiff must plead facts supporting an
inference that the corporation "has created a sham entity designed to defraud
investors and creditors." Factors:

1. adequacy of capitalisation for the undertaking;
2. solvency;
3. observance of corporate formalities;
4. siphoning of funds by the dominant shareholder;
5. whether the company functioned as a facade for the dominant shareholder.

Piercing "generally results not from a single factor, but rather some combination
of them," and "an overall element of injustice or unfairness must always be
present, as well."

**What does not suffice**

- That corporate assets plus mandatory insurance cannot cover the claim.
- That ownership was deliberately fragmented to limit exposure.
- Undercapitalisation and intermingled assets pleaded without particularity.
- A fraud-on-the-public theory: if carrying the statutory minimum is not fraudulent
  for one owner-operator, an enterprise "does not become either illicit or
  fraudulent merely because it consists of many such corporations."

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Burden | Plaintiff, at the pleading stage, with particularity |
| Governing frame | Agency, not free-floating equity |
| Enterprise-entity theory reaches | The larger corporate combine |
| Dummy-corporation theory reaches | The individual stockholder |
| Frequency in practice | Uncommon; rarer still for large corporations |

## Exceptions

- **Statutory minimums are for the legislature.** If required insurance is
  inadequate to protect the public, "the remedy lies not with the courts but with
  the Legislature." Responsibility for conditions on the privilege of incorporation
  is committed to the legislature by constitution.
- **Internal affairs.** A corporation's internal affairs are governed almost
  exclusively by the law of the state of incorporation, which is why Delaware
  standards control most of the course even where a seminal case comes from
  elsewhere.

## Leading case

[[Walkovzky v. Carlton]] — a taxi fleet split across ten corporations of two cabs
each, all carrying the $10,000 statutory minimum, and a complaint dismissed because
it never alleged the shareholder was doing business personally.

## Best counter-case

The dissent in the same case (Keating, J.), which is the strongest form of the
opposing position and worth treating as the counter-authority.

The distinguishing line: **the majority asks whose business was being conducted;
the dissent asks whether capital was illusory or trifling compared with the
business to be done and the risks of loss.** Keating would pierce only for a
corporation vested with a public interest, undercapitalised against liabilities
certain to arise in the ordinary course, where income could have covered higher
premiums — and expressly not where finances later dwindle through competition or
bad times.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Mixing the two theories.** Enterprise entity reaches the combine; dummy
   corporation reaches the stockholder. The commonest error on
   [[Walkovzky v. Carlton]] is pleading one and demanding the other's remedy.
2. **Arguing unfairness.** The majority does not dispute that the structure exists
   to defeat recovery. It holds the remedy legislative. An answer built on the
   equities is answering the dissent.
3. **Treating undercapitalisation as sufficient.** It was alleged in Walkovzky and
   was not enough. In Delaware it is one factor among five, and an overall element
   of injustice is required on top.
4. **Forgetting the door is open.** The court says plainly that a valid cause of
   action against this defendant is possible and that the plaintiff simply has not
   pleaded one. The case is a drafting lesson, not a holding that the structure is
   lawful.
5. **Reaching for veil piercing first.** Piercing is rare. Where the facts supply a
   direct duty, a contractual guarantee, or a claim against the larger enterprise,
   those routes come first.
