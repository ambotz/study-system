## Rule statement

A stockholder may inspect a Delaware corporation's books and records on a written
demand under oath for a **proper purpose**, meaning "a purpose reasonably related
to such person's interest as a stockholder."

**SB 21 rewrote the back half of the section.** Before March 25, 2025 the statute
granted access to the stock ledger, the stockholder list, "and its other books and
records," with the limits supplied by case law — credible basis, necessary and
essential, rifled precision. After March 25, 2025 the statute defines **"books and
records" as a closed list of nine categories**, adds three express gates to the
demand, and forbids the Court of Chancery from ordering anything beyond the list
except through two narrow escape hatches.

## Elements

### The pre-SB 21 structure

**Threshold showing, by a preponderance of the evidence**, that the plaintiff:

1. is a stockholder;
2. complied with the statutory form-and-manner requirements; and
3. possesses a proper purpose.

Then, separately, that **each category of records is essential** to that purpose.

**Form and manner.** "Delaware courts require strict adherence to the section 220
inspection demand procedural requirements." Where the demanding party is not a
record holder, the demand must "[1] state the person's status as a stockholder,
[2] be accompanied by documentary evidence of beneficial ownership of the stock,
and [3] state that such documentary evidence is a true and correct copy of what it
purports to be." An attorney or agent must attach a power of attorney. The demand
goes to the registered office in Delaware or the principal place of business.

**Credible basis — the judge-made overlay.** A stockholder investigating
wrongdoing "need only show, by a preponderance of the evidence, a credible basis
from which the Court of Chancery can infer there is possible mismanagement that
would warrant further investigation." The credible basis standard "sets the lowest
possible burden of proof," and may be satisfied "through documents, logic,
testimony or otherwise," including circumstantial evidence and sufficiently
reliable hearsay. A showing sufficient to inspect "may ultimately fall well short
of demonstrating that anything wrong occurred."

**Scope.** Access runs to records "essential" to the purpose and stops at the
quantum the court deems "sufficient." The order must be "circumscribed with rifled
precision" — a **qualitative** standard applied contextually, not a quantitative
cap on how many documents a stockholder may obtain, and not a sawed-off shotgun
blast.

### The post-SB 21 structure

**§ 220(a)(1) — "books and records" is now a closed list of nine categories.**

| | Category | Time limit |
|---|---|---|
| a | Certificate of incorporation, including agreements incorporated by reference | — |
| b | Bylaws then in effect, including agreements incorporated by reference | — |
| c | Minutes of stockholder meetings and signed consents | **3 years** |
| d | Communications in writing or by electronic transmission to stockholders generally | **3 years** |
| e | Minutes of board and committee meetings, and records of board and committee action | — |
| f | Materials provided to the board or a committee **in connection with actions taken** | — |
| g | Annual financial statements | **3 years** |
| h | Any agreement entered into under § 122(18) | — |
| i | Director and officer independence questionnaires | — |

**§ 220(b)(2) — three cumulative gates.** A stockholder may inspect "only if all
of the following apply":

- a. the demand is made **in good faith** and for a proper purpose;
- b. the demand describes **with reasonable particularity** both the purpose and
  the records sought; and
- c. the records sought are **specifically related** to that purpose.

**§ 220(b)(3) — what the corporation may impose.** Reasonable restrictions on
confidentiality, use or distribution; a condition that any information in the
records "is deemed **incorporated by reference** in any complaint filed by or at
the direction of the stockholder"; and **redaction** of portions "not specifically
related to the stockholder's purpose."

**§ 220(e), (f), (g) — the scope ceiling and the two escape hatches.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **(e)** | In a § 220(c) proceeding the court **may not** order production of any records other than the (a)(1) list, except under (f) or (g) |
| **(f)** | If the corporation **does not have** the (a)(1)c., e. or g. records — or, for an exchange-listed company, the (a)(1)i. questionnaires — the court may order **functional equivalents**, only if the stockholder met (b) and only "to the extent necessary and essential to fulfill the stockholder's proper purpose" |
| **(g)** | Other specific records only if the stockholder (1) met (b), (2) made "a showing of a **compelling need**," and (3) "demonstrated **by clear and convincing evidence** that such specific records are necessary and essential to further such purpose" |
| **(h)** | The court may impose (b)(3) restrictions on anything produced under (f) or (g) |

### Before and after, side by side

| | Pre-SB 21 | Post-SB 21 |
|---|---|---|
| What is reachable | Stock ledger, list, "and its other books and records" — open-ended | The nine (a)(1) categories, full stop, subject to (f) and (g) |
| Emails, texts, personal-account materials | Reachable as books and records if essential | **Outside (a)(1)** — reachable only under (g) |
| Demand contents | Purpose stated; strict form and manner | Purpose **and records** described with reasonable particularity; good faith; specific relation |
| Credible basis | Judge-made, lowest possible burden | Not displaced for proper purpose, but (b)(2) and (g) now do the limiting work |
| Redaction | Court's discretion under (c) | Corporation's right under (b)(3) |
| Incorporation condition | Court's discretion under (c); first imposed in Yahoo! | Corporation's right under (b)(3) |
| Burden for anything beyond the list | Necessary and essential, preponderance | **Compelling need** plus **clear and convincing evidence** |

## Standard of review / burden

| | |
|---|---|
| Vehicle | Demand under oath; on refusal or no reply within **5 business days**, application to Chancery under § 220(c) |
| Jurisdiction | Court of Chancery, **exclusive** |
| Burden, stock ledger and stockholder list | On the **corporation**, to establish an improper purpose |
| Burden, all other books and records | On the **stockholder** — status, form and manner, proper purpose |
| Burden, anything outside (a)(1) post-SB 21 | On the stockholder, at **clear and convincing evidence**, plus compelling need |
| Director inspection, § 220(d) | Purpose reasonably related to the director's position; burden on the **corporation** to show an improper purpose |
| Court's remedial discretion | May "prescribe any limitations or conditions with reference to the inspection, or award such other or further relief as the Court may deem just and proper" |
| Nature of the proceeding | Summary, to be "managed expeditiously" |

## Exceptions

- **Exculpation does not cut off a proper purpose.** A § 102(b)(7) provision is not
  an impediment, because stockholders may use the information for things other than
  a lawsuit — seeking an audience with the board, a stockholder resolution, or a
  proxy fight. And § 102(b)(7) "does not authorize exculpation for **officers**,"
  so a potential officer claim survives it. See
  [[Charter exculpation of director liability]].
- **Electronic records count.** Section 224 recognises records kept in "any
  information storage device," and limiting books and records to paper "could cause
  Section 220 to become obsolete or ineffective." Records held in a personal email
  account remain corporate records: "rights of shareholders secured by § 220 cannot
  be defeated simply by having another entity hold the records." Post-SB 21 this
  material is outside (a)(1) and runs through (g).
- **Subsidiary records** are reachable where the corporation has actual possession
  and control, or could obtain them through control, subject to the two provisos in
  § 220(b)(2)b. (pre) and § 220(b)(1)b. (post).
- **§ 220(b)(4) savings clause (post-SB 21).** The section does not affect a
  stockholder's discovery rights in litigation with the corporation, nor a court's
  independent power to compel production — though for (a)(1) records demanded by a
  stockholder, the (b) requirements must still be met.
- **The tools at hand.** Section 220 is the front door to a derivative suit. The
  Delaware Supreme Court's practice of directing plaintiffs to use the "tools at
  hand" before pleading demand futility makes a § 220 demand effectively a
  precondition to a well-pleaded complaint. See
  [[Demand futility - Rule 23.1 and the Zuckerberg test]].

## Leading case

[[Amalgamated Bank v. Yahoo!]]. Amalgamated demanded records to investigate the
hiring and firing of Yahoo's chief operating officer, Henrique de Castro, whose
without-cause termination after fourteen months triggered $59.96 million in
severance.

The opinion is the clearest statement of what the pre-SB 21 section actually
required. On form and manner, brokerage statements dated three days before the
demand sufficed, because Section 220 "must be applied with some appreciation of
the practical considerations surrounding its use" and what it requires is
documentation "sufficiently proximate in time to the date of the demand as to be
consistent with and corroborate the averment." On proper purpose, the record was
"troubling" and supported a credible basis to suspect both breach of fiduciary
duty and waste, without any holding that wrongdoing occurred. On scope, email and
other electronic documents are books and records, including material in Mayer's
personal account, because "what matters is whether the record is essential and
sufficient to satisfy the stockholder's proper purpose, **not its source**."

And on conditions, Laster imposed the **Incorporation Condition** as a matter of
first impression: further production was conditioned on the entire production being
deemed incorporated by reference in any derivative complaint, so that a complaint
could not be built on cherry-picked documents. The condition does not change the
pleading standard — well-pleaded allegations are still taken as true and the
plaintiff still gets all reasonable inferences — it only stops a plaintiff from
taking a document out of context and insisting on an inference the court could not
draw if it saw the rest.

**SB 21 codified that condition at § 220(b)(3) and made it the corporation's right
rather than the court's discretion**, alongside a redaction right. Meanwhile the
personal-email holding, which was the aggressive half of Yahoo!, now sits outside
the default: that material is not in the (a)(1) list, so obtaining it requires
compelling need and clear and convincing evidence under § 220(g).

## Best counter-case

The **post-SB 21 statute itself** is the counter-authority, and the comparison is
the assignment.

The distinguishing line: **pre-SB 21, a stockholder who cleared the lowest possible
burden of proof could reach whatever was necessary and essential, including emails;
post-SB 21, clearing that burden reaches a closed list of nine categories, and
anything else requires compelling need proved by clear and convincing evidence.**

Run any fact pattern in this order. Is the record on the (a)(1) list? If yes, the
question is only whether the (b)(2) gates are met. If no, ask whether the
corporation simply lacks a listed category, which opens § 220(f) functional
equivalents at the ordinary necessary-and-essential standard, or whether the
stockholder wants something the statute never listed, which opens § 220(g) at the
much higher standard. On a Yahoo! fact pattern, board minutes and committee
materials are squarely in (a)(1)e. and f.; Mayer's personal emails are the § 220(g)
problem.

Watch for the **§ 220(f) trap** in the other direction. A corporation that keeps no
real minutes, no committee records and no annual financial statements does not
thereby escape — the missing category is precisely what triggers functional
equivalents, at the friendlier standard. Bad record-keeping widens the inspection.

## Professor gloss

## Common trap

1. **Treating credible basis as a merits standard.** It is the lowest possible
   burden of proof and a showing that satisfies it "may ultimately fall well short
   of demonstrating that anything wrong occurred." Yahoo! says so three separate
   times.
2. **Reading "rifled precision" as a document count.** It is a qualitative and
   contextual standard, not a quantitative limitation on obtaining everything
   necessary and essential.
3. **Applying the pre-SB 21 open-ended scope after March 25, 2025.** Section 220(e)
   forbids the court from ordering anything outside the (a)(1) list except through
   (f) or (g). Name the date the demand was made.
4. **Missing the two different escape hatches.** Section 220(f) is for records the
   corporation **does not have** and applies the ordinary necessary-and-essential
   standard; § 220(g) is for records the statute **never listed** and demands
   compelling need plus clear and convincing evidence.
5. **Forgetting the three-year windows.** Categories c., d. and g. reach back only
   three years from the date of the demand.
6. **Treating exculpation as a bar.** A § 102(b)(7) provision does not defeat a
   proper purpose, both because information has uses other than litigation and
   because exculpation does not reach officers.
7. **Ignoring the burden flip.** For the stock ledger and stockholder list the
   corporation must prove an improper purpose; for everything else the stockholder
   must prove a proper one. Identify which record is being sought before allocating
   the burden.
8. **Skipping the (b)(2) particularity gate.** Post-SB 21 the demand must describe
   the purpose **and the records** with reasonable particularity, and the records
   must be specifically related to the purpose. A broad category demand now fails
   at the door rather than being trimmed at the scope stage.
