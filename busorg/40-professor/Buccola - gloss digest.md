## Aiding and abetting a fiduciary breach - knowing participation

`3/high` · Common law — the Malpiede elements, scienter, and § 876(b) substantial assistance

**Baseline.** A non-fiduciary is liable for aiding and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty where it **knowingly participates** in the breach.

**Gloss.** Rural Metro is the structure: the special committee's failure is a duty of care breach, not loyalty, 102(b)(7) exculpates the directors, and Malpiede still lets stockholders pursue the adviser for aiding and abetting that care breach. Buccola presses the conceptual problem rather than reciting the elements — how does one aid and abet someone else's negligence, when the principal ordinarily has a mens rea the care breach lacks — and his conclusion is that loyalty is the easier predicate. Note the perverse incentive he identified: exculpated directors who hold stock actually benefit from a finding of breach against the adviser. MindBody draws the line on the other side: Vista knew and still escaped, because an arm's-length counterparty need not disclaim its own benefits, and its silence on the proxy was not enough. So in an adviser hypo, argue the predicate duty first, then knowing participation, and expect the counterparty's arm's-length status to be the defence.

## Sale of control - Revlon and the duty to maximise price

`3/high` · Common law — the Revlon trigger and the duty to obtain the best value reasonably available

**Baseline.** When the break-up or sale of the company becomes inevitable, the board's duty changes from **preserving the corporate entity** to **obtaining the best value reasonably available to stockholders**.

**Gloss.** Name the acquisition structure first — asset sale needs the board plus a 271 vote, merger needs the agreement plus the target's stockholders, a stock purchase needs neither, which is why Perelman went hostile. Revlon shifts the duty to maximising price once takeover is inevitable, and inevitability is contestable with a single bidder; enhanced scrutiny exists because the business judgment rule is too weak and entire fairness too exacting. Always name the duty breached, though on Revlon's facts care versus loyalty did not change the remedy. Paramount supplies the limiting principle and he named it exam material: no actual sale of the business means no Revlon trigger. Office hours gave the reason, which is the argument to make: issuing new shares to buy something produces the same capital structure as a deal, so if Time were a sale then most corporate finance would be, and there is no good limiting principle once you go there — QVC then draws the real line at dispersed-to-controlling, precisely so the trigger cannot be gamed by paying in stock. He also reframed Revlon itself: it is best understood as a case where selling the company was how management protected itself, and the board would have won had it proved Perelman could not fund and Forstmann could, rather than arguing the offer was simply worse for shareholders.

## Agency - actual authority

`3/high` · Actual authority / manifestation versus intention

**Baseline.** Actual authority is created by a principal's manifestation to an agent that, as **reasonably understood by the agent**, expresses the principal's assent that the agent act on the principal's behalf (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 3.01).

**Gloss.** Buccola splits agency into an external relationship, which is mandatory and cannot be contracted around, and an internal one, which is fiduciary. Externally two things follow and he treats them as non-waivable: the principal is liable for the agent's acts under respondeat superior, and the agent can bind the principal to contracts and conveyances. Actual authority is what the principal conveyed; apparent authority is what the principal led the third party to believe, so the third party must know of the authority ex ante for the contract to bind. Motors Liquidation is the limiting case and the pathway when a hypo narrows a grant: authority is limited to what was given, but a principal cannot confine scope so nebulously that nobody can tell, and the working presumption is that a lawyer filing on your behalf binds you.

## Books and records inspection - 220 before and after SB 21

`3/high` · § 220 — proper purpose, credible basis, and the SB 21 rewrite of scope

**Baseline.** A stockholder may inspect a Delaware corporation's books and records on a written demand under oath for a **proper purpose**, meaning "a purpose reasonably related to such person's interest as a stockholder." **SB 21 rewrote the back half of the section.** Before March 25, 2025 the statute granted access to the stock ledger, the stockholder list, "and its other books and records," with the limits supplied by case law — credible basis, necessary and essential, rifled precision.

**Gloss.** Buccola frames 220 as a balancing test rather than a list: a stronger proper purpose buys access to more kinds of document, which is why Yahoo's reach into texts and emails started to look like ordinary post-motion discovery. The structural reason it matters is the judge-made heightened pleading standard for derivative suits, justified precisely because books and records are available first — so use 220 before filing, and say so. Two consequences to carry: Caremark pleadings get much stronger when the plaintiff has director communications, which is plausibly what SB 21 was reacting to; and the incorporation condition cuts both ways, since the defendant may use the produced documents to attack the complaint, effectively moving the motion to dismiss forward. Post-SB 21, 220 rolls back the categories and codifies incorporation. His framing instruction for every case is here too: say who is liable, on what standard of review, and to whom.

## Controller buyouts - the MFW dual protection structure

`3/high` · Common law MFW — both protections, ab initio; now codified for going private at § 144(c)

**Baseline.** In a controller <u>**buyout**</u> (going private), business judgment review applies **if and only if** the transaction is conditioned **ab initio** on both an independent, adequately empowered special committee that meets its duty of care and the uncoerced, informed vote of a majority of the minority.

**Gloss.** MFW and Corwin are taught as the pair that SB 21 codified — independent committee plus majority of the minority, now running through 144(a)(1) and (a)(2) for interested directors and 144(b) and (c) for controllers. The reason the dual structure exists is the one to state: a single protection leaves the controller's influence intact at the other end of the transaction, so the committee must be able to say no and the minority vote must be fully informed and uncoerced. Buccola's framing of the Revlon trigger belongs here too, since it determines whether enhanced scrutiny applies at all — breakup, sale, or change of control of some type — and he flagged identifying that trigger as exam material, pointing at Paramount v. ## Preferred versus common - the duty to the residual claimants

`3/high` · Common law — the residual claimant rule, the dual fiduciary problem, and contractual workarounds

**Baseline.** Directors must "strive in good faith and on an informed basis to **maximize the value of the corporation for the benefit of its residual claimants**, the ultimate beneficiaries of the firm's value, **not for the benefit of its contractual claimants**." Consequently: "it is the duty of directors to pursue the best interests of the corporation and its **common stockholders**, **if that can be done faithfully with the contractual promises owed to the preferred**," and "generally it will be the duty of the board, where **discretionary judgment** is to be exercised, to prefer the interests of the common stock... to the interests created by the special rights, preferences, etc. of preferred stock." **The principle is not about preferred stock.** "This principle is not unique to preferred stock; it applies equally to **other holders of contract rights** against the corporation."

**Gloss.** Trados is the case and the holding is that the board must maximise value for the residual claimants, not for the fund that nominated it — which bites because VC designees are dual fiduciaries whose obligations run to the common. Buccola then pressed the harder question he flagged as hard: what exactly is the duty in a hierarchical capital structure. He ruled out maximising preferred value, ruled out maximising common value alone since zeroing out the common is not value-maximising, and landed on maximising the value of the shares as a whole. Work his $60m example to see why the answer changes with the distribution, and note the outcome — entire fairness applied once the business judgment rule was rebutted, yet no damages, because the price was fair, so a fair price can cure an unfair process. NEA v. Rich supplies the private-company overlay: drag-along rights are valid as stockholder agreements binding the stockholder rather than the board, while a covenant not to sue is enforceable except as to intentional wrongs.

## Partnership loyalty - business opportunity

`3/high` · Common law duty of loyalty / appropriation of a venture opportunity

**Baseline.** Joint adventurers, like copartners, owe one another the duty of the finest loyalty while the enterprise continues.

**Gloss.** Buccola frames the partnership defaults as a set of risks rather than a set of rules: any partner binds the firm, partners are liable for partnership debts, partnership assets are exposed to an individual partner's creditors, but one partner's debts do not become another's. Internally the defaults are equal sharing with contribution among partners, mutual duties of care and loyalty, and dissolution at will or on death. The analytic payoff he drew is the holdout problem — risk rises with the number of partners, because any one can obstruct — and the moral hazard that a poor partner's equity is worth more precisely because he cannot cover the liabilities. ## Direct versus derivative claims - the Tooley test

`3/high` · Ct. Ch. R. 23.1 gateway — the two Tooley questions

**Baseline.** Classification rests on two questions and nothing else: 1. **Who suffered the alleged harm** — the corporation, or the suing stockholder individually? 2. **Who would receive the benefit** of the recovery or other remedy?

**Gloss.** Buccola's working line is harm distribution, not Tooley's words: direct means a single holder is singled out, derivative means everyone is affected equally — a dividend paid to all but one holder, or an issuance that dilutes only one, is direct. He laid out three litigation postures and two of them collapse into the same answer: a stockholder suing an adverse party where demand is futile, and a stockholder suing his own board, are both derivative. Riverstone is the move worth stealing, because it manufactures a direct claim out of what looks derivative: the merger cancelled the shares and waived the corporation's litigation rights against its own directors without pricing that waiver, which harmed the selling stockholders disproportionately. In any merger hypo, ask what intangible the deal gave away and whether it was valued — that is where a direct theory comes from.

**Gloss.** The mechanics carry the legal argument. **Baseline.** Because of "the **omnipresent specter** that a board may be acting primarily in its own interests, rather than those of the corporation and its shareholders, there is an **enhanced duty** which calls for judicial examination at the threshold before the protections of the business judgment rule may be conferred." ## Business judgment rule - scope of protection

`3/high` · § 141(a) / scope of protection / non-review of substance

**Baseline.** Where the business judgment rule applies, a court will not review the **substance** of the board's decision.

**Gloss.** The business judgment rule is a standard of review while care and loyalty are standards of conduct — which is why Shlensky never reached whether Wrigley satisfied care at all. Shlensky pleaded no conflict, only a bad profit-maximising decision, and that is what the rule forecloses absent fraud, illegality or conflict; Buccola's flat statement is that there is likely no duty to maximise corporate profits. Bayer shows the other entry point: identify a conflict and the good-faith presumption is lost, though the company may still rebut. The pathway in a hypo attacking a board decision is therefore never that the decision was reasonable — it is that the plaintiff pleaded the wrong thing, and the way back in is a conflict or a process failure, not a bad outcome. Entire fairness is the destination once the rule is defeated, and it too is a standard of review rather than of conduct.

**Baseline.** Where a transaction is **not subject to entire fairness**, approval by a **fully informed, uncoerced** vote of the **disinterested** stockholders invokes the business judgment rule in a **post-closing damages** action — and does so even if Revlon would otherwise have applied.

**Gloss.** Sort by remedy and by who is conflicted. ## Controlling stockholder transactions - the self-dealing trigger

`3/high` · Who is a controller, and when entire fairness attaches — § 144(e)(2), (3)

**Baseline.** Two questions, in order, and conflating them is the commonest error: 1. **Is there a controller?** At common law, a stockholder owning more than 50% of voting power, or owning less but who "exercises control over the business affairs of the corporation." Post SB-21, this is defined under § 144(e)(2). 2. **Is the transaction self-dealing?** Fiduciary duty alone "will not evoke the intrinsic fairness standard." The standard applies only where the duty is accompanied by self-dealing — where the controller "receives something from the subsidiary **to the exclusion of, and detriment to**, the minority stockholders." Post SB-21 this is defined under § 144(e)(3).

**Gloss.** Shareholders owe no duty because they do not control the corporation's assets, so everything turns on whether this one controls the directors — and office hours confirmed the duty runs to the other stockholders even where the controller sits off the board. Sinclair is the template: a pro rata payment is not self-dealing, which requires a pecuniary advantage to one holder at another's expense, so motive is irrelevant to the dividend claim, and past roughly 51% the controller increasingly harms itself. Tesla 1 adds the sliding scale below 50%, where the question is a non-ratable benefit. MindBody he marked as exam material against Sinclair: Stollmeyer could not sell his whole stake without crushing the price, which gave him a conflict in cashing out, and he hid information — a loyalty breach that drew only nominal damages. On the back end of a two-step deal, office hours gave the practical limit: a squeeze-out at a lower price than the front end invites an entire-fairness suit, so the delta cannot be large, and § 252(h) is now a safe harbour where the bidder states the back-end consideration up front and it is identical to the front end.

## Charter exculpation of director liability

`3/high` · § 102(b)(7) charter exculpation of monetary damages

**Baseline.** A Delaware certificate of incorporation may contain a provision eliminating or limiting a director's personal liability to the corporation or its stockholders for **monetary damages** for breach of fiduciary duty as a director, subject to four carve-outs.

**Gloss.** Buccola introduces 102(b)(7) as the legislative answer to Van Gorkom: it exculpates directors from monetary damages for duty of care violations, and it is the one thing he says charters actually capitalise on out of all of 102(b). The consequence to carry into every damages question is his own formulation — if the plaintiff wants money, the plaintiff must plead duty of loyalty. That turns exculpation into a pleading gate rather than a defence, so in a hypo the sequence is to identify the claim as care, apply 102(b)(7) to kill the monetary remedy, and then ask what is left: injunctive relief, or a loyalty theory the facts may or may not support.

## Director primacy - limits on constraining the board

`3/high` · § 141(a) limits on bylaws and stockholder agreements; § 122(18) response

**Baseline.** 8 Del.

**Gloss.** Gorman and Moelis are taught as a pair about who may take power away from the board, and 141(a) is the gate: only the board hires and fires the CEO, because that is a corporate affair, so a bylaw amendment by written consent cannot do it. Buccola then asked what Gorman could actually do, and the answer is the pathway for any hypo where a stockholder wants control — amend the charter, which needs the board to propose and the stockholders to approve, or simply replace the board and let the new board act. Moelis is the mirror image, a holder who wanted to divest his shares and keep control, and the response was legislative: 122(18) now permits stockholder agreements conferring such rights. Note the drafting condition on 122(18) and do not overstate it — the agreement must not contravene the certificate or Delaware law.

## Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)

`3/high` · § 144(b), (c) — statutory safe harbours for controller transactions (new in SB 21)

**Baseline.** **These subsections did not exist before SB 21.** Pre-amendment § 144 addressed only director and officer conflicts; controller transactions were governed entirely by common law, where entire fairness attached on self-dealing and only the judicially created **MFW** framework could restore business judgment review.

**Gloss.** Tesla 2 is the worked example and the step order is the answer structure: does the business judgment rule apply, which turns on whether the holder is controlling — if not, dismissed — then on whether the remaining directors are interested; if not, entire fairness on fair price and fair dealing. The outcome matters: poor process, fair price, entire fairness satisfied, so a bad process is not dispositive. Control at 22% was never decided and SB 21 fixed the threshold at one third in 144(c)(2). Post-SB 21 the routes are 144(b)(1), an independent committee of wholly disinterested directors, or 144(b)(2), a majority of disinterested stockholders, and a go-private or freeze-out needs both. Office hours added three operational points. Cleansing needs only one route, not all of them. Disinterested stockholders are established by the company in the proxy, by naming the largest holders, and this goes largely uncontested — a conceptually soft spot worth flagging. And if the plaintiff abandons the controller theory, the case does not die: it becomes an interested-director claim under 144(a), which on Tesla's facts would likely fail to cleanse and still reach entire fairness. His recusal aside is usable at the fact level — Elon sat in the room after recusing, which is subtle coercion.

→ [[Controlling stockholder safe harbours - 144(b) and (c)]]

## Disclosure settlements in deal litigation - the give and the get

`3/medium` · Ct. Ch. R. 23 — approval of disclosure-only class settlements, and the Delaware materiality standard

**Baseline.** A disclosure-only class settlement will be approved only where the supplemental disclosures address a **plainly material** misrepresentation or omission and the release is **narrowly circumscribed** to disclosure and sale-process claims that have been sufficiently investigated.

**Gloss.** Buccola treats the disclosure settlement as a market phenomenon rather than a doctrine: a merger draws a fiduciary-duty suit attacking the proxy as insufficient, the board responds by adding information, and the stockholders pay the plaintiffs' fees — which he calls a peppercorn lawsuit and an additional deal tax, since the corporation funds both sides and the class gets disclosure nobody reads. The numbers are the evidence to cite: roughly 95% of mergers were challenged before Trulia and about 45% after. In a hypo offering a disclosure-only settlement, the argument is that the give is worthless and the get is a fee, so the court should refuse it — which is Trulia's move and the reason the rate halved.

→ [[Disclosure settlements in deal litigation - the give and the get]]

## Limited liability - piercing the corporate veil

`3/medium` · Common law veil piercing / enterprise entity versus dummy corporation

**Baseline.** Incorporation exists to let proprietors escape personal liability, and the privilege holds unless the plaintiff pleads that the shareholder was **conducting the business in his individual capacity**.

**Gloss.** Buccola taught Walkovszky as two distinct theories that are easy to run together. Enterprise liability asks the court to ignore the formal lines when several corporations function as one — same shareholders, same purpose, same kinds of assets, no true differentiation and no reason for the separation except evading liability. Veil piercing is the separate claim against the owner, and his assessment is that it is very hard with multiple shareholders: Carlton is a good candidate and still failed, because the normal rule is strict separation. The pathway when a hypo gives you a fragmented corporate group is to argue enterprise liability first and name the legitimate reason separation can have — different tranches of risk for different investors — then explain why it is absent on these facts.

→ [[Limited liability - piercing the corporate veil]]

## Early corporate charters - enumerated powers

`2/medium` · Special-act charters versus the modern enabling statute

**Baseline.** Under a **special-act charter**, a corporation possessed only the powers the legislature granted it.

**Gloss.** Buccola gave the sources of corporate law as a ranked list worth reproducing: the general incorporation statute (DGCL), then the charter, then the bylaws, with fiduciary duties sitting alongside as the equitable layer — and his operative statement is that a corporate act is valid only when it is consistent with both the legal and the fiduciary constraints. Against that he set the three judicial routes and their relative weight: quo warranto and ultra vires are both rare, the latter because modern powers are drafted so broadly, which leaves the shareholder suit as the main threshold mechanism. The historical arc explains why: specific charters with narrow powers until 1830, general statutes with still-narrow terms to 1880, general and flexible after — so ultra vires withered as drafting widened.

→ [[Early corporate charters - enumerated powers]]

## Director oversight - honest mistake and ordinary diligence

`2/medium` · Common law oversight of subordinates / honest error of judgment

**Baseline.** A director undertakes "diligent attention" to the office and "a faithful and honest discharge of the duty which it imposes" — **not infallibility**.

**Gloss.** Scott v. Depeyster is where Buccola plants deference, and he keeps the two claims apart: the ultra vires point is a legal claim that the transaction was not insurance, while the liability point is a fiduciary claim that fails because the directors acted in good faith. Two propositions to carry: deference to directors on the duty of care starts here, and the care actually required changes with the times, so an old standard is not a safe guide to a modern one. His counterfactual is the pathway for a hypo about an act outside the charter — had Scott sought an injunction on the discounted notes rather than damages he would probably have got it, because any shareholder can hold the corporation to its authorised powers. The remedy sought, not the breach alleged, is what decides that case.

## Federal disclosure and the universal proxy - Rules 13d-1 and 14a-19

`2/medium` · The federal overlay on an activist campaign — Schedule 13D/13G disclosure and the universal proxy card

**Baseline.** Two federal rules bracket an activist campaign. **Rule 13d-1 governs the buying; Rule 14a-19 governs the asking.** Everything else in either rule is plumbing. **Rule 13d-1 — cross five per cent with a control purpose and you must announce yourself.** "Any person who, after acquiring... the beneficial ownership of any equity security of a class which is specified in paragraph (i)(1)... is directly or indirectly the beneficial owner of **more than five percent** of the class shall, **within five business days** after the date of the acquisition, file with the Commission, a statement containing the information required by **Schedule 13D**." The entire architecture of the rule turns on **one question**: are you holding with a purpose or effect of "**changing or influencing the control of the issuer**"? | | Control purpose | No control purpose | |---|---|---| | **What you file** | Schedule 13D — the long form, including your plans for the issuer | Schedule 13G — short form | | **When** | 5 business days after crossing 5% | Quarterly-ish (45 days after quarter end), or 5 business days after month end if you pass 10% | | **If you change your mind** | — | You flip to 13D in 5 business days, **and you may not vote or buy more** until the tenth day after you file | **Rule 14a-19 — run your own slate and everyone's names go on everyone's card.** "No person may solicit proxies in support of director nominees other than the registrant's nominees unless such person" gives the company advance notice of its nominees, files a definitive proxy statement, and "**[s]olicits the holders of shares representing at least 67% of the voting power** of shares entitled to vote on the election of directors." In exchange, both sides' cards "[s]et forth the names of **all** persons nominated for election by the registrant and by any person or group of persons that has complied with this section." That is the **universal proxy**.

**Gloss.** Buccola uses 13(d) only as the mechanism that makes the lightning strike possible, and that is the role to give it: disclosure is triggered at five per cent, but the ten-day window lets an accumulator reach 4.9% and then strike before anyone knows, buying cheaply from sellers who are misinformed about the company's prospects. Williams' rights plan was built to close exactly that gap, which is why its trigger matches the statutory threshold. Two aggregation features are worth naming because they are what made the plan arguably too stringent: holdings are aggregated across a group, and the acting-in-concert or daisy-chain provision aggregates two holders who never communicated with each other provided each communicated with a third.

→ [[Federal disclosure and the universal proxy - Rules 13d-1 and 14a-19]]
