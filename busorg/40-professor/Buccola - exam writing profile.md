# Buccola — exam writing profile

What his exam demands and what his circulated answers do on the page. Substance
lives in `30-doctrine`; this file is form.

## Mechanics

Three questions, **60 / 20 / 20**, identical in 2023 and 2024. Three hours.

This year's syllabus changes the constraint:

> "Grades will reflect the results of an in-class, closed-book, word-limited exam. Students may refer to a physical, unannotated copy of the DGCL, if they wish."

Both past exams said "There are no word limits." Every circulated answer in
`40-professor/` was written **open-book**. The craft transfers; the lookup
budget does not. Syllabus v2 (12 Nov 2026) adds the DGCL carve-out above: the
statute's text is on the desk, but nothing else is — no cases, no notes, no
outline. The lookup budget is still essentially closed-book for everything that
is not bare statutory language.

Four instructions do real work.

> "There are no word limits. But recognize that my aim in grading will be to assess the extent to which answers indicate mastery of the law of business organizations. **Offering extraneous ideas, complicating simple issues, or discussing matters not necessary or relevant to answering the question posed signals a weak grasp of what matters** and thus a weak grasp of how the law works."

> "Do not worry about citation formats. Assume you are writing for a reader who is familiar with the texts assigned in the course syllabus but who has not thought carefully about the relationships among them."

> "**you should not assume facts not in the prompt**"

> "The fact pattern in question 1 is inspired by recent, real-world events. The question is not, however, a test of your knowledge of those events."

Instruction 5 licenses bare section numbers and bare case names with no years,
courts, or pin cites — the style every strong answer uses, and the style that
costs nothing closed-book.

## How much pre-SB 21 law to know — answered in office hours

Asked directly, twice:

> "Focus on the current law, but the previous law will help you get there if you understand how it developed/why the current law is the way it is"

> "Likely won't get asked on the exam how an issue or hypothetical would come out under what the law as 10 years ago, but you need to know what that law was so the current law can be properly applied. **Might get some extra points for distinguishing what the change was over time**"

So: answer under current law. Do not run a parallel analysis under the old
regime — he is telling you that is not the question. But a clause naming what
changed and why is worth points, which suits his paired-authority essay exactly,
since several of those pairings are a statute answering a decision.

## Question archetypes

| | Q1 — 60 pts | Q2 — 20 pts | Q3 — 20 pts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | Delaware issue-spotter from a real deal (GameStop) | compare *Scott v. Depeyster* with *Marchand* | compare *Unocal*/*Revlon* with *Airgas*/*Corwin* |
| 2024 | issue-spotter (Twitter/X, corporation → LLC) | compare DGCL 102(b)(7) with 122(18) | compare *In re Oracle* with *Zuckerberg* |
| 2026 | issue-spotter (dual-class controller takeout) | 144(d)(2) against *Oracle* | — |

**Forty points every year are the paired-authority essay, and its framing is
boilerplate**, word-for-word identical across years:

> "Legal texts such as statutes and judicial decisions do more than clarify doctrine. They also reflect principles, insights, and values (and tensions between the same) that recur thematically across doctrinal categories… In that spirit, please compare [X] with [Y]."

The pairings cross case/statute lines freely. Nothing in the corpus is pure
advise-a-client, should-we-overrule, or drafting.

## What strong answers do

**Q1 opens with a heading, not a sentence** — three of four scripts. The heading
is the first doctrinal gate: `Direct or Derivative`, `Law or equity?`,
`Legal Theory`. The one roadmap in the set is also the shortest script:

> "This case combines questions of controlling shareholders' obligations, cleansing, and fairness in the context of a corporation's conversion into an LLC. I conclude that a Chancellor will probably find this transaction cleansed and entitled to business judgement on the basis of ratification by a majority of unaffiliated, informed, uncoerced stockholders, but this analysis turns somewhat on the difficult to resolve questions of whether the CEO is a controller and the extent of board-level conflicts. I proceed to the analysis."

**Q2 and Q3 open with a one-sentence thesis naming the tension** — seven of
seven. Nobody restates facts; nobody writes "This question asks whether."

> "The instantly recognizable conflict between Depeyster and Marchand is that the former seems to represent a laissez-faire view by courts towards the day-to-day happenings of business people, while the latter instead seems to take a rather skeptical view of a board's ability/trustworthiness to conduct business."

> "144(d)(2) appears to repudiate the analysis in Oracle. This abrogation reflects several themes of Delaware law: the emphasis on commercial reasonableness, the legislature's sensitivity to corporate interests, evolution to meet changing corporate structure, and the delicate balance between judicial oversight and private ordering."

That second one names four themes and then uses them as the four body sections
in order.

**Headings on Q1 are an invented standard-of-review taxonomy**, in the order a
Chancery judge hits the gates:

> Direct or Derivative · Legal · Who? · Controlling Stockholder · Conflicted Director · Entire Fairness · Revlon · Unocal · Hewlett-Packard · Aiding and Abetting · Conclusion

Two habits worth copying:

- **The negative heading.** "Not Unocal or Revlon" — a heading whose job is to kill a doctrine in 135 words. Instruction 4 penalises complicating simple issues; this is how you show you considered a gate and correctly dropped it.
- **A named Conclusion on Q1, always.** Not a summary — a disposition, with a recommendation: "The transaction should be conditioned on approval by a vote of a majority of the outsider class B stockholders, excluding both VCs and employees."

**Q2 and Q3 carry no headings at all** — continuous prose, four to eight
paragraphs, 100–180 words each.

**No bullets and no numbered lists anywhere in the corpus.** Multi-element tests
are folded into one sentence with the authority hung off the end:

> "To cleanse a controlling shareholder transaction, the deal must be conditioned ex ante on approval by an independent committee empowered to say no and a majority of unaffilitated, fully informed and uncoerced shareholders (MFW)."

## Authority

**Bare section numbers, no § sign, no "8 Del. C.", usually no gloss.** Case names
bare and parenthetical, no years, no courts. Density runs 22–31 case mentions
per 1,000 words — an order of magnitude above the Birdthistle scripts in the
same folder.

The dominant register:

> "Zest is a formal controlling stockholder (144(e)(2)(a))"
> "VC appointed directors are not conflicted merely because they are appointed by their VC (144(d)(3))"
> "Fair price matters more (Trados)."

**The most repeated move in the corpus is analogise-then-distinguish in one
sentence:**

> "Unlike in Tesla, a special committee was formed"
> "Unlike in Trados, he has no contractual right to this greater premium"
> "The case resembles Cheff where the board paid off a takeover artist through greenmail, but Zest is not a corporate raider."

Named judges are essentially absent — two across four scripts. Named theorists:
zero.

**The 2026 closed-book script carries the highest section-number density of the
set (5.7 per 1,000 words) and the least quotation.** Section numbers are the
authority form that survives having no book, and that script works entirely in
the post-SB 21 § 144 architecture — `144(d)(2)`, `144(d)(3)`, `144(e)(2)(a)`,
`144(e)(8)` — which is this year's governing text. Memorising the § 144
subsection map is directly indicated. The v2 DGCL carve-out makes the raw text a
backstop against a blanked subsection number, but it is a lookup, not fluency —
flipping pages mid-answer costs time this word-limited format does not give back,
and the map has to be in your head to see which safe harbour or savings clause a
fact pattern triggers. Keep memorising; treat the book as insurance.

## Commitment

**"Likely" is the workhorse**, carrying 24 of 33 verdict hits in one script.
"I conclude" appears once in the whole corpus; "I would advise" and "the better
view" never. He asks how a Chancery judge *will* think, so the answers predict
rather than recommend.

The characteristic move is **hedge, counter, resolve inside one paragraph**:

> "Zest might argue that he is being given a 'control premium'… However, while class B shares have voting control, they are intended to share ratably… So, the greater payout probably makes this a controlling stockholder transaction."

Where a question is genuinely open the strong answers say so and carry on rather
than stalling: "it's ambiguous whether the special committee was given the power
to reject the tranasction. **Assuming they were**, this committee likely meets
the requirements."

The 2026 closed-book script hedges roughly twice as much as the open-book ones.
Calibrate against that script, not against the open-book scripts.

## Budgeting

Allocation tracks 60/20/20 within a few points every time: 59/25/17, 58/21/21,
66/18/16. **Q2 and Q3 converge on 600–890 words across all four scripts and all
four years** — a 20-point theory essay is a ~700-word, four-to-eight-paragraph
artifact. That is a stable target to train against.

Total length ranges 2,969 to 4,903 words, and the shortest script has the
cleanest roadmap. Length is not the variable.

## Where this cuts against Ashwin's habits

- **Element-marching bleeds here.** The 184 habit of enumerating `(1)…(4)` is off-register on Buccola and arguably violates instruction 4. Fold the test into the sentence and hang the authority off the end.
- **Spelling out black letter bleeds here.** Opposite instruction from Baude.
- **Doctrinal history bleeds here.** No Buccola question rewards narrating how a line of cases developed; Q2/Q3 ask what tension two authorities disclose, and the strong answers reconcile rather than condemn.
- **The heading habit earns here, heavily** — on Q1 only. Q2/Q3 take none.
- **Flagging a gap is fine; assuming a fact is not.** "you should not assume facts not in the prompt."
