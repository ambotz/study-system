## Notes

### [[Omnicare v. NCS Healthcare (2003)|Omnicare]]

- **Buccola doesn't agree with reasoning/outcome of Omnicare: Good deal protection was thrown out. No guarantee Omnicare would've bought. Directors made a decision.**
- NCS - for sale
- Two potential bidders: Genesis and Omnicare
    - Situation: Omnicare willing to top Genesis bid → But Genesis was the one who put in the diligence/valuation work

### Genesis's concern

- Even if merger agreement is signed, shareholders must vote yes
    - So, they created voting agreement between Chairman and Chief Executive (majority voting power) → key deal protection term
- Additional deal protections: force-the-vote + break-up fee + no-shop + no fiduciary-out [must not renege [?] even for a better deal?]

### Legal Framework

- Omnicare (as shareholder) brings direct claim against NCS.
- Fiduciary duty breached: likely duty of care → Revlon duty; Omnicare only looking for an injunction, 102(b)(7) can't exculpate
    - Not [[Business judgment rule - scope of protection|BJR]] since its a sale of the business

### Analysis

- Omnicare bid was not guaranteed; conditional on diligence. The bid could've fallen → Not necessarily clear if price would've truly gone up.
- Why should the standard of review be more skeptical during sale of business? Revlon: Maximize price.
    - Changes market structure that gives leverage back to target and forces Acquirers like Genesis to not threaten to walk away constantly.
- Rule: No merger agreement that has (1) force the vote and (2) voting agreement/lockup provision
    - Even though Court cites [[Unocal v. Mesa Petroleum (1985)|Unocal]], this isn't truly a takeover case

### Revlon trigger

- Revlon trigger: Breakup, Sale, Change of control of some type

### Theories from Revlon regarding lack of price increase

1. Deal Protection - too much; skews competing offers
2. Market Check - wrong execution of diligence / not great diligence
3. Insufficient Negotiation - No bargaining
4. Insufficient disclosure - Proxy isn't informative enough

### After Revlon

1. More shareholder primacy Directors → Solely trying to increase $$$
2. More outside Directors on Board
3. Less retail investors; more funds own the equity. Attention given to a single company is higher from stockholders.

- Yet, 95% of mergers were being sued/scrutinized → Changed after [[In re Trulia (2016)|Trulia]] (Dropped back down 45%)

### Disclosure

1. Merger → lawsuit of breach of fiduciary duty → proxy materials → insufficient proxy materials (Direct shareholder class action). → Board adds more information and pays plaintiffs' fees (paid by shareholders) [peppercorn lawsuits]
    - Almost like an additional deal tax

### [[Kahn v. M&F Worldwide (2014)|MFW]] and [[Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings (2015)|Corwin]]

- MFW and Corwin → § 144(a)(1) and 144(a)(2); 144(b) and 144(c) → New [?]
    - Margin note: Independent committee + majority of minority shareholders
- → Post-closing monetary damages. Approval by disinterested stockholders = [[Business judgment rule - scope of protection|BJR]]
    - Use Revlon only for injunction, not post closing $$ claims
- 144(e) → allows equitable relief / nothing precludes injunction
