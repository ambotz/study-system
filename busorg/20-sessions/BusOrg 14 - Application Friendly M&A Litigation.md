### Conglomerates

- Lots of conglomerates in 1980s → gave rise to takeovers where corporate raiders wanted to take control and sell off pieces of the company (the individual parts could be worth more than the whole).
- How conglomerates exist: Capital lock-in → shs can't do much to change governance
    - CEOs of conglomerates were statesmen/nobility
    - Raiders: Conglomerate CEOs were sloths; not being opportunistic enough

### (Revlon)

- Bergerac doesn't want to sell company to Perelman
    - Asset sale and merger are off the table
    - Must acquire via **stock purchase**. (Don't need Board consent).
- Perelman offers $42/sh at first for sh trading $30 on the market; then $45 hostile offer
    - Hostile - against the Board, not shareholders
- Board can stop a tender offer by holding it as a breach of fid. duty to let shareholders entertain tender offer at such a low price.
    - If Lazard thinks shares can be flipped at $60-70, then why is it selling at $30?
    - If the different pieces are worth more, they **should** sell it.
    - Bankers are in a catch 22: they're arguing Perelman's offer is **not enough** (not that its invalid).

### Revlon (1986) cont.

- Note Purchase Right Plan: Allows shareholders to swap into a note that gives back a share for a 12% increase in one year.
- Add leverage: Makes M and F junk bonds are less appetizing when Revlon is already leveraged
- Revlon Board entertained offer (new) from Forstmann Little (not a raider; cooperative buyer).
    - LBO at $56/sh; Forstmann offer is friendly
    - Forstmann has access to books and records since Board is cooperating
    - Revlon and Forstmann sign definitive merger agreement (includes [[Deal protection devices - Unocal review of merger lock-ups|deal protection]] that makes it more expensive to walk away → $25M breakup fee; lock-up option that allows a discount price for a Business Unit, etc.)
        - → Forces shareholders to vote yes. → Merger
- Exam Note: Always have what type of fiduciary duty is breached
- Why does Perelman have standing to sue? Perelman is a shareholder; can sue on a direct theory, once merger goes through.
- Perelman sues for breach of fiduciary duty (duty of loyalty); Board is extinguishing their own exposure from previously issued noteholders (whose note values will go down once merger goes through).
    - → Buccola doesn't think this is very plausible: Covenants were defeasible. This is a classic takeover defense that was always undoable from the start.

### Implications

1. DE Court specifies: When takeover becomes inevitable, Board's duty becomes to maximize price to stockholders → Hard Q in other contexts (i.e. one bidder)
2. Intermediate Scrutiny: Enhanced scrutiny (BJR too weak, entire fairness too exacting b/c there will always be relationships).

- ***Duty of Care or Loyalty?: Court enjoins deal protection. No damages. **It doesn't matter for the remedy.** Post-closing, breach matters Corwin]
