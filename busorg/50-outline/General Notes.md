
1. Controller buyout
	1. Two tiers offers, etc.
	2. **The path to deference** is the MFW/§144(c) route: condition the freeze-out _ab initio_ on both an empowered independent special committee _and_ an informed, uncoerced majority-of-the-minority vote. Clear both and you flip from entire fairness to business judgment. A coercive two-tier squeeze-out has **neither** — no committee negotiated it, and the minority gets no free informed vote; they’re simply frozen out. So no safe harbor → entire fairness → it fails on both prongs.
	3. a frozen-out shareholder can demand **appraisal** — a statutory judicial determination of fair value in cash — instead of accepting the junk. Weinberger made appraisal the primary remedy for exactly this kind of squeeze-out, which is part of why coercive two-tier freeze-outs got litigated and regulated into near-extinction.
	4. controller stockholder → fiduciary to the minority (duty of loyalty; duty of care mainly a board duty)→ back-end freeze-out judged under _entire fairness_ (fair price + fair dealing), not Revlon — unless the controller used the full MFW/§144(c) committee-plus-MOM machinery to earn business judgment.
		1. the controller owes a **targeted duty of loyalty** — don’t abuse control to harm the minority through unfair self-dealing or non-ratable extraction — enforced through entire fairness when triggered. It is _not_ a duty of care, and it is _not_ a duty to maximize the minority’s value. That last distinction matters for your exam: a controller has no Revlon-style obligation to get the minority the best deal; its duty is a _floor_ (don’t exploit them), not a _ceiling_ (maximize for them). That’s precisely why the back-end freeze-out runs through entire fairness — a loyalty standard aimed at exploitation — rather than through Revlon’s value-maximization lens.

3. Posion pill
	1. ### So “the board effectively controls” — yes, with the right nuance
	2. The precise way to say it: the pill shifts the **decision over whether a sale happens** from the shareholders to the board. The shareholders own the shares, but the board controls whether the pill comes down, and nothing happens until it does. That’s why the whole fight in these cases is never “can the shareholders tender” — it’s “will the board redeem,” and that board decision is what gets reviewed under Unocal.
	3. ### The two escape hatches — why it’s “effectively,” not “absolutely”
		1. The control isn’t total, and this is the part that keeps the pill lawful rather than “just say never”:
			1. **The ballot box.** The one thing the board _can’t_ block is a shareholder vote. A bidder can run a **proxy contest**, elect a new board, and have _that_ board redeem the pill. The pill blocks the _tender offer_ but not the _election_. This is the avenue Moran promised would always remain.
			2. **The outer limits.** The board can’t make that avenue illusory. **Dead-hand/no-hand/slow-hand** pills (which stop even a newly elected board from redeeming) are invalid. And the pill must still survive **Unocal** — a legitimate threat plus a proportionate, non-preclusive response (Williams struck one that failed).

6. Substnative coercion (a valid threat under Unocal) vs. We-know-better Director response to voting (invalid)
	1. substantive coercion = a _selling_ decision, tied to an _inadequate price_, remedied _temporarily_ while preserving the vote → permitted. We-know-better = overriding a _voting_ decision, with _no price benchmark_, substituting the board’s judgment for the shareholders’ on who governs → forbidden. Williams is where a pill (selling tool) aimed at activism (voting phenomenon) gets judged by the franchise rule, and the “ignorance” rationale flips from permitted to forbidden.

8. Voting and pooling agreements
	1. Validity comes from § 218(c); enforceability comes from § 212(e); you need both, which is why real-world voting agreements bundle them.
	2. Limits on vote buying: **the rule is purpose/effect-based (disenfranchise → per se; else voidable/intrinsic-fairness); the buyer’s identity matters only because management-with-corporate-assets is the fact pattern that _creates_ the disenfranchising effect — using the opponents’ own money to dilute their votes — and the cure is a disinterested ratifying vote.**
	3. Franchise claims are the paradigmatic **direct** claim.
		1. **The franchise claim** (distorted vote → impaired voting rights → clean vote for the stockholders) = **direct**. This is what Hewlett litigated.
		- **A corporate-waste claim** (corporate assets misspent to buy votes → harm to the treasury → recovery to the corporation) = **derivative**. This was _not_ the thrust of the case.
		- A useful general rule to carry: **claims about the fairness or integrity of a stockholder vote, and interference with voting rights generally, are direct — even when the mechanism of the interference also happens to injure the corporation.** The vote belongs to the stockholders; a suit to protect it is theirs to bring directly.
	- Consent solicitation response: _**when was the plan conceived — before the contest, or in response to it?**_ Clear day → board likely fine. Raining → heightened scrutiny, board probably loses.
	- Franchise Response
		- **a reasonable justification = a real, non-pretextual threat to the corporation (anything but “shareholders will vote wrong”); a fine response = the narrowest tool that counters that threat, leaving the stockholders a realistic path to control, with persuasion always allowed and restraint counting in your favor**
	- § 228(a) consent = holders of a majority of _all outstanding_ shares (for majority matters) sign a written statement of the action, delivered within 60 days, and it takes effect with no meeting and no notice beforehand — a higher numerical bar than a quorum meeting, but faster and outside the board’s calendar, which is exactly why it’s an insurgent’s weapon and why charters often kill it.
	- Protection against tender offer vs. against franchise control after Coster:
		- So a board defending against a **proxy contest / consent solicitation** (your “hostile board attempt”) faces a _stricter application_ of the unified standard than a board defending against a **pure tender offer** (your “takeover attempt”), where Airgas shows how much room “just say no” gives. Same test, different intensity depending on whether the franchise is directly impaired.
		- **Is there still a difference between a hostile board (franchise) attempt and a takeover attempt?**
			- **As a matter of doctrine/test — no.** Both now run through one Unocal-shaped enhanced-scrutiny standard; the separate “Blasius” gate collapsed into it.
			- **As a matter of substance/application — yes.** When the defensive action hits the _vote_, the court demands a tighter means-ends fit and keeps the franchise-specific rules (“board knows better” is out, clear-day, persuasion/preclusion). The franchise still gets extra solicitude — it’s just delivered _inside_ the unified test rather than through a separate standard.
- Substantive threats
- **(1) coercion** → structural coercion.
- **(2) low price** → substantive coercion **+ opportunity loss**. Time: **both present**. This is where Time goes.
- **(3) non-shareholders** → the culture talk brushes it; the court doesn’t hold on it.
- **(4) protect management** → not permitted (Cheff’s primary-purpose floor; Williams).

	Time is **(2)**, not a new category. It only _looks_ new because the value is strategy-contingent and the board dressed it in culture.
	
	### The honest version
	
	The reason this feels unsatisfying is that it is. “We know the long-term value” is available to every board, which makes the line between (2) and (4) very hard to police. Delaware’s actual limit is **franchise vs. tender offer**: a board may tell holders they are wrong about _price_ and keep a pill up (Airgas); it may not tell them they are wrong about _who should sit on the board_ (Williams — “the we-know-better justification”). Time is uncomfortable because the restructuring eliminated the very shareholder vote Paramount’s bid was about to influence, which puts it closer to the franchise side than the court admits. That’s the push you should expect.
	
	**One-liner for an exam:** _Time’s threat was not structural coercion but opportunity loss plus substantive coercion — the risk that holders would take $200 in cash in ignorance of the strategic value of a Warner combination the board had spent six years building — and the board’s preference for its own time horizon was treated as a question of authority, not of value._
	Substantive coercion’s object is not the price. It’s the **stockholders’ judgment about** the price. The threat is “they’ll get it wrong about what we’re building,” not “your bid is too low.” The board isn’t saying _our reservation price is $X and you’re under it_; it’s saying _we have a plan, we believe in good faith it’s worth more, and whether and when to sell is our call, not theirs._
		Combination of control + long term value --> misleading shareholders if you dangle something in front of them that abandons this for short term gains
